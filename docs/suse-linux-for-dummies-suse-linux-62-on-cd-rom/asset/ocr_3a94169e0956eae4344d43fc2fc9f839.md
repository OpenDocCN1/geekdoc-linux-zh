# 38 Part II: Installing SuSE Linux

# Disk controllers

The two main types of disks are IDE and SCSI, and each type has its own controller. IDE is more common in PCs, and newer PCs usually have two IDE controllers rather than one. For each IDE controller, your system can have only two hard disks: a master and a slave. Therefore, a PC with two IDE controllers can have up to four hard disks. You should know which hard drive is which. Also, if you have a Windows system that you want to preserve, you should know which hard drive it resides on. Normally, on a Windows system:

+   - The first controller's master disk is called C.
- The next disk is called D and is the slave disk on the first controller.
- The next disk is E and the master disk on the second controller.
- The last disk is F and the slave disk on the second controller.

Normally, Windows is located on your C disk and data is on your other disks. This lettering scheme is just one possibility; your disks could be set up differently and even include CD-ROMs as drives on your IDE controllers.

Some high-end PCs have a SCSI controller on the motherboard or on a separate SCSI controller, either in addition to or instead of the IDE controllers. Older SCSI controllers can have up to 8 devices on them, numbered 0–7, including the controller. Newer SCSI controllers (known as wide controllers) can have up to 16 devices, including the controller.

If all you have a SCSI disk, usually disk 0 or disk 1 is your C disk, and others follow in order.

If you have a mixture of IDE and SCSI controllers, your C disk could be on any of them. The sections later in this chapter, "Getting Information from Windows 95" and "Getting Information from MS-DOS or Windows 3.1," show you how to identify how many disks you have, what type they are, and the controllers to which they are attached.

# Disk drives

You will need to decide whether you will be putting Linux on a separate disk from MS-DOS and Windows or whether you want the two operating systems to share one disk.

Most people think of Windows (either Windows 3.1, Windows 95, and Windows 98) as an operating system. All these, however, have an underlying system called MS-DOS that manages the disk and many hardware components of your PC. From here on, unless we mention a particular system, consider MS-DOS and Windows as the same thing.
