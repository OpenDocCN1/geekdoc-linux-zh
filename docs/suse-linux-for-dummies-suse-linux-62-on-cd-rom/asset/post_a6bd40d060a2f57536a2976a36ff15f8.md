# Sync or swim

In the preceding section, you find out about the dynamic buffer cache, which is used to reduce the number of physical accesses to the disks. The operation of syncing the file system is a request — initiated by the user or a program in Linux — to write the buffer cache out to the disk (also called volatile memory) for safekeeping rather than leaving it in RAM (also volatile memory). If a sync was never performed, some data might stay in RAM forever, without ever written out to the disk. If this happened, when the system crashed (due to a power failure, of course), you wouldn't know whether you wrote to it ever made it to the disk. When you execute a sync command, it writes all this data. Unless something is radically, most or all of this is written in the next few seconds. Then the cache becomes empty and starts to fill again, if necessary.

In Linux, the processing of the dynamic buffer cache has been given to a daemon (a program that runs in the background and does things for the user transparently) bflush, which flushes data every 30 seconds or so. Smaller, smaller pieces of data go out every five seconds. (This is one reason that your Linux system doing something with the disks every few seconds, even when you're not doing anything with your system.) Unless your disk fails, the work bflush means that you may never have a significant loss of data. bflush normally starts automatically when you boot Linux.

The dynamic buffer cache shares RAM with the virtual memory subsystem, and VM. As VM becomes more active and needs more RAM to be efficient, your dynamic buffer cache tends to become smaller, giving up its memory to the VM subsystem. As the buffer cache becomes smaller, the I/O load on the disks tends to get heavier. Each disk on your system will probably experience more I/Os but probably in an uneven fashion. When things quiet down and VM has a lot free RAM, the memory is automatically given back to the dynamic buffer cache.

You can picture your disks as being the tollbooths on a highway. When a road has only one toll booth, all the traffic has to go through it. If traffic is light, no problem occurs (other than paying the toll). If traffic is heavy, however, the cars back up. Putting in another toll booth allows twice as many cars to be handled. You see almost twice as many because the cars may not line up properly, so more cars may try to go through one booth (probably the next to the combination information booth and liquor store) than other tollbooth.

# Part IV: Maintaining Your System

Having one disk on your system is like having one tollbooth on the highway. All I/O on your system (including paging and swapping) goes out to disk. If you have two disks, some I/O load might go to one disk and some to the other. Although the proportions of data flowing to each disk may change over time, it would be nice if, on average, half the data was going to one disk and half to the other.

To try to accomplish this, some systems administrators put separate systems on different disks and separate the file systems across disk drives. For example, suppose you broke up the disks into the following directories and made a file system for each one:

```
/
/tmp
/usr
/usr/tmp
/home
```

You may find that under a normal load, data is written to each file system equally.

Separating the swap areas onto another disk means that even more I/O load is distributed evenly. Think of it as having a mountain of backpacks to carry. You can put them all on one poor, unsuspecting pack mule and watch its knee buckle under the load, or you can distribute them to several pack mules (but you have to manage them all as you trundle the trail).

Another reason for breaking up the data into separate file systems is what is known as head contention. Disks having moving parts called disk heads that are like the heads of an audio cassette deck. To read or write data, the data must pass underneath the heads. Unlike the heads in an audio cassette, the disk heads don't touch the disk but float a very short distance above the surface of the disk. Moving these heads from one place to another to read data is a slow process in computer time, and most people want to avoid them unnecessarily. By having all the data on one disk, each program fights with the other ones to move the heads where they should be for that program. By separating the data, you have a better chance that the data you want is close to the previous data you requested and not clear across the disk.

procinfo-proc-info tells you how many reads and writes you do per disk. Keeping an eye on these numbers tells you how much data is being written to your disks and how much the heads are moving. How can you tell your disk's peak potential for reading or writing data? The peak potential depends on the disk speed, how much data you are writing, and the order in which the data is written back to the disks.

In the literature that came with your disk, you should be able to find times for the following:

- Moving the head from cylinder to cylinder
- A full seek over the entire surface of the cylinder-to-cylinder seek
- An average seek (average being between the cylinder-to-cylinder seek and moving the disk head completely across the disk)
- One rotation; half this rotation is called the average rotational delay

Normally, in finding out disk capacity (or the average potential through your disk), you add one rotational delay, plus the average seek time, plus the rotational delay for the data to be read under the disk head. Depending on how much data you are reading, this can be up to one full rotational delay. If you need to read more data than that, you have to move the disk head again and reinstate the read, which may mean waiting another average rotational delay before the disk lines up with the data again.

On average, take one-half the rotational delay and one average seek for each read. This is your average access time. Suppose this is 100 milliseconds, or one-tenth of a second. Maybe the system can do 10 reads or writes to the disk, on average (or 600 reads or writes to the disk every minute). If you look at information from successive procinfo commands, you can get a feeling for how many disk I/Os your system is doing as opposed to how many it could be doing.

Remember the numbers reported by procinfo are averages taken over time. If the numbers are close to the maximum, you may have periods in which the disk is trying to answer every request but is overwhelmed by the number of I/Os. The bottom line is that you have to wait for your disks.

![](img/96a9ce6a13d8d15ed05f867f41574278_268_0.png)

Trying to reduce the number of I/Os to any one disk by spreading the load over several disks is important and can sometimes help a slow system — without the need for a CPU upgrade.

# Multiple swap partitions

If your system is paging and swapping heavily you want multiple swap partitions, rather than having just one, and distributing them across your disk drives. This will help alleviate disk contention because your programs will be swapped and paged multi-disk instead of just one. On Intel systems, Linux is limited to 128MB per swap and up to 16 swap partitions per system.

# Kernel Building

One of the items that may be slowing down your system is a kernel that is not tailored to your hardware or your needs. This has two major disadvantages:

- You may have your kernel compiled for a processor you don't have.
- You have more instructions and data than you need in your kernel.

This section describes both cases and what you can do to change the situation.

# Tailoring a kernel for your processor

Many Linux distribution kernels were compiled to install onto the largest range of systems. Therefore, they are compiled to run on the slowest processor of a particular architecture (Intel, Alpha, and so on). In the Intel side of the house, this typically means that the installation kernel (the one you install from the CD-ROM or the one on the boot floppy) is made for an Intel 386, not a Pentium Pro.
