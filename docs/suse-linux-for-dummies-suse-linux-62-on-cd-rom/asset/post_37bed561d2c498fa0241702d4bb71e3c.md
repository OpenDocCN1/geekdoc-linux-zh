
# Chapter 3: Discovering What's in Your System

Linux can be installed in 80MB of disk space, but for the graphical part of Linux, you need a total of about 300MB. If you install the default system with all the goodies, you will have installed a total of more than 650MB of computer programs, data, games, and so on. When considering whether your system needs more disk space or more RAM, remember that as a general rule, it is usually better.

Some early CD-ROM drives are not IDE; they attach directly to sound cards and other devices. Most Linux distributions tell you how to detect and use these. If you don't have an IDE/ATAPI or SCSI CD-ROM drive, you need to know the make and model number of your CD-ROM drive and what type of controller (perhaps a specific sound card) the CD-ROM drive is attached to. To do this, you have to open your system and look for the make and model number of the CD-ROM on the physical device.

You can install Linux, after making one or two disks, by copying files over the network. This is a viable way of installing Linux on the type of local area network used at colleges or businesses or you have a friend who has a Linux system, a CD-ROM drive, and a networking card of the same type as yours. If the network is not fast (that is, cannot pull lots of data quickly), however, the easiest way to install Linux is by using a CD-ROM drive attached to your system. Trust me.

You can install Linux on a notebook by using the notebook's built-in CD-ROM drive (if it has one), or a CD-ROM drive attached to the notebook's docking station, or a SCSI CD-ROM drive attached to a PCMCIA controller. If you don't have any of these, you could try to get a PCMCIA controller and do a network installation, given that another Linux system on the network has a CD-ROM drive.

You also need a keyboard, a mouse, and a video card. Linux supports a wide range of video cards. Even if your card is not supported directly, Linux might support it as a standard VGA, XGA, SVGA (or other graphical hardware standard) video card. Most video cards that have been available for a while are supported.

# Notebooks before PCMCIA support

Early notebooks had no built-in CD-ROMs, and until Linux had PCMCIA support, I had to install Linux using floppies. I still remember the day I got my new notebook and the new release of Linux that had PCMCIA support. I ran out and bought a PCMCIA SCSI controller that night so that I could use my SCSI CD-ROM to do the installation.

My life has been ever since. Having used floppies to install Linux on various notebooks not once or twice, seven times, I would have paid ten times the amount for what PCMCIA card.

# Finding Out What You Have

One of the most important preliminary steps for a successful installation of Linux is finding out the type of hardware you have. You can find most of this information in the manuals that came with your computer. The manuals won't be much help, however, if:

- You threw the manuals.
- You lost the manuals.
- You bought your computer secondhand from someone who threw away or lost the documentation.
- You bought your PC with Windows already installed.

The last reason is the most insidious because it means the people you bought the PC from did not bother buy the documentation for each part of the PC when they assembled it for you. That would have cost them more money. Instead, they used OEM parts (read that "sans documentation and colorful box") and bought only one copy of the documentation for their use. With Windows already installed, they reasoned, why would an end user need any documentation about the system?

Contact the place where you bought your system. Perhaps you can obtain a copy of the documentation from the dealer.

If that doesn't, open up your system and look at the components. For instance, a source of information about your disk is the label on top of the disk drive. If you don't feel comfortable opening your system, you might want to take it back to where you bought it — assuming that perhaps the dealer can open it up and tell you what you have.

You can also look to the World Wide Web for information. Many vendors use their Web site to provide technical data about their devices. For that, you need to know your equipment's manufacturer, make, and model number.

Another source of information is MS-DOS, Windows 3.1, and Windows 95. Step-by-step instructions in the following sections show you exactly how to use these operating systems to get all the information you need.

If you don't have any literature about your system, and you don't have Windows 3.1 or Windows 95 on your system (perhaps you like to run FAT free), and no dealer is within hundreds of miles, don't give up hope. Linux is good at sniffing out and identifying hardware during the installation.

# Disk controllers

The two main types of disks are IDE and SCSI, and each type has its own controller. IDE is more common in PCs, and newer PCs usually have two controllers: a master and a slave. Therefore, a PC with two controllers can have up to four disks. You should know which hard drive is which. Normally, on a Windows system that you want to preserve:

- The first controller's master disk is called C.
- The next disk is D and the slave disk on the first controller.
- The next disk is E and the master disk on the second controller.
- The last disk is F and the slave disk on the second controller.

Normally, Windows is located on your C disk and data is on your other disks. This lettering scheme is just one possibility; your disks could be set up differently and include CD-ROMs as drives on your IDE controllers.

Some high-end PCs have a SCSI controller on the motherboard or on a separate SCSI controller, either in addition to or instead of the IDE controllers. Older SCSI controllers can have up to 8 devices on them, numbered 0–7, including the controller. Newer SCSI controllers (known as wide controllers) can have up to 16 devices, including the controller.

If all you have SCSI, usually disk 0 or disk 1 is your C disk. If you have a mixture of IDE and SCSI controllers, your C disk could be on any of them. The sections later in this chapter, "Getting Information from Windows 95" and "Getting Information from MS-DOS or Windows 3.1," show you how to identify how many disks you have, what type they are, and the controllers to which they are attached.

# Disk drives

Most people think of Windows (either Windows 3.1, Windows 95, or Windows 98) as an operating system. All these, however, have an underlying system called MS-DOS that manages the disk and many hardware components of your PC. From here on, unless we mention a particular system, consider MS-DOS and Windows as the same thing.
