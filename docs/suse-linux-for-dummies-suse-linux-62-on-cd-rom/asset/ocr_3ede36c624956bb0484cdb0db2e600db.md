# # User Groups

User groups are springing up all over the country. Some are more active than others, but most have meetings at least once a month. Some groups are Linux only; others are attached to a larger computer group — either UNIX or a more general computer user association. User groups offer a good opportunity to ask questions. Also, user groups tend to stimulate ideas and methods of doing things.

You can find out whether a Linux user group is in your area by checking with GLUE (Groups of Linux Users Everywhere), which runs as a series by SSC (the publishers of the Linux Journal*). GLUE is an automated map of user groups and can be found at the following address:

```
www.ssc.com/glue
```

When you get to the site, click the button that says "Find a linux user group in your area."

![](bbox=[0.12, 0.465, 0.19, 0.545])

No user group in your area? Then leave a note saying you want to start one, and perhaps other people in your area will join you. Terrified at trying to start a user group? Leaders of user groups are often not the technically knowledgeable, but instead are simply good planners. They organize the meeting space, find (hound) speakers, sending out meeting notices, locate sponsors, arrange refreshments (beer), and perform other organizational tasks. Sometimes it seems like a thankless job, but when a meeting turns really well, it makes it all worthwhile. So, as a newbie to Linux, you might not know `grep(1)` from `awk(1)`, but you may still make a very good chairperson.

# # Bring in the Cavalry

Some people want to be able to hire people to manage or fix their systems. This is what the commercial computer world calls support. A lot of times, support is delivered by the place where you bought your computer, whether it's a store, a value-added reseller (VAR), or the manufacturer.

Linux has been criticized for not having this level of support. Although it's true that the number of hardware vendors that offers support is low, and more people are versed in Linux and are willing to offer some level of support, whether by e-mail, telephone, or on-site services.

The first group of people that offers support are the distribution makers. Red Hat Software, Inc., Calder, Inc., SuSE Inc., and others sell support contracts for their Linux distributions. These contracts range from e-mail support to telephone support.
