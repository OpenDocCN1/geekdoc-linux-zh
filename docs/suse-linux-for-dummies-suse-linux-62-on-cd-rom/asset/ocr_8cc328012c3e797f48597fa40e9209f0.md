Next, you need to know the following about your mouse:

+   - The model number and manufacturer
- The type of mouse it is (a PS/2 bus mouse or a serial mouse, as well as
then the make and model)

Again, your system's documentation should tell you this information, and
often the bottom of the mouse has some basic information.

A PS/2 mouse usually has a round connector on the end of its wire, or tail. A
serial mouse has an oblong connector with nine holes. All varieties of PS/2 bus
mice like the one to Linux. Different serial mice, however, have different
characteristics. If you have a serial mouse, you have to know its model number
and manufacturer, or whether it emulates some other-known mouse.

Three-button mouse are best with Linux, but you can get by with a two-button
mouse. Three-button mouse is very useful for cutting and pasting. If you are
using a two-button mouse, you can configure your Linux system to "emulate" at-
tree buttons. You can create the effect of the third, or middle, by pressing the
left and right buttons simultaneously. You can find out how to configure
mouse for your X window in the section of this chapter titled
"Configuration Conflagration.\ Please note that the increasingly pervasive
"wheel mouse" (the ones with the wheel between the two buttons) can func-
tion as a three-button mouse. You can configure the wheel to function, but
doing so is beyond the scope of this book.
