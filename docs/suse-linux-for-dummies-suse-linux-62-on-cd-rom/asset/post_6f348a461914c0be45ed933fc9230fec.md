## Becoming a geek

Susan Pink, one of my editors (a darn fine one), hates it when I say things like "fsck your file system." She wants me to say "run the fsck program against the file system." I have gone along with her for the most part, but here is why I tell you that UNIX (and Linux) geeks the following:

Geeks talk about "disks" when Susan would make me say "drive drives," and geeks change the words X, X11, and X Window System, while Susan personally interjects (and every time), "Did you mean to say 'X Window System?'" 

I also want to point out that geeks are not nerds. Geeks are people who love technical topics, but also have a wide range of other interests. Geeks are often outgoing, and many (but not all) enjoy being with people.

- fsck the file system instead of "run fsck on the file system"
- VEYE (rhymes with "EYE") instead of "VEE EYE" when talking about the editor "vi"

I point out that fsck is a noun, verb, and an adjective:

- I fsck.
- I fscked the file system.
- I put the files into the fscked file system.

but this concept makes her shudder.

One of my favorite T-shirts (I have more than 3,000 XL T-shirts) reads:

It hard being a Geek, but it is getting easier.

So, if you really start to know the Linux operating system and want to learn more, you may well be a closet geek. Welcome to the club.

Perhaps you looked at other books before you bought this one and were intimidated by their use of technical terms. For what you wanted to do, and you wanted something task-oriented. You may want to look over them again because your knowledge level will be higher after completing this book. TCP/IP networking, compiler design, operating system theory, formal language theory, computer graphics, and systems administration training are all topics you can study in depth.

# Chapter 17: Sources of Help

## Linux HOWTOs

Don't forget about the Linux HOWTOs, which are on the SuSE Linux For Dummies CD-ROM. These excellent guides are covered under the Linux Documentation Project copyleft, which means you can print them. You can also read them with more(1) or less(1). After you have installed SuSE Linux, the HOWTOs can be found in the directory /usr/doc/howto/en/

## School Days

Another source of information on UNIX and Linux is through formal courses, perhaps at a local community college. A lot of colleges have courses on UNIX, and many have started using Linux to teach the UNIX courses. With Linux, you can do a lot of your homework on your system or on your notebook anywhere. (I typed for the first edition of this book in a hotel in Auckland, New Zealand, and updated text for the second edition in the United Airlines lounge in Chicago.) ## In the News

You can obtain additional information on the Linux operating system from the Internet. A facility called netnews has tens of thousands of newsgroups, and each newsgroup is about a specific topic. Note more than 30 newsgroups devoted to Linux topics.

Access to reading netnews is usually a service provided by an ISP (Internet service provider). You can use a news reader (such as trn, tin, or pine) or one of several Web browsers (such as the Netscape browser included on your SuSE Linux For Dummies CD-ROM) to read netnews.

## User Groups

User groups are springing up all over the country. Some are more active than others, but most have meetings at least once a month. Some groups are Linux only; others are attached to a larger computer group — either UNIX or a more general computer user association. User groups offer a good opportunity to ask questions. Also, user groups tend to stimulate ideas and methods of doing things.

You can find out whether a Linux user group is in your area by checking with GLUE (Groups of Linux Users Everywhere), which runs as a series by SSC (the publishers of the Linux Journal*). GLUE is an automated map of user groups and can be found at the following address:

```
www.ssc.com/glue
```

When you get to the site, click the button that says "Find a linux user group in your area."

No user group in your area? Then leave a note saying you want to start one, and perhaps other people in your area will join you. Terrified at trying to start a user group? Leaders of user groups are often not the technically knowledgeable, but instead are simply good planners. They organize the meeting space, find (hound) speakers, sending out meeting notices, locate sponsors, arrange refreshments (beer), and perform other organizational tasks. Sometimes it seems like a thankless job, but when a meeting turns really well, it makes it all worthwhile. So, as a newbie to Linux, you might not know `grep(1)` from `awk(1)`, but you may still make a very good chairperson.

## Bring in the Cavalry

Some people want to be able to hire people to manage or fix their systems. This is what the commercial computer world calls support. A lot of times, support is delivered by the place where you bought your computer, whether it's a store, a value-added reseller (VAR), or the manufacturer.

Linux has been criticized for not having this level of support. Although it's true that the number of vendors that offers support is low, and more people are versed in Linux and are willing to offer some level of support, whether by e-mail, telephone, or on-site services, the first group of people that offers support are the distribution makers. Red Hat Software, Inc., Calder, Inc., SuSE Inc., and others sell support contracts for their distributions. These contracts range from e-mail support to telephone support.

Another group of people offering support are resellers of Linux systems. These people typically choose one or more versions of Linux and install them on hardware that you purchase from them. They will repair your system when it breaks (all with a fee).

The final group of people who offer support are independent consultants who have learned about Linux and are now in the business of offering support for the system. A list of consultants is available from a document called the Consultants HOWTO, which is available at the following address:

```
www.cyrius.com/tbm=Consultants-HOWTO
```

## Commercial Applications

While we're talking about commercial support, we should mention something about commercial applications. A lot of Linux users are frugal (we prefer term rather than *cheap*) and often want or need to use freely distributable software. And in many cases, the freely distributable software is very good and does exactly what they want. Other times, though, the application you want is available only commercially.

Other people who use Linux buy the latest and greatest hardware and vendors everywhere may be reading this, I cannot stress enough that you should look at Linux like any other operating system. You can sell your applications to a market that is not only appreciated but also believes, for various reasons, that its operating system should be open.

Take an example of functionality available from the commercial world, Applyx, a word processor, a spreadsheet, a presentation package, a mail front end, an HTML authoring program, a graphical program for drawing pictures (usually to include in your presentation or paper), an application building program, and a scripting language that allows you to tie these and other programs together.
