# Chapter 11: Windows on the World

## Window Tricks

You don't like windows? Luckily, KDE does all the hard work for you. You simply click here and drag windows around, make them big, make them small, make them any size you want. In this section, you practice the window tricks using the several windows you have on the screen.

Before doing something to a window, however, you have to get its attention. When a window has your attention, it's said to have focus, or to be active.

You can give a window focus with KDE in several ways:

- Click the window's name, which is in the task bar at the top of the window
- Hold the Alt and Tab keys down together and you will see a list of the currently open applications. By using the Tab key, you can scroll through the list. By releasing the two keys, you will make the currently selected application active, or in focus.
- Click the window itself, which typically also makes the window the topmost one.
- Shout "Hey, you!"

When the window is in focus, it remains until you select another window to be in focus using one of the above methods. For example, you can use KDE options that allows you to make a window focus simply by moving your pointer over it. Unfortunately, we can't cover every feature of KDE in this book, so we leave it to the curious readers to find out!

## Moving windows

Your screen should still be crammed with windows you opened in the last section: xman, xpaint, xedit, and any other programs. You start your window tricks by moving a window:

1. Give the xman focus. Remember, you give window focus by clicking the window's icon, or somewhere in the window itself.
2. Click the menu at the top left of title bar. A menu appears, as shown in Figure 11-6.
img/96a9ce6a13d8d15ed05f867f41574278_225_0.png
3. Click Move. You will notice the cursor changes shape.
4. Move the mouse to the next location and click the left mouse button when the window is in proper position.

Another way to move a window is to place the cursor in the center of the window's title bar and then drag the window. Release the left mouse button when the window is in place.

## Resizing to content

Sometimes a window is a too big or too small. To do that, follow these steps:

1. Choose a window and give it focus.
2. Click the menu symbol. The Window menu appears.
3. Click Resize. Your cursor jumps to the bottom-right corner of the window and changes shape again.
4. Move the cursor up and to the left just a bit. As you do this, you will notice that the window is changing shape. When you resize the window, it is divided into nine parts: four corners, four sides, and one center. The center section doesn't do anything in resizing. The four sides allow resizing in one direction (straight). The four corners allow resizing in two directions at once; by grabbing a corner, you can make the window both wider and taller.
5. When the window is the right size and shape, click the left mouse button once.

Another way to resize a window using KDE is to simply grab window border or side with your mouse cursor and drag. To grab window border, move your cursor slowly across either corner or side near the center. As you move slowly across the window border, the cursor changes. At that point, hold the mouse button and drag the window border to where you want it. Then release the mouse button.

## Minimizing

If you want to make the window disappear for a while without stopping its program, we have just you:

1. Click the menu symbol.
2. Click Iconify. Where did your program go? Check the status bar at the top of the screen and you'll find it.
3. To bring that window back, click its icon in the status bar. You have not made the window bigger, you have also given it focus.

Now that you had through the menu, do you want to know an easier way to minimize a window? Look at Figure 11-7. See those squares in the title bar? Click the one that is third from the right (it looks like a dot) and your program is minimized. That dot is called the Minimize button.

To minimize your window, click the button that is second from the right (it looks like a square). The window takes over the entire screen—sometimes with results depending on the subject. To bring the window back to its original size, you need to use the resize option you just found if you read the section “Resizing to content.” You did not forget how, did you? Stay tuned to find out about the button to the right of the Maximize button.

## Virtual work on Your Virtual Desk

You can see at this point that you can have lots and lots of windows on the screen. You might even lose a window behind other windows. Do you wish that your monitor was larger or that you have more than one monitor attached to your system, just so that you could open more windows and do more things at one time? Or maybe you don't want to close down or minimize one application before going to another?

Well, monitors are expensive, and a lot of systems can have only one graphics, but you are stuck with a single monitor. But you do not have to be stuck with one screen. X Window System (with the help of KDE) can give you virtual desktops.

A virtual desktop enables you to have a screen full of applications. By clicking a button or moving your cursor to a particular location, you can display an additional screen of different applications.

You may have noticed that on the bottom of your screen, in the middle of the panel, there are four buttons labeled One, two, three and four, as shown in Figure 11-8. These buttons represent the different virtual desktops that are available. The one labeled is the desktop that you are in right now.

## Scroll bars, menus, and buttons

X Window System has gone through a lot of changes. The original goal was to make it informal, so that it could make windows, create patterns, draw lines, and so on, but was not governed by policies about how scroll bars, menus, and buttons should look or work. Although everyone's scroll bars, menus, and buttons tended to look and feel almost the same, but not quite. It was hard to program every line of a menu and every character had to be coded by a skilled programmer, and programmers were continually reinventing the wheel (although the wheel had been around quite a long time before computers). Eventually, the X Window System community was fed up and developed a set of industry-standard libraries called Motif Open Software Foundation (OSF).
