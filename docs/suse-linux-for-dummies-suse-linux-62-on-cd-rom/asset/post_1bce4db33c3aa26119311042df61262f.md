To find a good ISP, first look in the local Yellow Pages under Internet or Internet Service Providers. List the names and telephone numbers of any you find. These companies are probably local companies, which is fine if you want to communicate from your home only. If you do a lot of traveling, you may want to call the following national providers:

- AT&T WorldNet: 800-967-5363
- Mindspring: 888-677-7464
- Earthlink: 800-395-8425

Next, talk to some friends who live close to you and ask them ISP they use and what they think of their service. If a service has used local access numbers, you get busy signals a lot. Also ask about the technical help, in some services is poor or nonexistent.

Finally, call the ISPs and tell them you have a Linux system and are interested in PPP services, with e-mail and news. Find out they have access numbers in your local calling area. You'll end up making a long-distance call each time you connect. Check their prices and whether they have unlimited service (a flat rate no matter how many hours you are logged). A typical price for unlimited service is $20 per month. ISPs that host Web pages are a plus, as are ISPs who can filter j-email, or spam.

This is a good time to verify that your own telephone service is flat rate and not metered.

Tell the ISP the highest speed of your modem. You will probably also be asked for a login name and a password for the ISP's server, so you may want to have these written down ahead of time.

# Chapter 15: Setting Up Your Internet Connection

After you choose your Internet service provider and arrange payment, the ISP provides you with certain pieces of information, including the following:

- Telephone access numbers
- A username (usually the one you supplied)
- A password (usually the one you supplied)
- An e-mail address, which is typically your username added to its domain name
- A primary Domain Name Server (DNS) number, which is a large number separated by periods into four groups of digits
- A secondary Domain Name Server (DNS) number, which is another number separated by periods into four groups of digits
- An SMTP (mail) server name
- An NNTP (news) server name
- A POP3 server name, which is used to download e-mail from the ISP's server to your machine

The ISP might also give you software on a CD-ROM or a floppy to help you get your account working, but this software probably works only with Microsoft operating systems. That's okay, because your Linux distribution has more than enough software to use with the Internet and to help you get your account working.

# Connecting Your Modem

To use Linux with the Internet, you must have at least one of these devices:

- a serial modem
- An ISDN line
- A broadband connector of some type (such as Ethernet, FDDI, a cable modem, or ATM)

For the second and third items, or someone you know (your boss, your systems administrator, or your 10-year-old) probably arranged for you to get the connection and even set up your computer for it. The serial modem is a device that you might have chosen yourself, or it may have come with your computer, and therefore you may have little knowledge of how to use it with Linux. For the rest of this chapter, we assume that you are connecting your system to the Internet using a serial modem and a telephone line.

Modems, quite simply, do three main functions:

- Convert digital information into tones that go across telephone lines (and vice versa)
- Dial the telephone number you want to reach and make the connection with a modem at the other end
- Negotiate with the modem at the other end for the speed to use when sending bits of information

# Types of modems

Modems can be either internal or external. An external modem stays in its little box and usually plugs into a wall outlet for power, the telephone line for the connection, and into a serial connector on the back of your computer. An internal modem is usually plugged into a slot on the motherboard inside the computer case. (Some internal modems are built directly onto the motherboard.) To the computer and operating system, an internal modem appears as if it were two or more serial ports. Internal modems draw their power from the computer system, and they plug into the phone jack.

Usually, internal modems are less expensive than their external counterparts, but some people like external modems because they can hook them up to different computer systems by simply unplugging the serial line from one system box and plugging it into another.

A third type of serial line modem is a PCMCIA card (sometimes called PC card). These are used most often with laptop computers.

Internal modems come in two flavors: hardware and software. Winmodems are not supported by Linux. If you have an external modem or an internal modem that is jumpered, you can skip ahead to the section titled "Finding the serial line." # Configuring a plug-play modem

If you have a plug-play modem, you need to create a file. This must be done when you are logged in as the root. The steps to do this are:

- Use the pnpdump program to probe your plug-play devices and direct that output to the config file:

```
/sbin/pnpdump -c /etc/isapnp.conf
```

You can verify that it worked by having a look at the output of this command:

```
/sbin/isapnp /etc/isapnp.conf
```

This command will initialize your plug-play devices. When the system reboots, it will automatically issue this command, so you do not have to do it manually. If the /etc/isapnp.conf file is correct, you should see output something like this:

* Board 1 has serial identifier ea 89 82 ff ab 02 00 72
56: USR0002
Serial No 311273091 [checksum ea]
USR0002/311273091(U.Robotics Inc. Sportster 33600
Fax Internal: Port 0xEB; IR03 -- Enabled OK

If you receive a warning message, then you likely have a resource conflict; the only way to resolve this is by modifying the settings in the config file you created:

```
/etc/isapnp.conf
```

When you open this with your favorite text editor, you will see lots of information, which looks scary at first! Don't worry—most of the lines in this file are not relevant. This file contains a section for each device in your computer, which may look like this:
