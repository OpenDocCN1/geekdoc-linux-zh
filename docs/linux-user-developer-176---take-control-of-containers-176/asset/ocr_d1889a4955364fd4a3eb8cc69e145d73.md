# In brief: compare and contrast our verdicts

| | Clipperz | Passopolis | Encryptpr | KeePass |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **UI and design** | Vault entries are categorised by keywords, making navigation super easy | A minimal approach is one thing, but this is a little too barebones for our liking | An impressive dual-menu system is used to help keep all the different key tools separated | Fairly complicated to navigate around, with a steep learning curve for new users |
| **Adding and customising entries** | Entries can be as detailed, or as basic, as you deem necessary for your account | The creation process is easy, but lacks some core options we'd expect to see | Every entry can be filled into one of three types, with each having their own set of options | Each entry can be specifically tailored to your exact needs and choice of security |
| **Security** | No readable data is stored on your server, so there's little chance of being compromised | Provides everything you need to keep your account details safe and secure | No two-factor support here is an annoyance, but all the other options are here | Different layers of security can be applied to every entry that you create |
| **Extra features** | One-time passwords are ideal for accessing your files on an insecure device | The import system is handy, but incredibly slow when it comes to it working | Multi OS support means that you can access your account wherever you may be | While a repair system is on hand for any corrupted files, it's a little hit and miss |
| **Overall** | Clipperz offers a unique approach to what a password manager should be | Passopolis, in its current state, fails to compete with the stronger competition available | Encryptpr has brought every thing we love into one handy program that every user should have | KeePass is credible, but it's not recommended for those not used to this sort of software |

# AND THE WINNER IS...

# Encryptpr

The core technology behind password managers has dramatically changed over the past 24 months, and perhaps the most pleasing element about undertaking this group test is that some of the best Linux offerings have also moved with the times.

Each of the four open-source Linux password managers we tested here all have their strong points, but it was Encryptpr that really impressed us.

Simplicity plays a big role in Encryptpr's success, and everything from the initial set up process to adding your first entry can be done in a matter of minutes. Although we should mention that advanced users can still find what they need here, with many additional options for tailoring the program to your desktop also available.

![](bbox=[0.34, 0.54, 0.93, 0.76])

Each entry you make within the program will fit into one of three categories, with each supporting their own set of options to help fill in all the necessary details. It's something you don't necessarily find in other management systems, but it works a treat here. Of course, it's also simple to merge all your entries together, if and when you need to.

It's no slooch in the security department with Encryptpr's password generator on hand to make sure your details are kept safe. The program's links with Spidercake also plays a massive role, helping you and your account stay completely anonymous when you use it. One omission is two-factor authentication, but it's something we expect to see later.

While we highly recommend trying out both KeePass and Clipperz as a viable alternative password managers, it's Encryptpr's scope of features and usability that really make it stand out from the crowd. There really is no better way to keep your password and account details safe.
