bound. For example, 7 is an upper bound of 6; so are 9, 12, and 65. Subsequenty, when most people discuss function growth, they talk about the *least upper bound*, or a function that models both the upper and lower bounds. ² Professor Knuth, the father of the field of algorithmic analysis, describes this as big-theta notation and gives the following definition:

```
If $f(x)$ is big-theta of $g(x)$, then
$g(x)$ is both an upper bound and a
lower bound for $f(x)$.
```

Then, you can say that $\mathcal{F}(x)$ is of *order* $\mathcal{G}(x)$. The order, or big-theta, of an algorithm is one of the most important mathematical tools for understanding algorithms in the kernel. Consequently, when people refer to big-o notation, they are more often talking about the least such big-o, the big-theta. You really do not have to worry about this, unless you want to make Professor Knuth really happy.

# Time Complexity

Consider the original example of having to count the number of people in a room. Pretend you can count one person per second. Then, if there are 7 people in the room, it will take 7 seconds to count them. More generally, given $n$ people it will take $n$ seconds to count everyone. Thus, you can say this algorithm is $O(n)$. What if the task was to dance in front of everyone in the room? Because it would take the same amount of time to dance whether there were 5 or 5,000 people in the room, this task is $O(1)$.
