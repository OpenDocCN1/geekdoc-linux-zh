### Hackable butler

Hello Linux User & Developer, I've enjoyed reading Joey Bernard's recent articles about programming a JARVIS-like system for home automation with the Raspberry Pi. I did have a go at putting something together myself, which was very fun, but obviously quite a simple implementation. However, the idea of the project has really grown on me and I would now like to implement a more complete system. My question is this: can you recommend a system combining both open source software and hardware that I can then play around with and extend myself, to better fit the devices and needs I have at home?

Joe R

Hello Joe, We're really glad that you enjoyed Joey's tutorials! In terms of a more complete system for home automation, your choice is limited at the moment. Amazon Echo is probably the closest to what you are after in terms of the system and functionality, though it is proprietary and so not open to hacking and extension.

One project that we've stumbled across recently is Mycroft. It's another Kickstarter (kok.st/1hV2fNf) and at the time of writing it has just cleared its funding target with fifty hours to go ($107k/$99k), so the backers' units should ship out in July 2016 - this should fit your needs pretty perfectly. The Mycroft is a voice-controlled unit, inputting and outputting natural language, that can hook into online services and also interact with a selection of IoT and media devices. Mycroft can set alarms, send tweets, tell you the weather and read your calendar appointments. You can use regular expressions to perform specific shell commands. It can play music and videos, control devices like coffee makers, thermostats and wireless locks, or even connect to local Mycroft units for voice chat between rooms or house-wide announcements. The crucial part is not just the implementation on the software side and the reliability of the natural language input, but the sources that Mycroft can interact with. Media can be sourced from Netflix, Spotify, Pandora, YouTube, Plex and NPR. It can also be outputted to Chromecast and Roku devices. It can control WeMo, Philips Hue and Nest gadgets, plus SmartThings and Iris Smart hubs. In addition, it acts as both a source and a destination for IFTTT. It's a very solid start. In terms of openness, it is based on the Raspberry Pi 2 and an Arduino-compatible microcontroller with Snappy Ubuntu Core and open APIs powering everything. The team promises to produce comprehensive documentation on all the hardware and software, too. Since there's a Pi inside this open system, you'll be able to customise your Mycroft and share your own mods. It's an interesting project and worth keeping an eye on: mycroft.ai.

### Linux on the go

Hi there. I’m a computing student and do a lot of my development work in Linux. I also quite like to work outside or in coffee shops. The problem is the battery life of my old laptop: I get into the flow of things and then the power LED lights up red, starting the countdown to 0% at the most inconvenient time! I have a couple of tablets lying around at home, so I’m wondering if there’s some way of putting Linux onto them?

Gemmy

Hi Gemma. One of the simplest solutions involves renting a cloud-based Linux server and then simply SSH-ing in using an app on your tablet, or using a VNC client if you need to use a GUI. We first discovered this solution a while back, courtesy of a series of interesting blog posts by Mark O’Connor – have a read through the articles yourself and see what you think: yieldthought.com/search/linux.

Essentially, Mark rents an SSD server from Linode (linode.com) and hooks into it from his iPad using a combination of iSSH and GNU Screen. He seems quite pleased with the setup in his posts and we’ve certainly been tempted to give it a go. Plenty of others have, looking at the responses, and it seems to be a reliable system.

The only issue with recreating his system is that the iSSH app is now defunct. Not to worry though, we can recommend a couple of replacements: Prompt 2 for iPads and JuiceSSH for Android tablets, depending on the devices that you own. As for getting a GUI up, we like using the VNC Viewer app by RealVNC, available on both iTunes and Google Play. For the server end, you can check out the remote desktop tutorials on Linode’s website: bit.ly/1iE2XQX.

It shouldn’t take too long to get your tablets set up with these apps and Linode registration is very simple. User feedback for the service is excellent too, with incredibly quick response times should you need to get in touch with customer service.

Linode is also affordable, even on a student budget – pricing plans start at $10 per month for a single-core plan with 1GB of RAM, rising for the 2GB and 4GB plans to a maximum of $68 and six cores for $80 per month (though you probably won’t need that). The service comes with a great Linode Manager app (both Android and iOS) that enables you to administer your server without having to use third-party SSH or VNC apps.

Once you set up on Linode, you should then be able to work on your tablets without having to worry about finding a way to install Linux. It’s certainly possible with Android (check out issue #144), but as far as we know there isn’t yet a reliable way of getting a fully-functional Linux OS working on an iPad, outside of virtualisation or some really hacky workarounds.
