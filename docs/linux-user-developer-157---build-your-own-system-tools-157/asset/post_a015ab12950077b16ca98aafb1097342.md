## Linux Foundation announces the Open Mainframe Project

### Brings together industry and academic partners

The Linux Foundation has announced a project in partnership with industry giants to advance an enterprise-grade platform for Linux on mainframe devices, unsurprisingly dubbed the Open Mainframe Project.

The project brings together industry giants ADP, CA Technologies, IBM and SUSE as founding platinum members, while BMC, Compuware, LC3, RSM Partners and Viron Infinity join as silver-level members. Its formation comes as industry demand for mainframe systems resurges thanks to interest in cloud computing and big data topics.

![](img/e7f197876caa0b235fb5da2d16a578ea_5_0.png)

“Linux today is the fastest-growing operating system in the world. As mobile and cloud computing become globally pervasive, new levels of speed and efficiency are required in the enterprise and Linux on the mainframe is poised to deliver,” claimed Jim Zemlin, executive director at the Linux Foundation, at the project’s announcement. “The Open Mainframe Project will bring the best technology leaders together to work on Linux and advanced technologies from across the IT industry and academia to advance the most complex enterprise operations of our time.”

The Open Mainframe Project is an official Linux Foundation Collaborative Project and aims to leverage the strengths of Linux in order to take advantage of the unique features of mainframe hardware. This includes the LinuxONE family, announced the same day by IBM and Canonical.

As well as industry members, several academic institutions have confirmed membership of the project, including the University of Bedfordshire. “This is an exciting time to be involved in enterprise technologies. The potential benefits of open source systems optimised to run on the mainframe’s highly scalable and reliable architecture are very significant,” claimed Herbert Daly, senior lecturer in computer science and technology at the University. “The Open Mainframe Project offers our staff and students a fantastic opportunity to collaborate with an outstanding field of talent, dedicated to shaping the future of business-critical enterprise computing.”

One of the first tasks for the Open Mainframe Project is the establishment of a neutral home for community meetings, events and collaborative discussions, providing structure around the business and technical governance of the project. On a technical level, the group aims to broaden the tools and resources that are intended to drive development and collaboration of mainframe Linux, while also coordinating mainframe improvements to upstream projects. This is in order to increase the quality of these code submissions and ease upstream collaboration.

“Demand has grown among academic, corporate and government partners for furthering the mainframe’s expansion into the open ecosystem,” claimed Ross Mauri from IBM. “IBM looks forward to contributing our expertise to the community and learning from others to find innovative ways to combine the flexibility of open-source software with the world-class speed, security and scalability of the mainframe.”

## Mainframe

### IBM and Canonical launch LinuxONE

#### Mainframe includes custom Ubuntu distribution

IBM and Canonical have jointly announced the launch of a new Linux-powered mainframe family, the IBM LinuxONE, featuring a variant of Canonical's Ubuntu Linux distribution.

Announced on the same day that IBM joined the Open Mainframe Project as a founding member, the LinuxONE family is based on IBM's z Systems platform and launches in two flavours. Firstly, the top-end IBM z13-based LinuxONE Emperor, offering a claimed ability to run up to 8,000 virtual machines simultaneously. Secondly, a scaled-down variant dubbed the LinuxONE Rockhopper, designed for entry-level mainframe users.

“Fifteen years ago, IBM surprised the industry by putting Linux on the mainframe, and today more than a third of IBM mainframe clients are running Linux,” claimed Tom Rosamilia, senior vice president of IBM's Systems division. “We are deepening our commitment to the open source community by combining the best of the open world with the most advanced system in the world, in order to help clients embrace new mobile and hybrid cloud workloads. Building on the success of Linux on the mainframe, we continue to push the limits beyond the capabilities of commodity servers that are not designed for security and performance at extreme scale.”

IBM has also pledged to provide open access to the LinuxONE platform with the launch of a developer cloud platform. Hosted at academic institutions as well as IBM facilities in Dallas, Beijing and Böblingen, instances will enable free trials and educational access at no charge.

“The largest enterprises in the world count on Ubuntu as their platform for scale-out applications, private and public clouds. With the expansion of Ubuntu support to IBM z Systems and LinuxONE, IBM mainframe customers will benefit from Ubuntu's ecosystem of scale-out and cloud solutions,” boasted Canonical's Jane Silber of the partnership. “IBM and Canonical teaming to bring Ubuntu to z Systems allows customers to leverage the speed and security of z Systems, and the open source software and tools of Ubuntu, to enhance their IT operations for a new universe of customers.”

Both LinuxONE mainframe models are available to order from IBM now, with pricing provided by the company upon request.

### Features in KDE Plasma 5.4.0

- 1. **Application Dashboard**
  A new full-screen menu, the Application Dashboard, takes over from the Application Menu with a focus on making full use of high-resolution screens. This is in order to find your software and files as quickly as possible, while including easy keyboard navigation and type-to-search functionality for the mouse-averse.

- 2. **Wayland preview**
  The latest Plasma release brings the new Wayland integration into, giving those who like to live on the cutting edge a chance to play with the new Wayland windowing system, without having to give up their existing KDE-framework integration.

- 3. **Network applet graph**
  If you would like to keep an eye on historical network traffic, you will be pleased to know that a new graph is set to appear in the network applet. The applet also gains SSH and SSTP-VPN support via two new default plug-ins.

- 4. **Easy monitor calibration**
  The gamma monitor calibration tool is new to KDE Plasma 5.4.0 and offers an easy way to adjust how colours are reproduced on your display, providing either point colour matching or simple adjustments to your personal preference.

- 5. **New icons galore!**
  For those who like a bit extra clarity, the news that KDE Plasma 5.4.0 includes 1,400 new icons providing Breeze-themed artwork, covering a range of applications and services, will be welcomed. These include such apps as Inkscape, Firefox and LibreOffice.

## Opinion

### Free Software

#### Software communes

The contribution of the free software movement, both in ideas and hard code, to the rise of Linux isn't always given its due recognition

![](img/e7f197876caa0b235fb5da2d16a578ea_7_0.png)

![](img/e7f197876caa0b235fb5da2d16a578ea_7_1.png)

October is the 30th anniversary of the founding of the Free Software Foundation, a moment that was barely noticed in its time but can be said to have transformed the development of software. The concepts that define free software grew out of Richard Stallman's experiences at the AI Labs at MIT, his personal history with the development of the LISP machine and his disputes with James Gosling over the licensing of Emacs. As early as October 1981, Stallman had written: “Emacs does not cost anything; instead, you are joining the Emacs software-sharing commune. The conditions of membership are that you must send back any improvements you make to Emacs, including any libraries you write... which could be seen as an early attempt to define copyleft and the first principles of the free software movement.

![](img/e7f197876caa0b235fb5da2d16a578ea_7_2.png)

James Gosling, who was later famous for the development of Java, wrote a version of Emacs, derived from Stallman's original concept of Emacs, in C for Unix. According to Stallman, he “seemed to have been influenced by the same spirit of sharing and cooperation of the original Emacs... He wrote in a manual that he called the program Emacs hoping that others in the community would improve it until it was worthy of that name.” Gosling initially enabled free distribution of the source code, to which others had contributed, and Stallman incorporated some of the code in early versions of GNU Emacs, but in April 1983, according to Stallman, “he staked everyone in the back by putting copyrights on it, making people promise not to redistribute it, and then selling it to a software house.”

Gosling had sold GDSMACS, his version of Emacs, to UniPress and quasi-legal threats were made against Stallman. When GNU Emacs was released in the year 1985, UniPress announced that “portions of the GNU Emacs program are most definitely not public domain, and that use and/or distribution of the GNU Emacs program is not necessarily proper.”

Stallman rewrote the offending sections of GNU Emacs, but the confrontations with Gosling and UniPress had convinced him that public domain was not sufficient to protect the rights of free software. Stallman's solution was to invert copyright law and reassign the rights to the user. The first tangible version of such a licensing regime was 'the GNU Emacs copying permission notice' in 1985, although the GPL as we know it first appeared in 1989. The GPL, and its preamble were the most complete expression of the principles of free software, but the moment that made free software a concept to be reckoned with was the release of the Linux kernel. Linux had been developed using the GNU tools and was licenced under the GPL.

Linus Torvalds later said that “making Linux GPL'd was definitely the best thing I ever did”. The informality of the model that grew around GNU/Linux and the GPL gave everybody the chance to take part and make a difference. You could do anything with Linux, bash it to pieces, install it on your virtual fridge or washing machine, share it or break it, create your own distribution and share it with the world. If you couldn't write code, you could write documentation, host websites, make friends or sell t-shirts.

> The informality of the model that grew around GNU/Linux and the GPL gave everybody the chance to take part and make a difference.

This surprising effect extended to the commercial world. Once more, as one hardware company had formed a commitment to Linux, it became obvious that there was a mutual advantage in contributing back to the project and they did. The framework that made this possible was the GPL. The release of core chunks of corporate code accelerated the development of Linux and ensured its success in the enterprise, enhanced by its portability across a wide range of hardware. The GPL encouraged commoditisation, and at the system level, commoditisation reduced the costs and subsequently benefited everybody.

Commoditisation of the operating system ensures commoditisation of standards. This makes it easier to port software between different machines and architectures, and makes it easier to design hardware itself. Companies don't use GNU/Linux because of sentimental attachment to Linux or the GPL, or “open source”. They use GNU/Linux and free software because it makes business sense. And free software, guaranteed by the GPL, gave assurance of the continued freedom and community of the code. The contribution of the free software movement, both in ideas and hard code, to the rise of Linux isn't always given the recognition it deserves.
