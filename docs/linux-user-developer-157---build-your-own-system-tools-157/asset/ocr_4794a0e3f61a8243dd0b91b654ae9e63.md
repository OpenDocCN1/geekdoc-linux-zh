October is the 30th anniversary of the founding of the Free Software Foundation, a moment that was barely noticed in its time but can be said to have transformed the development of software. The concepts that define free software grew out of Richard Stallman's experiences at the AI Labs at MIT, his personal history with the development of the LISP machine and his disputes with James Gosling over the licensing of Emac s. As early as October 1981, Stallman had written: “Emacs does not cost anything; instead, you are joining the Emacs software-sharing commune. The conditions of membership are that you must send back any improvements you make to Emacs, including any libraries you write... which could be seen as an early attempt to define copyleft and the first principles of the free software movement.

![](bbox=[0.38, 0.34, 0.57, 0.53])

James Gosling, who was later famous for the development of Java, wrote a version of Emacs, derived from Stallman's original concept of Emacs, in C for Unix. According to Stallman, he “seemed to have been influenced by the same spirit of sharing and cooperation of the original Emacs... He wrote in a manual that he called the program Emacs hoping that others in the community would improve it until it was worthy of that name.” Gosling initially enabled free distribution of the source code, to which others had contributed, and Stallman incorporated some of the code in early versions of GNU Emacs, but in April 1983, according to Stallman, “he staked everyone in the back by putting copyrights on it, making people promise not to redistribute it, and then selling it to a software house.”

Gosling had sold GDSMACS, his version of Emacs, to UniPress and quasi-legal threats were made against Stallman. When GNU Emacs was released in the year 1985, UniPress announced that “portions of the GNU Emacs program are most definitely not public domain, and that use and/or distribution of the GNU Emacs program is not necessarily proper.”

Stallman rewrote the offending sections of GNU Emacs, but the confrontations with Gosling and UniPress had convinced him that public domain was not sufficient to protect the rights of free software. Stallman's solution was to invert copyright law and reassign the rights to the user. The first tangible version of such a licensing regime was 'the GNU Emacs copying permission notice' in 1985, although the GPL as we know it first appeared in 1989. The GPL, and its preamble were the most complete expression of the principles of free software, but the moment that made free software a concept to be reckoned with was the release of the Linux kernel. Linux had been developed using the GNU tools and was licenced under the GPL.

Linus Torvalds later said that “making Linux GPL'd was definitely the best thing I ever did”. The informality of the model that grew around GNU/Linux and the GPL gave everybody the chance to take part and make a difference. You could do anything with Linux, bash it to pieces, install it on your virtual fridge or washing machine, share it or break it, create your own distribution and share it with the world. If you couldn't write code, you could write documentation, host websites, make friends or sell t-shirts.

> The informality of the model that grew around GNU/Linux and the GPL gave everybody the chance to take part and make a difference.

This surprising effect extended to the commercial world. Once more, as one hardware company had formed a commitment to Linux, it became obvious that there was a mutual advantage in contributing back to the project and they did. The framework that made this possible was the GPL. The release of core chunks of corporate code accelerated the development of Linux and ensured its success in the enterprise, enhanced by its portability across a wide range of hardware. The GPL encouraged commoditisation, and at the system level, commoditisation reduced the costs and subsequently benefited everybody.

Commoditisation of the operating system ensures commoditisation of standards. This makes it easier to port software between different machines and architectures, and makes it easier to design hardware itself. Companies don't use GNU/Linux because of sentimental attachment to Linux or the GPL, or “open source”. They use GNU/Linux and free software because it makes business sense. And free software, guaranteed by the GPL, gave assurance of the continued freedom and community of the code. The contribution of the free software movement, both in ideas and hard code, to the rise of Linux isn't always given the recognition it deserves.
