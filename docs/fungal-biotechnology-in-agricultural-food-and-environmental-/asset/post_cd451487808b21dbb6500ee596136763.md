### 1. INTRODUCTION

The problem of food spoilage has plagued humans throughout history. As humans evolved from gathering and hunting to raising crops, they were forced to store their own food. (Wait, "were forced to store their own food"). Early attempts to preserve food involved sugars, salts, and smoke. Today, however, preservation has used factors such as water activity (aw), pH, and temperature to control growth. No matter which factor is selected, the effectiveness of the antimicrobial depends on its chemical properties and safety in food (2001).

Fungi are the most common organisms given their ability to grow under diverse environmental conditions. Environmental conditions such as water activity (aw), pH, and temperature can be manipulated to control growth. However, measures need to be taken to exclude fungi; some species have adapted to extreme environmental conditions. Using water activity as an example, most molds are inhibited by aw of 0.80, although xerophilic molds can grow as low as 0.65 (Farkas 1997).

Most fungi are little affected by pH over short term commonly 3–8 months. Some molds grow at pH 2.0, and at pH 7 (MCSFF 80; Rahman and Labuza 1999). However, as pH moves away from an organism's optimum growth range, typically 5.0 for fungi, the effect of other growth-limiting factors becomes more apparent.

Fungi are generally inactivated by heat treatments such as pasteurization, although some heat-resistant molds with fruits and fruit products can survive severe heat treatments like pasteurized juices. Many molds grow over a wide range of temperatures below 0°C while others grow up to 50°C.

Oxygen is a requirement for growth. Many species are oxygen scavengers and grow in atmospheres that can reach 1.0% O2 (Cerny 1979). From a practical standpoint, it is difficult to inhibit mold growth in foods solely by exclusion of oxygen by packaging, which quickly equilibrates with the package headspace, and oxygen can leach through all the most impermeable packages.

Antifungal additives are effective, cost-effective, and are the only successful way to control growth in foods. Antifungal food additives are basically chemicals which can be found occurring naturally in foods such as organic acids and esters, or may be added to food during processing (Zaid 2000; Thompson and Singh 2000). The various antifungal additives are briefly outlined in this chapter.
