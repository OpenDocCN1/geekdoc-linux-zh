# 1 INTRODUCTION

At present, approximately 200 different fungicides have been introduced into agriculture and horticulture worldwide. Despite the enormous advances in chemical management of fungal diseases, some of the important pathogens such as vascular wilt, anthracnose-take-of-wheat, and other root infections remain uncontrolled by current fungal chemicals (Smith et al. 1997). The development of resistant strains of pathogens and the increasing concern about pesticides have intensified the need for better and safer compounds in novel modes of action, lower rates of use, and low toxicological and environmental risk. Commercial requirements for fungicides are making it increasingly difficult to discover new classes of compounds to justify the effort and cost of development. In order to discover fungicides that meet different characteristics, the exploitation of biological agents is becoming mainstream in antifungal research.

Microbial metabolites are represented by a number of chemical and biological fungicides. Microbial metabolites are capable of providing exquisite chemical structures with diverse biological activities beyond the scope of organic chemistry (Porter 1985). An unexpected and newly chemical structure is more likely to have new antifungal activity and mode of action, especially showing less resistance to current fungicides (Früh et al. 1996). Biodegradability is the next property of microbial metabolites that cannot be overlooked. They degrade unduly within a month or a few days, exposed to agricultural environment, thus leading to the residual level less harmful to the natural ecosystem (Tanaka and Omura 1993).

Microbial metabolites can be exploited in a number of different ways. They can be directly used as fungicides or for crop development. Antifungal activity is not the only factor to decide whether the metabolite is suitable for commercial use. Also, it should have chemical stability.

It is very unlikely that a newly discovered will have all the desired properties. Moreover, by using microbial metabolites as lead compounds.

In fact, more promising and effective strategy for the development of new fungicides is to use knowledge of the structure of natural compounds to synthesize derivatives with optimized activity, biological, and environmental properties. The activity of natural products can be improved by chemical modification. This approach relies heavily on the availability of quality chemical starting materials and the development of synthetic methodology. The fungicides that were developed in this year are fenbachil and fludiosiol (Nyfefer and Ackerman 1992) and synthetic derivatives of antibiotics from streptothricamycin and koremosin-methyl (Anke et al. 1977; Godfrey 1994). Such a derivative synthesized from microbial metabolites not only enhanced efficacy but also improved properties such as photochemical stability, cytotoxicity, and phytotoxicity.
