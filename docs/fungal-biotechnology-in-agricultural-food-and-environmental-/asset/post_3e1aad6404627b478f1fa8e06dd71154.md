Three staining systems are used for iszyme analysis: positive, negative, and fluorescent. The vast majority of iszyme visualization systems are based on positive staining. The general idea is that activity of a specific enzyme generates a colored precipitate at zones of activity. These enzymes are visualized against a background (Figure 1). Frequently used staining is visualized as light-colored bands against a dark gel. Negative staining systems include those for using superoxide dismutase and catalase. A third class of staining works by generating bands when exposed to ultraviolet light. Commonly used fluorescent staining systems include those for amylase and β-glucosidase.

## INTERPRETATION OF ISZYME DATA

The best guide for interpreting iszyme banding patterns is to perform a genetic analysis. Unfortunately, for many fungi this may be difficult or impossible. The purpose of this section is to provide information for accurate interpretation of iszyme banding patterns without a genetic analysis. Accurate interpretation of iszyme banding patterns requires knowledge of the organization of the organism and the number of subunits required to form the enzyme. ## Interpretation of Banding Patterns in Haploids

For haploid fungi, each individual will produce a single band on a gel. Such isymes can be easily scored by assuming that band corresponds to an allele at a single genetic locus. Each allele can be named by its relative migration on the gel. The easiest approach is to designate the common allele and all other alleles can be identified by their relative distance to the 100 allele. For example, an allele migrating 14% faster than 100 would be 124, and 15% slower would be 82 (Figure 1). More bands per haploid hybrid may indicate multiple loci. If each allele at each locus mig within a limited, defined region of the gel then it may be possible to score each locus separately. Each allele can be named as an individual allele, with 100 and 200 to indicate the presence of two loci coding for the iszyme-6-phosphate isomerase. Enzymes giving more complicated banding patterns in haploids should be avoided as accurate interpretation is almost impossible without genetic analysis. "Null" alleles which show no band should be regarded with a nullity as it is difficult to distinguish a nullity from a null caused by faulty technique. All potential null alleles should be verified through electrophoresis which may turn out be caused by poor enzyme extraction from particular individuals or other problems with technique.

Interpretation of iszyme banding patterns in diploids and polyoids is complicated, and by whether an allele is mono- or multimeric. For monomeric enzymes, homozygous individuals will produce a single band on a gel as described for haploids. Haploids that are heterozygous at a locus for a monomeric enzyme will give two bands in a properly stained iszyme gel electrophoresis. Each band can be interpreted as a different allele at a single locus or as composed of two subunits. In these cases the subunits must join to form the enzyme. Because dimeric enzymes (those composed of two subunits) are most common, they will be the subject of this discussion. However, concepts covered here are directly applicable to enzymes composed of three or more subunits. To interpret isomer binding patterns by dimeric enzymes, it is necessary to understand how the subunits combine to form the active enzyme. In heterozygous individuals, the subunits produced by the two alleles at random in the cytoplasm of the cell form the active molecule. ## 5 APPLICATIONS OF ISOZYME ANALYSIS

Isozyme analysis can be applied to a variety of biological functions. These markers are considered to be selectively neutral or are ideal for looking sources of inoculum for pathogen fungus or gene flow populations. The markers can indicate whether species are haploid or diploid, can add strain or species identification, and can be used to identify hybridization either in the laboratory or in nature.

### 5.1 Taxonomy and Species Identification

Isozyme analysis was first applied to fungal taxonomy during the 1960s (Clare 1963; Hall 1967; Meyer 1964); Turner and since it has been applied at many taxonomic levels from individual strains to delimitation and identification of species. The best enzymes for taxonomic purposes are those that are monomorphic but different among. A caveat is that isozymes cannot be used to distinguish closely related strains with the same migration rate in a given set in field. Furthermore, it is not possible to indicate whether an allele is ancestral or to estimate the number of mutations that cause the alleles to migrate differently; alleles of similar size may be moved with different migration distances. Furthermore, clustering analysis such as neighboring joint (Saito and Nei 1987) are not appropriate for evaluation of races on isozyme analysis. Instead data are analyzed by calculating cluster distances using the Weighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean (UPGA) (Miller and Sokol 1975). These analyses can be performed with various computer programs such as NTSYS (Ryan 1990), PHYLIP (http://www.ubc.ca/~c/phylip/phylip.html), and PAUP (http://www.pup.edu). ### 5.2 Genetics

In addition to Mendelian genetics (Bonde et al. 1988; Burdon et al. 1986; Hall and Christopher 1991; Shockat et al. 1996a; Spelmanak et al. 1990) and isozyme analysis (May and Royse 1982b), isozymes can be used to identify non-progenitor from progeny in hybrid test (May and Royse 1982a; Burdon et al 1986b) and fungi (Goodwin and 1990), and to infer theploidy level of vegetative phase (Goodwin et al. 1994; Shockat et al. 1986). Dimeric enzymes are also used for this kind of analysis (Figure 3). Isozymes can also be used to analyze genetic variation in laboratories. Isozyme analysis impacted hybridization as proving the origin of a new species of cereal rust in Australia (Burdon et al. 1981) and naturally identifying occurrences by host field isolates of Phytophthora species. Evaluation of relationships through isozyme analysis among strains of anthracum brunescens used to aid the choice of parents in a mushroom breeding program (May and Royse 1982b).
