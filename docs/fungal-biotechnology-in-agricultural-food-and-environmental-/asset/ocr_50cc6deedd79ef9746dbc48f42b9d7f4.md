# 3 DECOLORIZATION OF DYES BY MICROORGANISMS

Synthetic azo, anthraquinone, triarylene, and other dyes are extensively utilized for textile dyeing, paper printing, and color photography. A necessary criterion for the application of these dyes is that they resist photodegradation and leaching. These dyes are also resistant to microbial degradation and are not easily removed from water by conventional biological treatment (Mayber 1981; Shaw 1991). The removal of dyes from water is achieved by elimination of the dyes using adsorption, chemical oxidation, and flocculation.

Azo dyes and aromatic rings are soft to nature and highly resistant to oxidative degradation. Partial degradation of azo dyes during activated sludge treatment, has been observed. Significant removal of color from the water was attributed to adsorption of the dye onto the substrate (Aftabzadeh 1991). The dyes are susceptible to anaerobic degradation. The reduction of azo dyes to colors produced by bacteria has been reported (Mayber 1981). The initial step in this transformation is the reductive cleavage of the azo group, resulting in the formation of aromatic amines. The decolorization of a number of textile dyes and effluents by an immobilized anaerobic consortium has also been demonstrated (N-Nagam 1996; Osseping 1996).

The decolorization of chrysosporium-induced dyes, such as Poly-B41, Poly-R8, and Poly Y-606, has been correlated with its lignin degrading degree (Sle and Gold 1983). Strains of Postreat have also been known to effectively decolorize Poly-B41 (Plat 1983); P. pulvinaceous has increased Poly-B decolorization, as well as enhanced laccase production. Laccase is produced by Pleotus sp. as part of its ligninolytic system by dye decolorization by this fungus

The utilization of the white-rot fungi and their nonspecific ligninolytic enzyme system for the biodegradation of other industrial dyes has increased interest, since it also became known that P. chrysosporium is able to decolorize and decolorize a range of sulfonated dyes, heterocyclic, and triphenylamine dyes (Bumpus and Brock 1988; Cripps et al. 1990; Paczynski et al. 1992; Sadler and Evans 1992). The triphenylamine dye, crystal violet was decolorized by P. chrysosporium. These metabolites were identified and the dye N-demethylated by fungal peroxidase providing evidence that the enzymatic system is least effective in oxidative biodegradation (Bumantag 1998). Paratone and car violet red were also decolorized.

A number of azo, triazine, and heterocyclic dyes can also be decolorized by strains of P postreat (Table 1). This further demonstrates the effectiveness of the ligninolytic system in biodegrading and decolorizing wastewater contaminated with dyes from textile and other industries (Cripps et al. 1990); Fluorescent acid (Gogya et al. 1992) and triazine dyes (K and Neto 1991) were also decolorized by P. postreat. Peroxidase was used by P. chrysosporium, although H2O2-enhanced decolorization by P. postreat has also been noted (Vyas and Molinar 2015). Knapp et al. (1995) suggests that more work is needed on the effect of the decolorizing capacity of other members of the Basomycetes.
