A widely accepted definition of an endophyte is: “entities systematically colonize the internal, tissues of hosts without even the endophyte, may after infection or latency period, cause” (Petrini 1991). This definition includes various mechanism which colonizes the tissues of plants. For example, plant-pathogen fungi, such as fungi; the endophyte shows symptoms after the infection (Stone et al. 2000). Endophytes are known to enhance plant tolerance to environmental stress, damage from harmful insects, and diseases caused by pathogens and nematodes. There are also studies on the plant growth effect of ending fungi (Yuan et al. 1998) but significant in plant weight, height, and shoot diameter in mycorrhizal-infected plants, 28 days after planting compared with uninoculated plants. Fusarium contorta inoculated with Phaeolopsis fortinii increased uptake of phosphorus and nitrogen, that resulted in enhanced growth of inoculated plants with noninoculated fungi (Jummonville and Trappe 1998).

## 3.2 Mycorrhiza

Initial evidence for the role of ectomycorrhizal fungi in disease suppression was provided by a field of observations that showed mycorrhizal-associated seedlings and roots of trees and gymnosperms were more resistant to pathogens than their nonmycorrhizal counterparts (Marx 1973). Ectomycorrhizal roots of Pinus spp and Spika (Picea species) were shown to be resistant to infectious endophytic fungal root rot (Lebovskina 1954). Richard et al. (1971) suggested that the suppression of ectomycorrhizal fungi, Sullus graminis in the substratum prevented any negative effect of endophytic Myizal. Hashimoto and Kawasaki (2001) showed that the ectomycorrhizal fungi suppressed the effect of endophytic M. radici-aditrovens in Betula japonica.
