Traditional fermented milks are made in many households. These products provide an important source for energy, protein, vitamins, and minerals (Loretan et al. 1998). Because of the economic changes that are taking place in Africa some traditional fermentation technology uses microflora (Loretan et al. 1998), interest in traditional fermented milks is increasing, especially examination and the identification of the high number of lactic bacteria and yeast and their role in the fermentation process of the milk (Loretan et al. 1998).

Examinations of different kinds of cheese showed that the yeast population often develops from a heterogeneous to a homogeneous population during the ripening period (Bockelman and Hoppe-Seyler 2001; Hansen et al. 2002; Petersen et al. 2002; van Tempel and Jakobsen 2008). Yeast need an energy source to grow, therefore the ability to assimilate or ferment carbohydrates and acids is important for the yeasts to survive in the cheese and to compete with other microorganisms during ripening (Rostila Feet 1996). Also the affinity towards NaCl and the effect of NaCl on the uptake of lactate and carbohydrates are important (Petersen et al. 2002). The assimilation of dairy carbohydrates seems to be affected by environmental factors. Investigations of Danish cheese showed the assimilation of Lactate (Petersen et al. 2002; van Tempel and Jakobsen 2000) and showed that it has specific affinity for some strains at (0% to 6%) lactose and in the (14% w/w) NaCl (then van Tempel and Jakobsen 2000). Similar results were seen for the assimilation of lactose and galactose. The pattern was observed for Y. lipolytica although inhibition only occurred at low levels of salt, i.e., (2% w/w) NaCl for some of the strains (examined then van Tempel and Jakobsen 2000).

## 4.6 Cheeses

Yeast are isolated from all sorts of cheeses (Bockelman and Hoppe-Seyler 2001; de Boer and Kuik 1997) Novitgard and Hardt 1988; Rostila and Feet 1996; Schmidt and Lenoir 1998; Jakobsen 2000; Ekanen et al. 1998. The number of yeast can be found in the range 10 c/g or higher. The most common yeast species in cheese are D. hanseni, Y. lipolytica, G. geotrichum, K. lactis, and other species, with D. hanseni as the most predominant. The development of yeast occurs spontaneously unless controlled use of yeast starters as cheeses are used for the production of some kind of mild ripened and ripened cheeses, but for others only briefly.

It was widely assumed that the yeast primarily originated from the cheese brine. Some of them are salted in brine containing 22–25% NaCl (Hansen et al. 2001) and the brine is not controlled or maintained between the salting of cheese to allow some tolerant yeast (Tourd and Byrd in 1993) of the (10^6 c/g)-Devoyd and Spomito 1970; Roh et al. 1992; Seeller and Busei (van Tempel and Jakobsen). It has also been suggested that the yeast originated from raw milk, but the survival of the yeast depends on the environment of the cheese (van Tempel; Vadillo 1980). In a study carried by Petersen et al. (2002) the successes of the yeast on the cheese of Danbo was found to be dependent on human factors. The dominating flora D. hanseni and the dominating strain did not originate from raw milk, or the starter culture, but from the dairy “microflora,” which also includes yeast in the ripening room, ripening process. This depends on an impact “microflora” where the balance can easily be disturbed because the cheese surface in many ways is exposed to the environment with its inherent population of micro-organisms (Bockelman and Hoppe-Seyler 2001).

### 4.6.1 Surface Ripened Cheeses

Semi-soft and soft cheeses that develop a smeared microbial growth on the surface of cured ripened cheeses have maturation times varying from several weeks to months and a temperature ranging from 10°C to 20°C. Examples of these are Limimburger, Danbo, and Tilster (1990; Bockelman and Hoppe-Seyler 2001; Petersen et al. 2002). The microbial nature on the surface of these cheeses is very important for the ripening process and a major role for the flavor and quality (Bockelman 2002; Hansen and Hoppe-Seyler 2001; Feet 1990; Narvus 1996). The cheese is a bio-mixture of dairy and bacteria (Bockelman and Hoppe-Seyler 2001; Feet 1990; Mann87 and Jakobsen 2000). The yeast species D. hanseni are dominant and the most important yeast during the maturation yet other yeast as Trichospor, Y. lipolytica, K. lactis, and D. anmophilus are present during the ripening (Bockelman 2002; Petersen et al. 2002). The microbiological ecology of the surface is very complex, but during the first days of cheese a natural selection takes place (Petersen et al. 2002). The most suitable group of yeast in the surface ripened cheeses, seems to easily adapt to the microenvironment with high NaCl concentration, low pH and lactate as main source. Among the yeast D. hansenii in moist grow fast and become the dominant species. It has been observed a particular subspecies over the other strain and benefit of D. hansenii (Petersen et al., 2002). The growth of D. hansenii on the surface of the cheese enhances the growth of smear bacteria by metabolizing the lactate (Leclerc-Perlat et al., 2019) which drops pH and allowing more-acidic B. linens to grow (Bockelman and Hoppe-Seyler 2001; Petersen et al. 2002). The number of yeast decreases after one or two weeks of ripening while the bacteria increases (Valdés-Saber et al. 1997). Furthermore, yeast appears to support the bacterial growth of vitamins and amino acids (Vilip 2001). Differences between observed strains against NaCl content and pH played an important role in the ripening process (Hansen et al. 2001). In four-week Danablu, the most common yeast are D. hansenii (van Tempel and Jacobsen 1998). Examinations of Danablu produced different varieties and the Danish cheese showed that the yeast population developed from a heterogeneous flora to a homogeneous flora of D. hansenii during the ripening period (Hansen et al., 2001; van Tempel and Jacobsen 1998). It is worthy to consider an important role in the ripening of blue cheese and contributes to positively microbial environment by assimilation of the residual carbohydrates and acids. Yeast are assured to create a stable microenvironment, which prevents undesirable microorganisms produced. The fermentation is likely to promote the development of P. roquefort (Coiggins 1979). Positive interactions detected between D. hansenii and P. roquefort simulation in Danablu (van Tempel and Jacobsen 2000). The yeast D. seriveis is known to have a positive effect on growth and proliferation of P. roquefort (Hansen and Jakobsen; Hansen et al., 2001).

### 4.6.2 Yeast in Blue Cheese

Blue cheese are semi-soft cheeses produced by growth of M. roquefort. Blue cheeses have a significantly higher content of NaCl compared to ripened and white cheeses. The NaCl concentration in blue cheese after ripening is 3.2% (w/w) in the core and 7% (w/w) in the surface. After eight weeks the NaCl reaches 2.0% (w/w) and 4.0% (w/w) in the layer. The pH at 24 hours is the pH 4.6–4.7. During ripening, the pH increases to about 6.5 and 5.9 in the surface (Gobert et al. 1997; Godino and Hef 1992; Hansen 2001). To permit air enter the cheese center and carbon dioxide to escape the cheeses are pierced before maturation which also affect yeast. In blue cheese, yeast are produced in high quantities without quality negative (Fleet 1992; Hansen et al., 2001; van Tempel 2000). The number of yeasts detected in blue cheese is in the order of 10⁶–10⁷/g in the surface and, higher counts were best observed (Hansen et al., 2001; van Tempel and Jacobsen 1998). The most common yeast like cheese yeasts are D. hansenii, F. catenata, C. formatus, L. lipolytica, K. kraemer, T. kefir, and L. lactis (Besay 2002). In Myquella, which is produced by maturation, the predominant yeasts isolated from both the core and the surface is D. hansenii, but in the beginning of the maturation other yeast like Zygosaccharomyces spp., G. geotrichum Y. lipolytica, and C. rugosa are seen, in low.
