### 5.3 Population Biology

Gene flow is the movement of individuals among populations and can be estimated through isozyme analysis as the number of migrants per generation (Slatton and Burden 1989) or by "private" alleles—those limited to a single species. Different species, especially with different allele frequencies compared to individuals from a single panmictic population, can perform this type of analysis. Differences in genetic variation among populations can help identify the center of any species. diverse populations should occur at or near origin, derived populations will usually contain a subset of the total diversity. This idea has been confirmed with isozyme analysis to indicate that Agarius bisporus has high diversity throughout America (Brown 1989) and that Phytophthora infestans, P. palindrova, and M. megaby occurred in central Mexico (Tooley et al. 1985), central Asia (Mechia and 1992), and central Africa (Niás et al. 1999), respectively. Very few species, except P. sp., Phytophthora cinnamamoora, and Phytophthora aleopeci had only limited introductions into Australia (Burden 1982; et al. 1984; Riley et al. 1998), although the original source for the populations could not be identified for certain.

Applications of isozyme analysis in epidemiology have included identifying specific species of Phytoph infestans (Goodwin in 1995; Legacy and Fry 1996), determining the various forces of evolutionary change in the inoculated populations of the barley fungus Rhynochosporum secal (Goodwin et al. 1998) and estimating the genetic diversity between individuals of the lycoporter perforatum on decaying logs (Hass 1993).

The first attempt to use isozymes to analyze the genetic locus of populations for non-reproductive meiosis was by Speith (1975). That analysis revealed a low level of genetic variation but low differentiation among populations. Species' pioneering work was rewarded by additional studies within but low differentiation and has continued. Many of these studies have similar results and demonstrated high genetic diversity within but low differentiation among populations of a wide diversity (Andrews et al. 2008; Goodwin et al. 1993; Hass 1996; Webber et al. 1998). Other authors have found varying degrees of subdivision among host-associated foci (Harvey et al. 2001; Lehmann and Chiang 1989) and more, many showing high similarity between loci.

# 6 CONCLUSIONS

Isozyme analysis is an extremely powerful and versatile technique that can answer many questions about fungal genetics, taxonomy, and population biology. Compared to alternative techniques, it is cheap, fast and easy to interpret. They assay expressed genes, and by analysis of dimeric enzymes, can distinguish individuals from fungal mixtures of cultures—an advantage that is not shared with molecular markers. However, electrophoresis systems are suitable for small size samples and can be portable to remote locations. The disadvantage of isozyme analysis is a low level of polymorphism, but this can be overcome by increasing the number of enzyme assays. Such isozyme analysis should ensure its place in the molecular toolbox for many years.
