Sterols from maize significantly increased aflatoxin production by A. flavus strains at 0.5 and 1.0 mg/kg flavonoids. Anthocyanins and resveratrol which occur naturally in maize kernels, inhibited aflatoxin production by more than 50% at 0.76 mg/kg (Norton 1999). Highly glycosylated forms of the anthocyanins to be less effective in inhibiting aflatoxin production (Norton 1999). Carotenoids with an alpha-type ring were more effective in inhibiting aflatoxin production by A. flavus, with some having at 0.6 µM (Norton 1997). Most strains of A. flavus exposed to beta-carotene at 50 µg/ml aflatoxin production was inhibited by 90%, or some percent strains derived with less than 5% (Norton 1998). Improved plant genotype could enhance the ability of plant kernels to inhibit chemical species and inhibit growth of F. graminearum (Dowd et al. 1997). A. flavus was considerably more resistant to quinones produced by Fusarium species and F. verticillioides (moldiforme) (Dowd et al. 1997). ## 2.3 Plant Breeding Strategies for Enhancing Resistance to Mycogenic Fungi

Several resistant hybrids have been identified in the Third Illinois trials (Campbell and White 1995, 1995) and have been incorporated into an aflatoxin-resistant breeding program whose objective was to improve Midwestern corn lines such as B 73 and M 17. In addition, the increase of resistance in kernels observed in M17 was noted (Miller and White 2000; White 2001; White et al. 1995b), and in the case of high resistance inherited kernels, genetic dominance was identified. Overall, results indicated that selection for resistance to Aspergillus ear and aflatoxin should be effective, and development of resistant hybrids for use in commercial hybrids should be successful (White et al. 1995a).

Chromosomes regions associated with A. flavus and aflatoxin production in corn have been identified through Restriction Length Polymorphism (RFLP) analysis in four "resistant" lines (R001, LB31), and in the Illinois breeding program, after mapping populations were developed using B73 and M17 maize identified as the susceptible "parents" (White et al. 1995b). In some cases, hybrids were associated with resistance to Aspergillus ear and not aflatoxin, and vice versa, whereas others were found to be associated with both.
