## 4.2 Micrograde

Micrograde is a silk milk that has been produced by Propionibacterium shermani and then pasteurized (Hover 2000). The product protects the shelf life of cheese by inhibiting spoilage bacteria (Lym et al. 1993). The product is an antagonist towards yeast and molds. Mold resists of protons acid, diacetyl, acetic acid, and lactic acid (Al-Zorky 1991).

## 4.3 Reuter

Reuter is a low molecular weight nonproteinaceous, highly soluble, neutral metabolite produced by Lactobacillus reuter. The compound is a broad-spectrum antimicrobial with activity that encompasses yeast and molds as well as bacteria. It may apply in preservation of food by reducing populations of pathogens, and spoilage microorganisms (Dancer and Poewer 1992).

## 6 ESSENTIAL OILS

The antimicrobial activity of extracts from several types of plant and plant parts has been recognized as flavoring in foods for many years. Some of these essential oils have antifungal properties. Conner and Beaut (1994) investigated the effects of garlic against yeast and other investigators have shown these extracts to inhibit molds. Alderman and March (1976) showed the effect of lemon and orange oils on Aspergillus flavus and growth when the olives were added to growth at concentrations of 3000–3500ppm, growth and aflatoxin production was suppressed. When orange oil was added to either medium at concentrations of 7000ppm, growth and aflatoxin production was greatly reduced although still evidence reported that the essential oils of anise, coriander, Roman cinnamon, basil, and oregano were inhibited by food and industrial spices (Cao Elaygar et al. 2003).

## 7 PHENOLIC ACIDS

Phenolic acids have been shown by several researchers to possess antifungal activity. Chang and Bran (1975) demonstrated in a glass medium, 1000ppm butylhydroxanisole (BHA) inhibited growth and aflatoxin production of Aspergillus parasitus, and >250ppm inhibited growth and aflatoxin production of A. flavus. They found that at high BHA, total aflatoxin production was more than twice that of the control, with no effect on mycelial growth. Results indicate that at high levels, BHA may serve as an antifungal agent, however, at low levels BHA may stimulate aflatoxin production. The BHA has been documented as inhibiting A. flavus, parasitic, Penicillium, Geotrichum, Bysochlamys species, and S. cerevisiae (Davidson and Natch 2000).

Since the primary use of these compounds in foods is as antioxidants, their effectiveness as antifungal agents in foods has not been adequately studied. While experiments in growth media indicate that these compounds exert antifungal effects, extrapolation of these to foods should be done with caution. Interaction of these compounds with food components would undoubtedly affect their antifungal properties.

Ozone O3 is a strong antimicrobial agent in the food industry. It has been used in many countries and recently given GRAS status in the United States. ## 8 GASES AND MODIFIED ATMOSPHERES

Elimination of oxygen can be used as a control for inhibiting the growth of molds. No growth of yeast occurs. Studies on bakery products have demonstrated that O2 levels must be reduced to 0.1-1.1% to inhibit mold growth. As tested by Csted (1979), mold would occur in 3 days in air; 5 days in 99% N2–1% O2; and >100 days in 99% N2–0.5% O2–1% CO2–0.22% O2–100% CO2. This demonstrates that although molds are considered aerobic organisms, certain species have the ability to grow at low levels of O2. Effectively controlling mold by gas flushing columns can be effective. Carbon dioxide may be used to displace or to supplement gas flushing (Farber 1991). Oxygen scavengers will also provide protection against leaks and infiltrations of O2 through the package.

Carbon dioxide exerts antifungal action that simple exclusion of O2, and thus is more effective than nitrogen alone. The gas probably exerts antifungal activity by altering pH levels (Gorris 1994).

Recent research has shown that CO2 can inhibit the growth of mold. Carbon monoxide also inhibits yeasts that cause postharvest decay in fruits and vegetables (Wagner and Moberg 1989). The potential toxicity of this compound to workers requires special handling procedures.

Sulfur dioxide is effective against yeasts and molds. It is used extensively to control growth of undesirable microorganisms in fruits, wine, drinks, seafood, shrimp, and pickles. The antimicrobial activity of SO2 is associated with the ionized form of molecule. Therefore it is most effective at pH <4, where this form predominates (Weidichera 2000).

Ethylene oxide has been used to reduce contamination and to kill insects in various fruits. The gas has been used to treat grains, dried fruits, corn, wheat, seeds, dried egg, and gelatin (ICMS 1980). Concern over toxicity of residues limits the use of this gas in recent years.

## 9 INTERACTION OF FACTORS

Many of the antifungal agents discussed in this chapter need to be used at extreme concentrations or in foods to be effective when alone. However, a variety of factors can prevent growth of fungi. When fungi are more tolerant to adverse environmental conditions (such as inhibitory factors as temperature, water activity, or pH) with antifungal agents can result in improvement of the microbial stability of foods. Combinations of growth-limiting factors at subinhibitory levels can be used so that microorganisms can no longer proliferate.

Sorbic acid at 100ppm and pH 7.0 will not inhibit mold growth. However, if the pH is lowered to 5.0, growth will be inhibited (Lee and March 1985). Addition of BHA and BHT has been shown to potentiate the action of sorbic acid (Scott 1989). In particular, antifungal additives become more effective as environmental conditions allow for the optimum.

The level of a single growth-limiting factor that inhibits a microorganism is determined under which all other factors operate. In preserving foods where a factor is usually relied upon to control growth, addition of another factor in itself does not give full inhibition, yet produces products in the presence of other inhibitory factors. The effect of supersimiting factors is known as the “hurdle concept” (Leist 1999).

This technology is currently available on combining inhibitory factors to preserve food. It is very time consuming and expensive to develop preservative systems using the hurdles concept by random design.
