## 2.2 Amplification-Based Methods

Sensitivity can be greatly improved through the use of different in vitro methods such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR), ligation chain reaction or self-sustained sequence nucleic acid amplification (NASBA). With exception of PCR and the above-mentioned in vitro amplification methods have only limited practical relevance in food monitoring (e.g., Stokes et al. 1994).

### 2.2.1 Polymerase Chain Reaction

Since discovery, a considerable number of PCR-based assays have been developed, they have been applied to clinical and environmental samples and many more for the detection of food-borne microorganisms. The PCR technique allows rapid and selective identification and/or detection of microorganisms in different matrices by amplifying specific gene sequences. The reaction cycle consists of (a) denaturation of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), (b) annealing of short DNA fragments (primers) to single strands, and (c) extension of the primer strand with a thermostable DNA polymerase. Following the completion of one cycle, the denaturation step is repeated, the original target region is amplified, but also the amplification product of the first cycle, leading to an exponential increase of the number of copies to the target DNA. The detection of amplification products is possible through electrophoresis, fluorescence or staining. To increase the sensitivity and to confirm the identity of the amplification product, Southern blotting and hybridization with a specific probe can be carried out (Hendolin et al. 2000; Löffler et al. 2000; Sandhu et al. 1995). The NASBA is an isothermal nucleic acid amplification method that specifically amplifies RNA sequences using T7 RNA polymerase (Compton 1991). ### 2.2.3 Quantification of Results

Besides quantitative detection of spoilage microbes, it is also desirable to know the abundance in water and foods. Quantification of the PCR results can be carried out through different methods including densimetric measurement of PCR products, by HPLC with a UV detector (Kata, 1996), quantitative PCR (qPCR), or by real (Cross, 1995). Two of them, qPCR and real-time PCR are the most promising techniques for quantifying fungi in foods and juices.

The QC using internal DNA standard provides the means for determining relative amount of target DNA. The PCR process amplifies DNA together with target DNA. The competitor DNA is one of sequence (typically identical to the target DNA with added deletions or insertions) as the target DNA (Bak and Keneley 1998; Haulang et al.

During conventional PCR, an endpoint analysis is carried out by examining the fluorescence of ethidium bromide strands generated by gel electrophoresis. With real-time PCR, the continuous analysis of amplification-associated fluorescence during the whole PCR reaction gives a graphic display of the time course of amplification of the PCR product interest. Both direct and indirect methods are used for generating the fluorescence monitored during the PCR cycle (Walker 2001 and others there; Figure 1). Indirect assays employ a system that yields fluorescence generated during the process of extension amplification. This is exemplified in the Taqman system. Taq probes utilize the intrinsic 5' nuclease activity of Taq polymerase to digest a probe that has annealed to the site of interest. The method of generation of reporter fluorophores is proved by a specific DNA sequence, Taq polymerase-associated 5' digestion of the probe results in degradation of the probe and release of the fluorophore. Direct methods refer to those systems in which fluorescence is a direct result of binding of a fluorescent molecule to the amplified product. A simple direct method involves the use of fluorescent dye such as SYBR Green. These dyes possess selective affinity for dsDNA. Binding of the dye enhances fluorescence at 530 nm proportionally to dsDNA. Specific hybrids can be detected when bound to the gene of interest. All these probes are based on fluorescent energy transfer (FRET; Walker 2001). Molecular beacons are essentially hairpin probes with a variation on fluorescence interference (Figure 1). A variation on the hairpin probe concept has been employed to develop unimolecular probe systems such as the Sun or Scorpion primers, which are incorporated into the amplified sequence during the PCR. A variety of PNA-probes have also been developed for real-time PCR applications (Strender et al. 2002). The LightCycler hybridization system consists of a labeled and acceptor probe. Fluorescence is generated when both are annealed to the probe (Figure 1). Level of fluorescence is proportional to the amount of DNA generated during the PCR reaction (Löffler et al. 2000). The PCR reaction can be carried out in small volumes in a glass capillary that separates the products. The LightCycler system combines rapid in vitro amplification in glass capillaries with real-time determination and quantification of DNA, enabling 38 samples to be completed in 45 min.

### 2.2.4 Limits of PCR

One of the drawbacks of PCR-based detection methods, is that besides living cells, dead cells with relatively inactive DNA are also detected to false positive results. This can be circumvented by including a preparation step prior to PCR analysis, or by using RNA as template transcription coupled with PCR, or NASBA reactions (Vaitiling et al. 1998; Widjoatmodjo et al. 1999).
