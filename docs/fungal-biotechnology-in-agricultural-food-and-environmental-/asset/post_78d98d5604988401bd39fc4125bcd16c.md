## 5.3 Saprophic of Attached and Fallen Litter

It has been estimated that the maintenance of the mycorrhizal association costs between 9 and 28% of the total photosynthetic production annually (Finley and 1992; Leake and 2001; Vogt et al. 1992). The benefits of such a costly relationship to the phototroph are not limited to nutrient scavenging and mobilization, and to other resources which may extend to the ability of the mycorrhiza to construct extensive and persistent mycelial networks. This allows for retention of a mineral budget in a fluctuating and discontinuous environment, where the mycorrhizal roots undergo decomposition and formation and leaching of imported nutrients to the rhizosphere. Leake and Reid (2017) discuss the ecological significance of degradative activities of mycorrhizal and noncommonizal fungi.

### 5.3.1 Litter-Decomposition at the Substrate Community

Estimates for litter production in tropical woodlands ranges from 3-ton/ha/year (Quercus species) to 5.7ton/ha/year for Norway spruce (Picea abies), although tropical litter may be less. This forms the bulk of litter material, representing decomposition involves the activities of fungi, bacteria, and animals, to produce humus, which becomes incorporated into the mineral soil. Decomposition may take months (e.g., for Formus and Extrus and Pinus sylvestrisytica), and may accumulate to form layers if decomposition is high and decay rates are low. Fungal successions on leaves may decay, senescence, and fall; and are well characterized and described in detail by elsewhere (Aubuchard and Frankland 1994; Webster 1995; Kinkel; Pongo 1991).
