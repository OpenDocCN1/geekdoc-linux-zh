# # 1 INTRODUCTION

Alcoholic beverages containing higher concentrations of alcohol than can be obtained by wine fermentation have be prepared by distillation. After the distillation process all the ingredients of the original fermented mash or liquid, except volatiles, remain in the waste, called stillage, spent wash, or vinho. The composition of this distillery waste depends on the original raw material (sugar or beet molasses, fruits, etc. wheat, barley, cassava, potato, rice). These materials have to be prepared for alcoholic fermentation by distillation or milling, including hydrolysis and addition of nutrients. Stillages remaining after distillation are 8–14 times the volume of the alcohol produced and have a high content of organic substances (Shooji et al. 1999). The pollution load is measured by biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) and chemical oxygen demand (COD). Distillery stillages vary. With different types of distillery water such as fruits, the separation of liquids is impossible (Wilkie et al. 1986). There are several sub-processes to prevent further making wastes acceptable. The presence of a large amount of organic substances of natural origin opens the possibility of bioconversion by microorganisms. While bacteria participate in anaerobic processes producing biogas, fungi are suitable for aerobic bioconversion. There have been several attempts to use fungi for treating distillery wastages. The specified treatment is to purify the consumption of organic substances, thus reducing its COD and BOD, and at the same time to obtain some valuable product, such as biomass for protein-rich animal feed, or specific fungal metabolites.
