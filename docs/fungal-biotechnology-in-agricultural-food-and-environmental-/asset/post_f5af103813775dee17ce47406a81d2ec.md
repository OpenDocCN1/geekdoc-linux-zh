Since, the unassociated portion of the acid molecule is primarily responsible for antifungal activity, effectiveness depend on the dissociation constant of the acid and the food to be preserved. If the dissociation constant is between p3 and 5, organic acids are generally more effective at low pH. With solubility determines the functions in which organic acids may be used.

A few species possess mechanisms of resistance to organic acids. Saccharomyces resist to high concentrations of sorbic and benzoic acids (1977). Molds in the genus Penicillium can grow in the presence of high concentrations of sorbic acid and benzoic acid with an extremely strong kerosene-like odor (Liwen and March 1984; Tsi et al. 1988). When resistance to metabolism of an organic acid is a problem, other preservative systems must be used.

## 2.3 Propionic Acid

This organic acid inhibits molds but not yeasts. It occurs in some foods as a natural result of processing. It is present in Swiss cheese up to 1%, where it is produced by the bacterium Propionibacterium frechenii (Besauch 2000). Since yeast are generally unaffected, the acid can be added to bread without interfering with leveraging (Rann 1999).

In the industry, propionic acid is used as a sodium or calcium salt (Ray 2001). Propionates are used for antimicrobial properties, they will inhibit Bacillus mesentericus, the rot causing bacterium. Propionates are also used in a limited extent to inhibit mold growth in processed cheese.

The antifungal activity of propionic acid is weak compared to the other organic acids. Therefore, it must be used in relatively high concentrations. To be effective, the pH value of the food to be preserved should be low. Because of its dissociation constant, propionic acid is active in a pH range similar to that of ascorbic acid (Eckland 1990).

## 3 ANTIBIOTICS

## 3.1 Natamycin

Natamycin, formerly called pamicin is an antibiotic that possesses antifungal properties, yet is not active against bacteria. It is currently used in various countries. Researchers have demonstrated that natamycin is active at low concentrations against many of the fungi food supply. Levels of 10 ppm have been reported to be effective in Swiss cheese while growth of Penicillium while 1000–2000 ppm are effective as dips for cheese (Jay 1982; Pugahenti et 1999). Natamycin inhibits aflatoxin formation by molds when growth was inhibited (Ray and Bullerman 1982).

## 3.2 Nisin

Nisin is active against gram-positive bacteria but has not been reported to be effective against yeasts and molds (Luchansky 1999).

## 4 METABOLITES FROM LACTIC ACID BACTERIA

A wide variety of foods are preserved by lactic acid fermentation, milk, fruits, and vegetables. Reduction of pH and large amount of carbohydrates are the primary reactions that act by lactic acid bacteria (LAB) to provide a preservative effect for food. These are largely ineffective in preventing the growth of fungi. However, it is also recognized that LAB can produce inhibitory substances other than organic acids (Baisch et al 1997). The antifungal properties of LAB are well documented. Nisin, a type of genera Lactococcus and Lactobacillus produces antifungal substances. Nisin has received little attention, however, several metabolites of LAB have been reported to be antifungal.

Baisch et al (1998) screened dairy cultures for their antifungal activity with the goal of exploring their antifungal properties. They found several strains of Streptococcus that inhibited a wide variety of molds. While the antifungal substances produced by the LAB were not identified, maximum protein production occurred at 30°C and pH 6.8. Several specific LAB metabolites have been reported to be antifungal agents.

## 2.4 Medium-Chain Fatty Acids

Generally, fatty acids are most effective as inhibitors of positive bacteria and yeasts, although some fatty acids exhibit antimicrobial activity. Clapley et al. (1981) observed that fatty acid reduces growth and aflatoxin production by Aspergillus spp. Polyhydric fatty acids have great potential for use as preservatives in food markets (Razani-Rahmani and Griffiths 1994). They also possess antifungal properties, and may exert a preservative effect in foods. Kato and Shibibataka (1975) demonstrated strong antifungal activity of glycerol monoester and glycerol towards Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Sucrose monoester and manolate were found to be slightly inhibitory to a spoilage-forming yeast incorporated into a soy sauce substrate (Kato 1981).
