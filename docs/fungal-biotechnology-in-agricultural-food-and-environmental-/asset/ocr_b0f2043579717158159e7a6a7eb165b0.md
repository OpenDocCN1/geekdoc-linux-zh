# TYPES OF MYCORRHIZA

The term mycorrhiza describes the symbiotic relation between diverse assemblages of fungi and plant roots. Mycorrhizal associations are considered mutualistic; they are both the normal state (Smith and Read 1997) and a constant feature (1995) of most plants under ecological conditions. There are several types of mycorrhiza characterized by the anatomy of the fungus involved called arbuscular mycorrhiza (AM). The AM fungi (Glomes, Glomyomycetes) occur in most terrestrial environments (Allen et al. 1995) and in association in grassland, shrub, and agricultural ecosystems. They are characterized by branched hyphae penetrating root cortical cells to transfer immobile nutrients such as phosphorus (P) to plant. Arbuscular fungi fungi are obligate biotrophs, obtaining all of their carbon from the host plant, without forming a mantle of fungal tissue.

The second most common mycorrhizal type is ectomycorrhiza (EM). The majority are basidiomycetes, with species forming a Hartig net (Smith and Read 1997). They fuse almost exclusively with woody trees found in plants of the family Fagaceae and Ericaceae (Allen et al. 1995). Ectomycorrhizal fungi do not penetrate the cell wall but form between individual roots with a mantle of fungal tissue. These are extensively used in forest reclamation, where the most common being Pinosinus taarius (Marx and Cox 1998), Marx et al. and Sullivan (Chen 2001) are some less common mycorrhizal types that form species-associations with ericaceous plants and deciduous plants which should also be included in their restoration efforts.

# 4 MYCORRHIZA FUNCTION

Despite the differences in associations among mycorrhizal fungi, the function of mycorrhizal systems depends on the ability of fungi to absorb inorganic or organic nutrients from the soil and transport them to the host roots (Smith and Read 1997). Hyphae extend the root network several meters from the root source (Fries and Allen 1991), to obtain resources well beyond depletion zones (Yates and 1977) and to facilitate for distributed nutrients (Cul and Caldwell 1996). Fine, branched hyphae explore the soil surface (Hart and Smith 1983; Rousseau et al. 1994), produce enzymes for nutrients that are normally unavailable to plants (Adcock and Read 1986; Bauer et al. 2000), and because of their extreme diversity experience less competition as a result, mycorrhizal fungi undergo physiological changes which included increased rate of growth and seed production, increased nutrient status, enhanced water uptake and tolerance, and improved resistance to plant pathogens.
