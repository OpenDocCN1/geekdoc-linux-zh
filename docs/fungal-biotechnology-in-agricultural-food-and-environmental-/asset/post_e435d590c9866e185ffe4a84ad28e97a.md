The advantage of GC is its high performance in comparison to HPLC-MS and its relative ease of operation. Furthermore, GC is easily interfaced to MS (GC-MS) or other detectors, providing a powerful tool that can deliver high separation power and give structural information in one run.

The most important aspect in GC is the injection, which can poorly have a severe effect on the spectrum of the spectrometer. The most commonly used techniques for liquids are split, on-column injection of trapped volatiles, and headspace (GC 1993). Currently used high-resolution GC for liquid-gas chromatography is what is needed to achieve separation of mixtures of various molecules and sesquiterpenes.

Stationary phases as well are chosen based on the polarity and volatility of the compounds to be separated. Volatile compounds may require a thicker column, whereas high separation is better obtained by thin column (Colmers 1995).

The spectrometer can be used to: (a) provide structural information from fragmentation in electron ionization (EI) easily searched in databases, (b) accurate mass using the modern time-of-flight (TOF) instrument, or (c) ion-traps (or multiple MS–MS instruments) as highly selective detectors (to get very detailed fragmentation).

Both TOF and MS–MS instrumentation increases the capabilities of the instrument, and the metabolite profiles that are obtained from these instruments are generally not needed for identification and are desired further.

The most promising important detection method is MS in many cases will give a molecular ion (and thermally mass) and a characteristic pattern–mass spectrum—from chosen columns. The identification of unknowns can be an insufficient information content of the mass spectrum and stereoisitional isomers in aromatic compounds (Ramasawki 1986). A great potential for MS is to select for a number of characteristic ions–selected ion monitoring (SIR or SIM) which can improve detection sensitivity up to the pg levels.

Flame ionization detection (FID) is an important and robust method, with sample high sensitivity and over a large dynamic range, but without selectivity. If metabolite profiles are compared on two different columns of substantially different polarity, similarity in the retention times of metabolites on these two columns can be used for their indirect identification (Davies 1990).

Fourier transform infrared spectrometry (FTIR), can be used to detect compounds originating from GC columns. Most functional groups, in particular the carbonyl group (C=O), have a strong absorption (fingerprint) from vibration energy. FTIR will give an unambiguous identification of compounds and it is possible to identify them by comparing spectra to known authentic. The method is nondestructive and can be used in combination with FID or MS. FTIR will also provide information between structural- and isomers. The major drawback of GC-FTIR is a lower sensitivity compared to GC-FID and GC-MS.

## 3.4 Atmospheric Pressure Ionization MS

The last decade has seen a tremendous development in biological MS, and is currently one of the fastest growing analytical techniques in biotechnology.

The MS is used to determine the mass ratio of charged molecules or highly specific fragments of these (as in GC-MS). They can be either positively or negatively charged. In the atmospheric pressure ionization (API), ions are generated in the atmosphere and transferred to the mass analyzer.

There are two dominant ionization techniques: Electrospray ionization (ESI); and atmospheric pressure ionization (API). In ESI the eluent is sprayed through a capillary where a high voltage is applied (around 3 kV). This will produce a spray of highly charged droplets. The solvent is evaporated from the droplets by a heat, leading to shrinking and disintegration into charged species through a complex process. The ions are formed either in the solvent or during spray shrinkage and key factors influencing the ion production: solvent composition (surface tension, volatility, modifiers, pH, ion strength), source parameters (temperature, gas flow, potential), and interaction between analysis in the atmosphere. The charged species are sampled into the vacuum of the mass analyzer.

In API the eluent is sprayed through a co-axial capillary with a heated gas to evaporate the solvent. Solvated molecules are ionized by a corona discharge needle that is usually placed near the sampling analyte. Analyte molecules are ionized by chemical reaction in the gas phase at atmospheric pressure through a process like the classic chemical ionization.
