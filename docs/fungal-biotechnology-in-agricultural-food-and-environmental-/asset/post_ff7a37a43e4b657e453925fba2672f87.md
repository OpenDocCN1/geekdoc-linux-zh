## 1 INTRODUCTION

Mycotoxins are fungal metabolites that can contaminate foods and feeds, and exhibit toxic effects in higher organisms that consume the commodities (Sharma 1991). The regulatory guidelines and advisory limits issued by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) on some contamination can facilitate severe economics to U.S. agriculture domestically and worldwide. Mycotoxins that signify include toxins produced by *Aspergillus striatum*, deoxynivalenol or DON produced by *Fusarium ochraceum* and toxins produced by *Fusarium verticillioides* (synonym *F. flavus*) used in literature the first time (Brown et al. 1988) also be included on this list of significant mycotoxins. Aflatoxin, potent liver toxins, because of their ability to cause cancer in humans [reviewed in Dyer et al. 1987, Payne and Groopman 1994]. However, mycotoxins such as DON are of concern for the brewing industry which has cutoff levels as low as 0.5 ppm for DON in barley (Robens 2001). In addition, recognizing the potential for mycotoxins to cause animal or human health problems (Marasas 1996), the FDA has now announced the availability of a final guidance document entitled “Guidance for Industry: Fumonisin Levels in Human Foods and Animal Feeds,” Federal Registry November 2001. More than 50 countries have established or proposed regulations for controlling aflatoxins in foods and feeds, and at least 15 countries have regulations for levels of other mycotoxins for sale of feed (Haumann 1995). The FDA has set limits for aflatoxins 0.05 ppb, and because of both food safety concerns, it is estimated that over $1.5 billion in crop losses occur annually due to contamination of corn, cottonseed, and wheat with aflatoxins (Robens 2001). Mycotoxins and inadequate post harvest strategies have long been recognized. However, studies have revealed that such contaminated with mycotoxins at the relatively stage [reviewed in Lee and Libbaugh 1991, reviewed in Brown et al. 1998]. Therefore, management strategies focus on reducing levels of mycotoxins for example, as corn treatment with the gene encoding *Bacillus thuringiensis* crystal protein (BT maize), reduced levels of fumonisins (Dowd 2000). Integration of peanut essentially prevents aflatoxin contamination by preventing drought stress (Cole et al. 1998). However, optimization of management practices to control mycotoxins is not always possible due to production costs, geographic location, or the nature of the production system for corn vulnerable to mycotoxins. In addition, the best management practices are sometimes negated by biotic and abiotic factors that are hard to control and by environmental conditions. The complex biology of aflatoxin contamination on corn (Wicklow 1991) can drastically affect the outcome of measures. Therefore, there is an urgent need for development and utilization of state-of-the-art technologies to prevent mycotoxin contamination. Current trends are insufficiently published and high-impact research involving molecular based technologies has been accomplished that enhances a plant resistance strategy for controlling mycotoxin contamination and enhance resistance against other groups of mycotogenic fungi.

## 2 MYCOTOXIN PREVENTION ENHANCEMENT OF HOST RESISTANCE

Prehost resistance is a widely explored strategy for combating fungal attack. By far, many studies aimed at the incorporation of resistance against mycotogenic fungi have been applied toward resistance against aflatoxin contamination in corn (Brown et al. 1998). With corn, the strategy of enhancing resistance to aflatoxin contamination through breeding has become prominent because of the identification of resistance genes to aflatoxin contamination (Brown et al. 1998b). The significant advances in the identification of natural resistance mechanisms and traits (Brown et al. 1998; 1999; Chen et al. 2001). These investigations indicate that aflatoxin contamination involves multiple chromosomes in maize (D'Araujo and Davis 1999). Attempts to select for resistance traits in the development of commercial varieties, while maintaining agronomic characteristics, have been slowed due to a failure to identify expressed genes and traits involved in resistance. This is especially needed for genetic backgrounds. Therefore, research is needed to elucidate the biochemical mechanisms that confer resistance in corn and other types vulnerable to aflatoxin contamination. Gaining an understanding of the natural resistance will provide a valuable 'lesson' about the specific requirements for seed-based resistance against fungal attack. This information will help efforts to incorporate and enhance resistance in other classes such as cottonseed, peanut, and nuts.

### 2.1 Development of Aflatoxin-Resistance Screening Tools

Several screenings have been developed and facilitate corn breeding for developing resistance to fungal or aflatoxin contamination (King and Scott 1982). Infection methods employed with corn include the pin inoculation technique (inoculating kernels through the silk zone) and infiltrating ears with insect larvae infected with *A. flavus* (King and Scott 1992; Tucker et al. 1988). Two resistant members (Mp420 and Smith 1931; Zuno 1980) were discovered and used in different locations using earbar technique and through means of selective resistance. A rapid kernel screening assay (KSA) was developed and used to study aflatoxin production in kernels (Brown et al. 1995). This study evaluated the resistance of two varieties at the pericarp and at the superbase. The superbase level of resistance was shown to ensure a viable embryo (Brown et al. 1993). This KSA further demonstrated a method for identifying resistance in kernels (Chen et al. 2006) and highlighted qualitative and quantitative differences between resistant and susceptible genotypes (Gembeth 2001; Russin et al. 1997). This research was based on the prior identification, field studies, of a resistance breeding population, MA-gk (Wicklow et al. 1987). The KSA also discovered sources of resistance among three tested hybrids in field trials (Brown et al. 1995; Campbell and White 1996). This demonstrated that the KSA can be used at an initial stage to confirm the field traits of field resistance to aflatoxin contamination. The KSA was used as a preliminary screening for resistance to aflatoxin. This method has advantages over field screening techniques (Brown et al. 1999), mainly because of the speed of the assay. Field trials are irreplaceable for confirmation of resistance. Resistance was improved by implementing a method to quantify fungal biomass using the β-glucosidase (GUS) or green fluorescent protein (GFP) (Du et al. 1999; Wind and Williams 1998; Windhem et al. 1999) resistance gene A. flavus stress traits. A stress tester genes expressed a gene construct using the GUS reporter gene linked to A β-tubulin gene promoter for monitoring fungal growth (Brown et al. 1995; 1997) or the reporter gene linked to aflatoxin biosynthetic pathway gene which could provide a quick and economical way to measure aflatoxin levels.
