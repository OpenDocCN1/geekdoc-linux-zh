The establishment of mycorrhizal associations depends largely on two factors: the characteristics of the host plant and the spatial pattern of planting. Plants exhibit different degrees of may, primarily based on their capacity for nutrient uptake and growth response to colonization. In early work by Stahl (1900), plants divided into endcorrhizal, facultatively mycorrhizal, and mycorrhizal categories showed varying responses to the most mycorrhizal fungi (Allen and Allen 1990).

Studies conducted along sequences have demonstrated that early-plant species are often mycorrhizal, followed by facultative and late-successional (James 1986) species in the Chenopodiaceae, Brassicaceae, and Amaranthaceae (Greenham 1968) are nonmyizal and persist on disturbed soils where mycorrhizal densities are low (Allen and Allen 1998). Forest restoration is possible to establish late plant species after early-successional species.

This is because mycorrhizal are adapted to local plant populations (Weinbaum et al. 1996), using locally adapted seeds for the vegetation of a particular species is more effective. Promoting mycorrhizal colonization by introducing mycorrhizal seedlings onto disturbed sites can increase AM fertility if fungal hyphae expand into open habitats and roots, spreading the association across adjacent plants.

Manipulating the spatial arrangement of plants to concentrate resources and create resource islands may provide greater benefits than intensive treatments over a large area (Allen 1988; Allen and MacMahon 1985). These resources can provide seeds and inoculum for surrounding areas. Replanting multiple shrubs in a clump enhances mycorrhizal recovery by trapping wind-carried propagules between plants than would be deposited individually spaced plants (Allen et al. 1997).

Despite the benefits of mycorrhizal associations, although has not been used on a commercial scale. For commercial development, large quantities of inoculum must be produced. Since AM are obligate biotroph, they must be produced and grown on living plants. This is problematic because of the high risk of plant pathogens and contaminants entering the culture system (Jasriel and Silva 1997). In contrast, EM fungi may grow on agar media in the base of a host root or the vegetative material of fruiting bodies collected directly from plants (Molina and Palmer 1982).

The first step in cultivating mycorrhizal fungi is producing the stock of individual fungal isolate on host plants. For AM fungi, spores or colonized roots from the host are used to produce larger quantities of inoculum for growth on suitable substrates (Fritz 1990). Although large amounts of EM fungi can be easily collected in the field, spores are rarely used to isolate EM fungi.
