# 1 INTRODUCTION

The identification of filamentous fungi has always been considered difficult and many misunderstandings and misidentifications can be found in the literature (Frisvad 1998; Mantle 1987). Phenotypic characters, morphology and growth form form the basis for fungal taxonomy (Donsvad 1996; Mantle 1987; Patel 1979; Raps 1977; Raphael 1978). Advancements in the developments of analytical methods have allowed the use of “secondary” metabolite profiling for fungal identification and been used within genera such as Aspergillus, Penicillium, Fusarium, and their perfect. The status of metabolite profiling in the classification of filamentous fungi relies on the fact that a major part of the fungal growth is caused by the production of diverse metabolites, most of which are extracellular. In general, agar for optimal secondary metabolites and mycotoxin production have been based on containing yeast extract. Yeast extract (YES) broth for filamentous fungi as a 'sensimytic' by Davis (1976) has shown to be very effective medium when used with yeast extract. However, many media occasionally present as certain medium of yeast extract, malt extract, potato extract, peptone, or tryptone, which may differ significantly in composition, adding manganese sulphate (Fishberg et al., 1990). It is important to note that metabolite profiles are strongly influenced by the analytical scheme, which can only be compared if they are produced by the same analytical protocol. Developments in chromatography and mass spectrometry (MS) have greatly increased their resolution, sensitivity and selectivity in analytical chemistry. There is a broad range of tools available to format a small amount of sample. As a result, a broad range of metabolites can be determined with high selectivity. Furthermore, some of the newer techniques have shown their potential general profiling working directly on raw samples. However, it is not wide enough to fully utilize chromatography in complex mixtures. Implementing an analytical approach the following conditions must be met: (a) A priori knowledge about metabolites produced by the genus, (b) whether the general chemical classes are alkaloids, acids, neutrals, volatiles, as large or small molecules, (c) sample media or growth composition and, (d) number of samples to handle, (e) biological variation, and if large or small chemical diversity is expected, (f) sensitivity of analytical instrumentation, and (g) cost.
