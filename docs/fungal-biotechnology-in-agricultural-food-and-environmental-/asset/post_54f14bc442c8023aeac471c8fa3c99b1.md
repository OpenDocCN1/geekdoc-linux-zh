## 3.2 Simultaneous Saccharification and Fermentation

### 3.2.1 Process Description

We give the description of a typical SSF process that aims to recycle waste solid into ethanol, which functions as an alternative fuel. The solid utilized in this process is approximately one-third waste and one-third pulp mill. This solid contains approximately 55-third cellulose. The waste is pre, sterilized, and then forwarded to 2500 gal. A culture of mutant *T. reesei* is inoculated in each reactor. The fungus continuously produces a full culture of cellulases that degrade cellulose. The total residence time for each cellulase production is 48h. The cellulose introduced into the reactors is degraded into sugars, fermented, and subsequently, the degraded cellulose is cooled in a heat exchanger and sent to reactors for fermentation. In this system, one can use four reactors simultaneously full swing. The temperature is maintained at 40°C. The initial feed to the reactors contains 8% of cellulose, and the remaining 92% is degraded to glucose.

In 1970, Gulf Oil Company developed a process for simultaneous saccharification and fermentation (Katon and Mathews 1995). Researchers at the University of Arkansas later implemented SSF process. Conceptually, we simply replace the hydrolysis step with enzyme and carry out the combination of hydrolysis and fermentation in the same reactor to convert biomass into ethanol. Most reports on the SSF process combine saccharification and fermentation into one reactor as an advantage; however, this claim is misleading. Because the researchers may ignore the fact that the enzyme has to be produced separately in economical fashion. In practice, there is an additional cellulase production step, which is nontrivial from operation and economic. In Srock's study (1989), the filamentous fungi *T. reesei* produced the cellulase preparations, and a thermolerant yeast strain *Kluyveromyces fragilis* carried the conversion of sugars to ethanol. ## 3.2. Mixed Cultures and Fermentation

One disadvantage of the SSF process is the number of steps because of separate steps needed for culture generation. In the original SSF process, cellulase, yeast, and other nutrients are all grown in one reactor. To reduce the number of steps and cost, properly mixed cultures and fermentation have been developed. In this process, more than one organism in the same reactor converts cellulose into sugars, other fermenters produce ethanol, acetic acid, and lactic acid. This process is more efficient because it eliminates the steps associated with the production of the enzyme from the product. For example, cellulase saccharifies cellulose into glucose while the same organism also hydrolyzes cellulose to xylose and xyulose. Furthermore, it ferments glucose and cellulose to ethanol, acetic acid, and lactic acid. The second organism, C. thermolytic, ferments cellulose, xylose, and xyobiose to ethanol, acetic acid and lactic acid. Mixed culture fermentation is performed under anaerobic conditions, at a temperature of 60°C and pH 7. In summary, C. thermolytic efficiently degrades cellulose and hemicellulose, and C. thermacterium transforms xylose to generate ethanol and useful byproducts. replaced by lower priced gasoline. With the oil crises in the 1970s and increasing environmental concerns, ethanol has gained some again as an alternative motor fuel (Linko 1987) or as an alternative to gasoline. Because ethanol is an oxygenate that reduces exhaust emissions, especially formally (Volford 2000). To remain successful in that role, the cost of production must be lower than gasoline.
