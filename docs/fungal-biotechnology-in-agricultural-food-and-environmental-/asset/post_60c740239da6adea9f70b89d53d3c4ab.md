## 3.1.2 Trichoderma Biological Control

Several studies have shown that T. harizarium can control diseases caused by many root-infecting, Fusarium, Rhizoctonia, and Pythium (Table 1). T. harizarium strain KRL-AG2, which was formulated as F-Stop, when added to a soil mix prior to seeding reduced the incidence and severity of Fusarium root rot in lycopersicum (Dattoff et al. 1995). T. harizarium strain T-22 also provided control of Fusarium crown rot of tomato (Nemec et al. 1996) and the fungi could be recovered from the roots of treated plants 26 days after application, significantly reducing the incidence of Fusarium rot caused by F. oxysporum f sp. lycopersicum, when added to medium seeding (Latin and Favel 1998). The application of T. harizarium strain T-22 was also found to inhibit this disease and was reduced when it was incorporated into potting mix at only 0.02% (Latin and Favel 1998). Strain T-22 of T. harizarium was generated by fusing a mutant strain of colonizing plant using protoplast techniques (Harmann 2000). This new strain had the ability to colonize the root system of plants, resulting in greater efficacy as a biological control for long-term protection of roots (Harmann 2000; Hijdjord and Bjørne 1991). Pathogenic structures, such as hyphae, being degraded by the production of cell wall enzymes as chitinases, glucanases, and proteases (Gerenia et al. 1993; Schirmbock et al. 1994; Thrane et al. 1997; Zeilinger et al. 2005). Trichoderma can compete with pathogens, namely by colonizing substrate and exclude from pathogens and induce healing of wounds (Hijdjord and Thomson 1998). Some strains of Trichoderma are good controls. It is also known that T. harizarium (strain T-22) has the ability to enhance growth and development in the ambience of plants (Harmann 2000) and this effect was due to the production of a growth-regulating factor by the fungus (Windham et al. 1986). Altamer et al. (1999) proposed that the ability of T. harizarium to increase growth was partly due to the versatility which Trichoderma possess in biological activity. It has also been reported that a strain of T. harizarium was able to trigger defense mechanisms in plants to enhance cell wall degradation and induction of defense, suggesting an indirect effect on the host plant by the biological control (Yedia et al. 1999).

### 3.1.3 Trichoderma Mechanism of Action

Trichoderma can act as biological control through several mechanisms, including antibiosis, parasitism, competition, and induction of host resistance (Hijdjord and Thomson 1998). Trichoderma is known to produce volatile and nonvolatile metabolites, which inhibit other microorganisms and are considered antifungal. A number of commercial formulations are available for strains of Trichoderma to control diseases on a wide range of crops. Among these products, RootShield™, T-22G, and T-Plant™, Trichoderma harizarium strain T-22 is also found to survive in the rhizosphere of plants (Flavel 2000).

### 3.1.4 Biotechnological Manipulations of Trichoderma

Techniques have been applied to control the role of hydrolytic enzymes, such as chitinases and glucanases, by Trichoderma that could lead to biological control activity. Transforms of T. longibraceum extra β-glucanidase copies of the gene were found to be better at suppressing Fusarium in cotton (Miguelheli et al. 1998). In addition, transforms of Trichoderma overexpressing the gene had due to a high-level increase in protein to protect cotton from R. solani (Flavel et al. 1996). Transforms of Trichoderma overexpressing the chitinase gene showed more effective in inhibiting the growth of the pathogen R. solani in vitro with wild-type strains (Limon et al. 1999). A mutant of T. harizarium that was selected for its ability to hydrolyze cellulose, had a 2-fold increase in β-1 and β-6 glucanase activity compared to the wild-type, produced three times more extracellular proteins and compounds, and showed greater inhibition of B in vitro (Rey et al. 2001). These studies examine the roles played by Trichoderma control of plants also highlights the successes in manipulating biological control strains to enhance efficacy. G. catenatum strain J1446 was able to colonize cucumber roots for 5 weeks after application, indicating that the fungus has the ability to survive and proliferate in the rhizosphere of plants. ## 3.3 Nonogenic Fungal Biocontrol of Seeding, Root, Crown, and Vascular Wilt

Glaucidium miller, Gilden and Fosters, a biocontrol agent of a wide range of crops, is classified within the genus Trichoderma due to morphological characteristics similar to members of this genus (Genn et al. 1991). One key characteristic of *T. harizarium* is the ability to produce various metabolites of Trichoderma shared with other strains (Papav 1995). *G. viridis* (T. virens) strain GL-3 was evaluated for reducing damping-off due to P. ultimum and R. solani (Flavel 2000).

Nonogenic fungi highly to plant species have been reported to provide biological control of a number of pathogens, including Fusarium, Rhizoctonia, and Pythium. Competition of nonogenic strains for plant nutrients, colonization of infection sites are mechanisms (Hijdjord and Thomson 1998). A commercial formulation of strain GL-3 of G. viridis is registered in the United States, and can control P. ultimum and R. solani on ornamental seedlings (Kooch 1999). Like *T. harizarium* strain T-22, strains of G. viridis have the ability to colonize the rhizosphere of plants (Harman 2000). The production of glucan occurs rapidly (within a few hours) and can persist for days provide high levels of pathogen suppression (Lundsen et al. 1992; Strategy 1996).
