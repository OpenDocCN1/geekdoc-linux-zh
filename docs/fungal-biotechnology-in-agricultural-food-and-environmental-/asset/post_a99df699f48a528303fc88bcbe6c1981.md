Several methods have been developed to reduce the number of artifacts produced by PCR amplification. Using two pairs of PCR primers, or nested primers (Pilakos et al. 1996), prevents this. A fragment is formed by the second pair of primers (nested) binding to a DNA sequence. If the first pair of primers binds correctly, then a slightly smaller PCR fragment is made. This method requires a knowledge of the sequence immediately adjacent to the first pair of primers in order to synthesize the "nested" pair of primers.

If the DNA host is not known, Amplification Length Polymorphism (APLP) will also reduce the number of PCR artifacts (Vargas et al. 1995). In this technique, the target DNA is digested with restriction enzymes to yield different fragments. Specific double-stranded oligonucleotide adapters are used as PCR primers to select the sequences with selective 3 nucleotides are utilized under high stringency PCR amplification and electrophoresis. This technique is efficient and amplification is not as complete as RAPD.

When the target DNA is known, other methods are available, such as PCR amplification of internal transcribed (ITS) of ribosomal DNA (rDNA). An example of the application of this PCR method is Beck and Logan (1995) which PCR detects Salmonella enterica triticum in wheat. These primers were designed from specific DNA sequences of the pathogen's ribosomal DNA. The amplification of ITS rDNA also has been employed to identify a wide variety of fungi that are pathogenic or allergenic (Maughlin et al. 2011).

Knowledge of sequences of pathogenic fungi requires high stringency PCR and avoids the problems of affinity and low reproducibility associated with random-primer methods (Sout and Strain 2000). One method of identifying fungi is based on the sequence variability found in the introns of metabolic and structural genes. Glass and Donaldson tested several small oligonucleotide primers for their ability to amplify DNA that spans across a selection of these genes. This method proved useful for phylogeny of filamentous fungi and related fungi. These oligonucleotide primers were used to differentiate Fusarium species (Donaldson et al. 1995). The PCR fragments generated were digested with several 4bp recognition restriction enzymes. The short region site, four bases, of the restriction endonuclease increased the probability that a single base polymorphism could be detected.

Two methods of high-stringency PCR are microsatellite based PCR amplification and the use of small ribosomal DNA (SSU rDNA) based primers for fingerprinting (Scoff and Straus 2000). For example, Gargas and DePristo (1996) used a list of PCR primers to identify small sequences of fungal DNA. This information identifies species (amplification-intron, species, etc.) and represents a valuable tool for further research. Grope et al. (1995) synthesized oligonucleotides corresponding to regions of the microsatellite locus of Aspergillus asycypete. These were used for PCR amplification of DNA from different species isolates. The DNA from most produced a potentially useful result for the selection of a molecular marker for population studies of Epichloa and other unrelated fungi.

DNA sequencing, while not yet utilized because of time and resources required, has been employed in a limited space in species identification. Wang reported on the beta gene to identify species of Aspergillus flavi. The isolates were isolated and their hyphae grown on media and media. Mitochondria were collected by centrifugation and DNA extracted. The sequence of the beta gene has also been used to distinguish species of Aspergillus fumigatus (Wang et al. 2000) and for identifying the phylogenetic relationships of species of Aspergillus flavi.

Single-Strand Conform Polymorphism (SSCP) is another method that requires knowledge of the target sequence to generate oligonucleotide primers. In this technique, the target DNA is amplified using a labeled primer. The PCR product is then denatured and revealed. Any changes, mutations, etc.) in the DNA are detected as altered separated sequences in autrograms. Previous studies about the exact sequence can be obtained by using targeted DNA and amplification for sequence detection (Hayashi 1991).

Heterologous analysis, like SSC, is a recently developed technique that can detect a single base difference in DNA after PCR amplification. The strands are incubated after heat denaturation and then annealed to form mismatched nucleotides. Nucleotides, by substitutions, insertions, deletions, etc., will affect the DNA structure of the heteroduplexes and lower mobility. The heteroduplex is then subjected to electrophoresis to duplexes with base considered to electrophoresis. Kumed and Saio (2001) used this technique for the detection of intraspecific variation in Aspergillus flavi. In this heteroduplex analysis (HAP), the fragments of the internal sequence (ITS regions of the rDNA from the different isolates were amplified. Heteroduplexes were then generated with the standard ITS reference framework and then subjected to electrophoresis. Many of these studies were designed to develop methods to distinguish between closely related taxa within a single genus or species for which morphological or physiological characters take too long to develop.
