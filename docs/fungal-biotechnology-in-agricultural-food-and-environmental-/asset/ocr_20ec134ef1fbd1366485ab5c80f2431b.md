# 2.3 Origins and Evolution of Degradation Pathways

The xenobiotic nature of these compounds and their poor solubility may explain why, to date, they have accumulated in the environment. However, within the last decade after considerable exposure, bacteria are now being isolated from contaminated environments that are capable of mineralizing some of these explosives. For example, a TNT has been isolated from contaminated water samples by nitrobacterium (Parales 2000). Examination of the genes associated with this bacterium indicates that the evolution of the pathways is a recent event. This is suggested by the fact that the pathway is not optimized, there are vestigial genes, a lack of co-ordinated regulation, and incomplete (Parales 2000).

# 3 BIODEGRADATION OF TNT

In contrast to the newly evolved pathways we see in the bacteria, fungi have the capability to biod explosives using existing, co-metabolic, pathways that lead to mineralization. Parrish (1971) published the results of a screening for TNT degrading capacity among 190 fungi. This study showed that fungi were capable of TNT degradation, but the sensitivity of the fungi to levels exceeding 20mg/ppm was such that Benuri (1994) reported that the fungi used in biodegradation were inhibited. Results of this work have led to more de novo exposure of these explosives for some years. However, more recent screens have shown that the ability to degrade TNT to some degree was demonstrated by fungi genera such as Asoderma, Mycomdesporium (Schreiber et al. 1997b). Recent data suggests that the fungi are capable of achieving mineralization of TNT at rates far higher than Hawari et al. (2000a).

# 2.2 Nitrate Esters and Methylene Nitrate Cycles

Nitrate esters, including glycerol nitrate (nitroglycerin) and pentatethyl tetrahydro nitrate (PETN) are chemically active at low and at high concentrations they are acutely toxic (Geronty et al. 1994). Other nitrate esters, like nitrocellulose (Figure 1c), are non-toxic and relatively stable. Naturally occurring, biologically generated, or de-nitrated esters appear very rarely. There is a single report of an insect sex female that contains a high level of nitro ester (Hall et al. 1993).

Another group of compounds that are significant environmentally contaminants are cyclic ethers of methyl nitramine (Geronty et al. 1994) and Figure 1d and e). This group includes the most powerful explosive, RDX (cyclicotrimethylnitramine) and HMX (cyclicotetramethyletetraninitramine). RDX is often mixed with TNT and is therefore a common environmental-contaminant. It has also a limited aqueous solubility of 42.3 mg/l at 20°C (Geronty et al. 1994).
