## 1 INTRODUCTION

The first use of yeast for winemaking is lost in the dawn of the first agricultural civilizations. There are reports of winemaking as far as 7400 years ago. Until middle of the last millennium, wines were mainly produced around the Mediterranean Sea and the Caucasus. Then, winemaking spread with the European colonies (Preterius 2000). Until 1860, fermentation was not well understood from a microbiological point of view. In fact, Pasteur showed that a living microorganism, the yeast, was responsible for the formation of the sugar present in the must into ethanol and CO2. In many genera, the Saccharomyces, and mainly the species S. cerevisiae, is responsible for this transformation. Because of this, S. cerevisiae is referred as the “wine yeast” (Preterius 2000).

*Saccharomyces-pulcherrina* is often present, followed by a group of film-forming yeasts (Pichia anomalus) or pigmented yeasts (Rhodotorula sp.). In a general study of yeasts isolated from grapes, it was noticed that the profile of yeast species may also vary from region to region (Martini and Vaghan-Manti 1990). Numerous factors affect the total yeast population and the relative proportions of individual species conditions. These environmental factors, the variety and the degree of maturity at harvest, the use of fungicides and the physical damage of the grapes (Fleet and Heard 1992). The yeast diversity in wine-producing strains is strongly related to the variety and characteristics of the wine produced from one to another. Most significant finding was that S. cerevisiae is practically absent from grapes and vineyard soils (Martini and Vaghan-Manti 1990). The presence of other species is the subject of debate. Some authors propose that this is a “natural” organism present in plant fruits (Mortimer and Polinski 1999; Siniewski et al. 2002). Others argue that it is due to hybridization of other Saccharomyces species, which have been selected over several centuries to fully adapt to man-made environments such as wineries (Martini 1993). Finally other authors state that S. cerevisiae is a domesticated species that originated from its closest relative, S. parados, a wild species found all around the world that is associated with insects, tree exudates, and fermenting plant extracts (Naumov 1996).

### 3.1 Methods for Yeast Identification

The specificity of nucleic acids has prompted the development of several methods for rapid identification. The comparison of RNA (rRNA) and ribosomal DNA (rDNA) has been used extensively in recent years to assess closeness and detect relationships among many kinds of organisms as a result of the conserved sequences within the region, and the concerted evolution among regions (Kurtmann and Robert 1998). Using the information from region, different methods have been developed for yeast identification.
