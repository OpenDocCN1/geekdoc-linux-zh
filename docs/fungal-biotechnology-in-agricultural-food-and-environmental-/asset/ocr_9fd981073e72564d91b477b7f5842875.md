# 6 POTENTIAL APPLICATIONS OF WHITE ROT FGI

# 7 CONCLUSIONS

# 6.1 Upgrading Agricultural Wastes for Feed

The direct use of lignocellulosic residues as ruminant food, or as a component of such feeds, represents one of the oldest and most widespread applications and, as such, it plays an important role in the ruminant diet. The lignocellulose complex in straw and plant residues is degraded slowly by ruminants, by physical and chemical induced enzymatic processes, preventing free access of hemicellulose to their substrates. The natural rate of plant debris is proportional to its lignin content. Delignification by white rot fungi seems to be the most promising way of improving digestibility (Kanra and Zadarski et al. 1982; Zadarski and Reinert 1988). The role of fungi in agricultural waste conversion by different species has been recently reviewed (Cohen and Hadar 2001).

Lignin degradation plays a central role in the earth's carbon cycle, since it hinders decomposition of the most renewable carbon sources, cellulose and hemiculose. White rot fungi able to complete wood decay are the most efficient agents for recycling the lignified tissues. Considerable research has been devoted to understanding the structure of lignin, and the degradation of lignin by these fungi. Many small enzymes and molecules including reactive oxygen species and acids have been demonstrated playing a role in lignin degradation by white rot fungi in nature. However, a majority are required to effective contribution of the individual components of the lignolytic system and the synergism that exists between them. Overcoming the difficulties associated with studying the degradation of such complex polymer could enable this. Research in these directions is advisable in developing the many potential biotechnological applications for the lignolytic system of white rot fungi.

# 6.2 Biopulp

The objective of biopulp is to extract cellulose fibers from plant material, or soft wood tissues. Mechanical and chemical processes are usually employed for this purpose. However, a biological approach using white rot fungi could replace environmentally harmful, chemicals (e.g. chlorine) and improve the quality of pulp and the properties of paper (Breen and Singleton 1999). The ligninolytic enzymes of white rot fungi selectively remove or allow lignin and cellulose fibers to be isolated. A potential and economically feasible alternative to current pulp methods (Akhtar et al. 2000; Scott et al. 1998).

# 6.3 Mushroom Production

# REFERENCES

Several of the fungi that utilize lignocellulose are edible mushrooms. They have been successfully cultivated at a commercial level using woodocellulosic wastes as the main substrate (Wood and Smith 1987). Agaricus bisporus, known as the “mushroom”, and L. edodes, known as “oyster mushroom”, are two examples of this agricultural-biotechnological crop. Bioconversion of lignocellulosic residues through mushroom cultivation could offers the potential for converting these residues into protein-rich palatable food, reducing the environmental impact of the wastes.
