## 7 MODELING FUNGAL DECAY COMMUNITIES

From here it is evident that significant limitations exist in both field and laboratory-based studies of wood-fungal communities. The information reported is at best quantitative; a complete understanding of the environment based on observation is probably unattainable due to the complexity, which may be approached in various ways. Conventional methods often involve an empirical base or field based to investigate the interactions of fungi and their temporal response to the biotic and abiotic environment. These may be analyzed systematically or further clarified using models based on experiments. Early fungal ecology research involved the development of conceptual and experimental models (Carroll and Walker 1992). The use of conceptual representations or models has been instrumental in expanding the boundaries of knowledge and understanding of the natural environment. A model is, necessarily, a simplified version of a reality. The value of models is that allows us to first represent them and then make measurements and predictions that would otherwise be awkward. Theoretical or mathematical models based on experimental observations have been characterized as increasingly being used, e.g., in hypothesising about population dynamics (which might be considered here but the reader is referred to Dekker 1999). A particular advantage of linking models is the ability to test them and make predictions. It is therefore possible to test and validate the theoretical model by predicting the result of changing an factor in the experimental system and then observing whether the result occurs. The development of theoretical models to describe any real ecological system will always be contentious, given the inevitable simplification or reduction of the system required by such approach. However, theoretical models should allow inferences to be made regarding the mechanisms and feedback loops involved within a working model. So, as Moorhead and Reynolds (1992), "the process of developing tests under the system identifies areas of uncertainty and, importantly, provides a means of examining alternative frameworks." Future work may improve and test the suitability of general ecology laws for organisms.
