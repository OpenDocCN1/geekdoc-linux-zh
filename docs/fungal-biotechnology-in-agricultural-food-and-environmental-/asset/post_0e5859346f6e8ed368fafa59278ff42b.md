# 4 BIGRADATION OF NITRATE ESTERS BY FUNGI

Substituted nitrates have a fairly low aqueous solubility. Glycerol nitrate saturates at 1.5 g/l, whilst fully substituted is completely insoluble in water (Williams and Bruce 2000). Since these compounds are relatively stable, there is some evidence that in the natural environment they can be reduced, forming alcohols and nitrates (Williams and Bruce 2000). There is a dearth of literature on fungal biodegradation of nitrate esters and the possible pathways by which degradation could occur. The few cited reports have some negative effects on nitrate are attributed to mitotic fungi and in which C-C bond, ring formation was shown to have denitrification capacity, generating dinitrate and glycerol monitrate the predominant (DuBois et al.). In Basidiomycetes, the enzymes responsible for this process are glutathione-S transaminases (Servant et al. 1991). Glutathione-S transferase was found located in the cytosol. Furthermore, workers also identified a cytosolic and P450-like activity with similar activity. There are at least two classes of enzymes involved in the denitrification of TNT; an oxygen insensitive glutathione-degrading enzyme that liberates nitrate, and an oxygen-sensitive NADP+-dependent P450-like activity liberates nitrates. In all cases of fungal biodegradation of nitrate esters, adequate carbon sources had to be supplied, and degradation is only partial. Even when a cellular stimulus is combined in situ with nitrating species (Sclerotium rolfsii solarium) fungi decomposition is incomplete (Sharma et al. 1995).

# 5 BIGRADATION OF RDX/HMX BY FUNGI

In contrast to TNT, RDX and HMX are inherently less recalcitrant. There is a change in the ring structure, for example by N-O bond or C-C bond, the ring structure undergoes spontaneous decomposition, producing mineral and carbon compounds that are easily mineralized by many species of microorganisms (Hawari 2000). However, there is only a limited literature on the biodegradation of cyclic nitrates (Sundaram et al. 1995). Bayman and Redkar (1997) report that mycelium of P. chrysogenum, Cladosporum resinae, Cyathus patyll, and Cunninghamella var elegans were not inhibited by 50–100 μ/ml RDX, and that the RDX “disappeared” when incubated with these fungal species (Scheibner et al. 1997a,b).
