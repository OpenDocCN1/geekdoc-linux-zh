This increased scrutiny of environmental and mammalian safety of entomopathogenic fungi is part of a movement towards more awareness of potential negative impacts of technology. ## 7 CONCLUSIONS

Biotechnological approaches to the study and development of entomopathogenic fungi have advanced the field in recent years. Improvements in formulation allowing new biopesticides to succeed under unexpected conditions (such as control in Africa), selection and identification have not only biopesticide formulation, but understanding of disease processes and ecology. Some methods have enabled better monitoring as well as development of phylogenetic classification. Despite some, use of entomopathogenic fungi is restricted by high cost, inadequate or inconsistent efficacy, limited production capacity, and poor self efficacy. However, entomopathogenic fungi have several advantages for formulation in biopesticides that have a robust spore stage, capable of survival for months or years. Some are to go on simple methods and can be formulated using several procedures.

Products have required mammalian toxicology packages to be submitted for registration to demonstrate safety, and been considered to have low mammalian toxicity [Don-Pavello et al. (1980) and Shaduck et al. (1982)]. Recent papers on Metarhizium and Beauveria have raised some issues regarding mammalian safety of insect metabolites (Flaker et al., 1994).

In addition to viewing entomopathogenic fungi as potentially useful, there has been consideration of their effect as hazards in entomopathogenic fungi. Some species are showing showing activity towards humans, such as P. bassiana producing cytotoxic cytomans (Mann et al., 2001). Production of such toxicity has caused problems in the registration of some fungi in Europe. Strasser et al. (2000) demonstrated that secondary metabolites (such as bassianin, beauvericin, and beauaverides) produced by the genus Beauveria, Metarhizium, and Toboldium. found that fungal baits posed no obvious risk to humans, although the number of details studied is limited. Some studies have shown activity against selected selected baits as destructors of Metarhizium with an intrapetoabdominal injection LD50 of 1-16mg/kg. However, the levels of metabolites produced during insect infection have much lower than expected.

There are many new techniques that will aid in the further development of entomopathogenic fungi. Application of molecular biology to enhance entomopathogenic fungi also holds the promise to improve growth through genetic modification, or assist without genetic modification. For example, techniques such as protoplast fusion and chromosome exchange, knowledge of chromosomal gene expression and selection of superior strains in single isolates. Understanding the genetics of fungi is specificity, the toxins and enzymes involved in the disease, and processes of fungal structures as sporulation and germination are all under the world. There is a growing research on realistic impact of fungal-based insecticides (e.g. Gottlieb et al. (2001) and Hagen and Jaeger (2002), which have not shown negative effects. Some evidence shows that they are safe in production from both an environmental and mammalian toxicity viewpoint (Gottlieb et al., 1993). While the fungi have not found to be risk to soil and natural exposure, the development of novel formulations will require careful evaluation to ensure their safety. Any development of genetically modified strains in all countries are becoming more stringent, especially for genetically modified organisms. The potential of entomopathogenic fungi lies not just in their applications, but also in the isolation and development from various sources, and as their techniques for enhancing the potential for biopesticide use as mixtures of pathogens. While the history of biopesticide development is limited with more failures, the future seems brighter. As the knowledge of commercially successful products and new technologies, we can expect to see more biological control methods to inspect pests using fungal species.
