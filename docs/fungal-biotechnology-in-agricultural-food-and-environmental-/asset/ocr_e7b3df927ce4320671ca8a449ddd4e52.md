# 1 INTRODUCTION

Lignocellulose are defined as plant or wood cell walls in which celluloses and hemicellulose are intimately associated with lignin. The role of lignin is to provide strength, serve as barrier against microbial attack, and as water impermeable seal the cell walls in the system tissue (Argyropulos and Massias 1997). Most aromatic monomers are synthesized from the phenolic precursors, coniferyl, sinapyl and p-coumaryl alcohols (Sarkanden and Ludwig 1971). Free radical condensation of these precursors initiated by plant cell wall peroxidases yields the formation of a heterogeneous, amorphous, randomly and highly branched polymer with at 12 different types of linkages such as ether- and carbon bonds connecting the aromatic nuclei. These structures impose unusual restrictions on the enzymatic attack (Hataka 1994; Higuchi 1990; and Farrell 1987). Most biological molecules such as cellulose are generally linear polymers whose subunits are linked by a repeating unit, and the mechanism of polymerization is generally centered around the common bonds. The complexity of the lignin polymer means, however, that any initial attack must be oxidative, non-hydrolytic, and extracellular (Hataka 1994; Higuchi 1990; and Farrell 1987). Lignin is particularly complex to biodegrade, the cellulose, hemicellulose, and other cell wall constituents with which it is intimately associated are

protected from degradation and as a result their bioavailability is very low. Lignin degradation, therefore, plays a central role in the earth's carbon cycle, since most renewable carbon is either in lignin or in cellulose and hemicellulose. This chapter will focus on the biodegradation of lignin with particular emphasis on white rots, which are the most efficient degraders and the only organisms that can completely degrade lignin to carbon dioxide (CO2) and water (H2O) (Kirk and Farrell 1987).

# 2 BROWN AND SOFT ROT FUNGI

Only a few organisms are capable of degrading the aromatic polymer, the most efficient of which are fungi. Three groups of fungi are capable of lignin degradation (Eiksson 1970): White rot, brown rot, and soft rot (Table 1).

Brown fungi belonging to the basidiomycetes degrade cellulose and can only modify the lignin (Eiksson 1990). Demethylation is the obvious consequence of attack on lignin these fungi. The brown rot fungi grow mainly in the cell wall next to the sapwood (generalized 2000). The residual wood is brown and cracks into cubic pieces when dry. Brown rot fungi have an obvious preference for coniferous substrates, which are softwoods (gymnosperms); A survey of substrate reports reported reported that 19% of North American basidiomycetes are brown rot fungi.
