
## 7.3 Screening for New Fungal Strains

Strain selection for explosive-degrading fungi is far from complete. The rather random screening approach used to date has shown the ability to degrade substrates like TNT across fungal genus (Schiebner 1997a,b). Most of the species have been from temperate regions. However, the tropical species have greater lignolytic capabilities as they are able to decompose lignin more widely than temperate species (1976). Many fungi with lignin degradation ability can partially degrade TNT and explosives by unknown metabolic routes. Understanding the enzymology associated with these reactions would be a priority. ## 7.5 Use of Sorption Strategies

Biotransformation strategies are generally considered alternatives to mineralization (Hawari 2000a,b; Istabler 1984; Weber et al. 2002). In experiments, mass balance equations prove that mineralization occurs but TNT products "disappear." The process appears to be due to irreversible binding (sorption) of TNT products with the soil humic and clay fraction (Achtman et al. 1999; Elovitz and Weber et al. 2002; Sorption mechanisms). (3). Sorption interactions and consequences also depend on the content of the pollutant, and the amount of clay and organic content of the soil. TNT can also be modified via oxidative-reactive pathways including binding (Hague 1992). The driving force behind accepting that TNT is a saturated endpoint in bioremediation is the poor results so far obtained in remediation of soil using fungal inoculum. Such approach needs careful evaluation as Palmer et al. (1999) found that apparently irreversibly bound products retain sufficient nutrients under moist conditions to be detectable in barren soils. The presence of these pollutants was not detectable by conventional analytical chemical techniques. Sorption is influenced by pH and will release sorbed molecules (Head). ## 8 CONCLUSIONS

There is clearly plenty for new approaches to bioremediation of explosives-contaminated soils and sediments. There is increasing evidence that metabolically produced products of the degradation of explosive compounds such as TNT are not tightly bound to clay and organic matter as was found. Their presence in the ecosystem can be detected by assays, in terms of chemical metabolites, and various techniques have been used to fully exploit the capacity of fungi to minimize these compounds by naturally occurring pathways. This appears to be a matter of selection of strains and fungi that have been screened for their degrading capability. We need better understanding of metabolic pathways involved in the strains and the microbial ecology of these fungi when used in bioremediation. Further work is needed before large scale, field-based trials are undertaken that may create many challenges as they overcome.
