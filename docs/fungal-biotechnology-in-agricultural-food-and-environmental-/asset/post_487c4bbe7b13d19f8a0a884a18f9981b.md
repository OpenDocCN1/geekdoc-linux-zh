## 3 Biological Factors

Weeds are highly variable by nature, with many species possessing various biotypes. For this reason, the genetic diversity of weed populations can present several challenges for herbicide agents, such as extreme variability in strains of fungal pathogens (Auld and Morin 1995; Boyetchko et al. 2002). Such is the case with nutgesedge that has went variability within populations, and greater variability in susceptibility to herbicide agents (Horak et al. 1987; Okoyi et al. 2002). The success of Coleotrichum gloosporioides f. sp. aeschymorene as the Colego® was attributed to the ubiquity of the susceptible host (Tempelson 1998). A better understanding of the target weed specification will contribute to the consistent performance for herbicide agents on genetically diverse weed species (Boyetchko et al. 2002). Another consideration is the view that herbicides might target only one particular weed species. The advantage of a highly host-specific bioicide candidate is the assurance that neighboring, beneficial plant species, or crops closely related to the weed will not be affected (Makowski 1997; Mortensen 1998). ### 3.3 Technical Factors

The feasibility of controlling biocicide agents depends on the ease and cost of manufacturing and producing large amounts of viable, and highly effective microbial pathogens (Auld and Morse 1995; Mortensen 1998; Stinger 1998). It is generally believed that submerged fermentation is the most efficient mass-production method for biocontrol agents (Auld et al. 1996). Although general conditions for fungal germination and inoculum composition are available in public data (Stanbury et al. 1995), most commercial formulations are designed for specific and are generally treated as such. However, when seen in bioherbicide technology, the ability to produce the agent economically and at a cost that is affordable for farmers represents development and commercialization potential. Lack of reliable field performance due to inadequate formulation and application technology has also a major reason for the loss of control in bioherbides (Greaves et al. 2000; Peng et al. 2001). It has been claimed that suitable technology may help reduce some of the environmental factors particularly moisture requirements that often hinder the development of bioherbicide candidates and the discovery and evaluation phase. Foliar-applied fungal agents generally require free water for germination.
