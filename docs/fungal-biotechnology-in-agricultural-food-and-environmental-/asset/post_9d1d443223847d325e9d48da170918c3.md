Unlike plants, fungal chromosomes can be very variable, and many fungal species differ in length and size of their chromosomes. ## 3.6 Inserted Elements

Both nuclear and mitochondrial genes in fungi can contain a wide range of inserted elements [e.g., Edelmann and Staben (1994): chapters 11 and 12]. At the very simplest these may be short sequences left after insertion and removal, and at the more complex they imply a wide range of different insertions [Deaboussi (1997); Daubaix and Langais (1994)]. Inserted elements, particularly investigated as potential molecular markers at the population level and the higher phylogenetic level [DoPrest (1993; Neveglise et al. 1997].

## 3.7 Application of Markers

The suitability and resolution of identified markers will depend much on the fungi being considered, and a marker that is useful for differentiating species in one group, may be less convenient in other. One example of this is the use of putative RLPs (AT rich DNA). The mtDNA RLPs have been used for determining species and subpopulations in some species of Aspergillus, for example the species Metarhizium and Verticillium. Similar approach applied in other subgroups (Koupovas and Typas 1997; Vargas et al. 1994; F. oxysporum sp. f. cDNA RLPs have been used for differentiating between different races and species that support the same host as the existing race 1 or 2 (Thomas et al.). In the basidioderma Ganoderma bonense mtDNA RLPs have been found useful for defining individuals (Miller et al. 1999), and in Phytophthora species they were used for differentiating lines (Whittaker et al. 2004).

## 3.5 Mitochondrial Sequences

The mitochondrial genome has been used in the investigation of population structures in the plant pathogen. In fungi the mitochondrial genome is a circular structure of 171 and 122 kb (Zee et al (1968); Roche and Koch (1999); Grossman and Hussard (1985); Lu (1996)). The mitochondrial genome is mostly smaller than the nuclear DNA and contains a high proportion of sequences that lack coding function. In addition it may contain many repeated sequences and introns, and these features can allow for variation in mitochondrial sequences between closely related organisms (Clark-Walker et al. (1987); see chapters 11 and 12 in this book). As is seen in many copies, mtDNA can be inherited uniparentally with the nucleus during meiosis but undergo recombination. In addition, mtDNA can be transferred independently of the nuclear genome during vegetative fusion (Collis and Savillo 1990). mtDNA can contain GC rich palindromic repeats, simple repeats, and in fungal morphology, this has been used in DNA restriction protocols to prevent premature mitochondrial RLPs (e.g., Koupovas and Typas 1997). In some closely related species may have different mitochondrial genomes, and one example of this is the case in Saccharomyces, where mtDNA varies from 24 to 78 kb between species (see Geman and Hudberg (1985)). Analysis of mtDNA variation has been used at a variety of population and systematic levels, and these have correlated with species, vegetative incompatibility groups, different populations and individuals (Gordon and Okamoto 1992; Jacobson and Gordon 1999; Miller et al.).

Differences in the level of variation within a marker from different to species is not significant, and appear to be features of markers investigated for population level investigation. This is of the degree of sequence difference between isolates of a single species, or closely related species. As an example there is found up to 5% sequence variation within isolates group of Colletotrichum (Sreesenivas Prasad 1998). In Rhizctonia solani, up to 30% in the mtDNA sequences has been found between isolates of the same anastomosis group (Kunnaga et al. 1997).
