# Molecular Methods for Identification of Plant Pathogenic Fungi

Maren A. Klich / Edward J. Mullaney U.S. Department of Agriculture–Agricultural Research Service, New Orleans, Louisiana, USA

## 1 INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 Need for Molecular Methods in Fungal Identification

For centuries, fungal identification has been based on morphological, physiological, and chemical characteristics of specimens. For the most part, these systems still work extremely well. They provide accurate species identification inexpensive, not labor-intensive, and require little equipment beyond a microscope and chemical reagents. Since phenotype is the result of expression of genes the molecular classification is difficult and generally unsound. A drawback of the traditional identification methods is that they require some technical training in order to acquire the skills necessary to identify fungi or characterize strains. Training has become increasingly easy to acquire and molecular techniques are now widely used in secondary schools and can be applied to a multitude of fields. Another drawback of the traditional is that they may take weeks or more for fungal colonies to grow and develop characters for identification. In some cases, characters never develop. The molecular methods identify more quickly and do not rely on the reproductive structures.

Molecular biology has brought many powerful tools to fungal taxonomy including the potential for rapid identification of isolates, methods for rapid determination of virulence or toxicity of strains, and methods to evaluate the relationships among fungal species. DNA sequence data provide data points that can be compared among fungi and analyzed to determine sequence relatedness, which can be assumed to reflect phylogenetic relationship among species. If “phylylogenetic trees” from molecular species concur with one another and “trees” based on morphological and physiological data, one has fairly strong evidence that the phylogeny is accurate. Some fungi do not grow or do not sporulate in culture. Molecular methods allow identification of these isolates by comparison of DNA sequence data from the unknown isolate. Molecular methods have also been used to distinguish species with morphological differences and to distinguish strains (or specific isolates) within a species. In studies of fungal metabolites, especially mycotoxins, there has been no way of knowing whether a nonproducing strain is truly (genetically) incapable of producing it under different environmental conditions. The genes of a metabolic pathway have been cloned, they can be used to determine whether or not a strain possesses the genes for production of the metabolite, providing a better indication of the potential of a given strain to produce a metabolite.

### 1.2 Assumptions and Limitations

The assumptions made in fungal identification using molecular methods are the same as those made using traditional methods. A species (or any other taxonomic level) is identified while working in the field of agriculture. These assumptions are based on reliable data, but there is always some degree of subjectivity in the process, especially when considering related species.

None of the current methods used in fungal identification can scan more than a minor portion of the genome. While a single method can be used to distinguish all species or all genera from one another. Generally, if the data provided by a molecular method support current taxonomic thought on a taxon, they are used.

### 1.3 Scope

It would be of benefit of this paper to review all of the studies using molecular methods for identification. We provide an overview of the methods and then give one or two recent examples from the literature. These citations will interest readers seeking a starting point for finding related citations.
