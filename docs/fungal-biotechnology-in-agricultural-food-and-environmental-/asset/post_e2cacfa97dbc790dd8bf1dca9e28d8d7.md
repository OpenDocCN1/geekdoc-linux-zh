## 3.5.3 Other Mycotoxins Produced by Fusarium

Some fungi are capable of producing mycotoxins other than CTs and Fe. Zealenone (ZE) [6-(hydroxy-6-oxo-trans-1-undecenyl)-β lactone], a mycotoxin produced by scabby wheat fungus, F. graminearum species), is most known. Also called F-2, is a phytotoxin causing estrogenic effects and reproductive problems in offspring, ZE is known to bind to estrogen receptors and stimulates protein synthesis by mimicking hormonal action (Zepnik et al. 2001). Zealenone can be toxic to plants; it can inhibit seed germination and growth at low concentrations. Natural contamination with ZE occurs in cereal grains such as corn, and contamination of feed with ZE.

## 4 PREVENTIVE MEASURES

## 4.1 Management of Mycotoxin Contamination

The economic implications of the mycotoxin problem and potential health threats to humans have created a need to eliminate or at least minimize mycotoxin contamination of food and feed. As association between mycotoxin contamination and inadequate storage has been shown in various studies, crops are contaminated with myoxins prior to harvest (Lisker and Lillehog 1991). However, management of mycotoxin contamination in commodities must include both pre and postharvest control measures (CAST 2003). Myoxin can be reduced somewhat by using resistant varieties (effective, but not all) and after early harvest, crop production methods, control of insect pests, etc reviewed in Bacon al (2001); Duvik (2002); Shini and Bhatnagar (1999). Significant control of toxin contamination is expected to be dependent on a detailed understanding of the physiological and environmental factors that affect the growth of the fungi, and the problems of the host-plant interactions. Efforts are underway to study these parameters primarily for the most significant significant toxins, namely AFs, Fms, and TCTCs

## 4.2 Postharvest Contamination

After harvest, crop should not be allowed to wither in the field as well as subject to injury and mechanical damage. Grains can be cleaned and dried to less than 10-30% and stored in a clean area to avoid root and infection (Trehlin et al. 1998). Postharvest mycotoxin contamination is prevalent in some areas of a hot, wet climate with sub-adequate methods of handling, storage, and practices, which often lead to severe fungal growth and mycotoxin contamination of food and drink (Birzele et al. 2000; Phillips et al.). Some toxins have been found in storage and transport to prevent health concerns. However, this is not a solution to the contamination problem. Irradiation has been suggested as a possible means of controlling infection and microbial populations in stored food, reducing the hazard of mycotoxins under these cases in Sharma (1998). Significant emphasis has been placed on the detoxification methods to eliminate the toxins from the contaminated lots or at least reduce the toxin hazards by bringing down the mycotoxins under the acceptable limits.
