# Genetics and Biochemistry of Mycotoxin Synthesis

Jiujiang Yu U.S. Department of Agriculture–Agricultural Research Service, New Orleans, Louisiana, USA

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Mycotoxins are low-molecular weight, non-proteinaceous, organic secondary metabolites produced by fungi from amino acids, shikimic acid, or malonyl-CoA. These compounds are toxic, mutagenic, teratogenic, and carcinogenic to animals and humans (Bennett 1987; Groupman 1994; Hall and Wild 1994). Over 300 mycotoxins have been identified, but only those in mycotoxosis involving humans have been studied in detail in biochemistry. Research on the natural origin, identification and characterization, biosynthesis and genetic regulatory control of mycotoxins, as well as prevention and control of mycotoxin contamination of food and feed has been made since the incidence of Aflatosis disease in 1960 when 10,000 turkeys died due to aflatoxin contamination in the peanut-meal. Due to the risk of contamination of food and feed on human health and livestock production (Bennett et al. 1998; Eaton and Groupman 1994), regulations have been established in over 50 countries for acceptable levels of mycotoxins in commodities for commerce or food and human and animal consumption (Bhatnagar et al. 2002; Sharma and Saluki 1991). Since then, significant progress towards the understanding of these toxins has been made. In this chapter, the genetics and biochemistry of only the most significant mycotoxins, aflatoxins, sterigocystin (ST), trichotheces, and fumonisins, are discussed. For other mycotoxins such as alternative Aflatoxins, ochratins, citrinin, cyclopiazonic acid (CPA), patruline, zearalenone, ergot alkaloids and related toxins, or other neurotoxic mycotoxins, kindly refer to the most recent comprehensive reviews on this subject (Bhatnagar et al. 1988; 2002).

## 2 AFLATOXINS AND STERIGMACOSTYTOXINS

### 2.1 Occurrence and Toxicity

Aflatoxins are toxic and carcinogenic secondary metabolites produced primarily by Aspergillus flavus and parasitic as A. nomius. One isolate of A. tamari (Goto et al. 1999) and A. ochraceaceous have been reported to produce aflatoxins (Klich et al. 2000). The aflatoxins consist of a group of at least 16 related structures (Klich 1969) which are designated as B, B1, B2, G1, G2, AFB, AFBG, and AFG which are the most common types. Aflatoxins are polyketide-derived, bis-chain-containing dihydrofuran and tetrahydrofuran moieties (rings) with a substituted carbon (Figure 1). A. flavus produces aflatoxins B, B1, B2, G1, and G2, and M1, M2, which are the four major forms of aflatoxin. Aflatoxin B is found in milk. Aflatoxin B (AFB) is the most potent naturally occurring toxin and carcinogen (Sifris and Samson 1999). The relative toxicity of the toxins has been established in the following: B1 > G2 > G1. Aflatoxin M1 is 10-fold less toxic than B1, but is present in milk of human (Ullman et al. 1987; Galvano et al. 1996). Due to the toxic exposure of aflatoxin by Lombard and Eaton (1981; Eaton and Groupman 1994), these are the most studied of all the mycotoxins, and significant research has been conducted on their biosynthetic pathway and genetic control of its regulation (Bhatnagar et al. 2002; Chang et al. 1996; Payne and Brown et al. 1995).

Sterigmatoxin and dihydrosterigmatoxin (DHST) are produced by various Aspergillus including A. nidulans, several genera of Biotrichum and Penicillium are also reported to produce ST (Cole and Cox 1987). Aspergillus niger is an industrial fungus and has the model organism for the study of ST biosynthesis, and ST and DHST are precursors (Figure 1; Cena 1989; Cars 1999). Like aflatoxin, ST and DHST are carcinogenic and mycotoxins. (1989) is considered a health hazard as well. Since ST and DHST precursors, they share a common biochemical pathway, homology and regulatory mechanism. For these reasons, aflatoxins and ST are discussed together.

It has long known that the aflatoxin pathway genes may be clustered with common genes (Cleveland and Bhatnagar 1991). The first experimental evidence showing the clustering of aflatoxin pathway genes was, however, demonstrated by Trail et al. (1998) when they found that the nor- and ver genes were linked in a cluster (norA) with the regulatory gene arf and a putative aflatoxin pathway gene, wom (now fas-1) in between. This observation provided evidence that at early stages of development they are linked by clustering of genes. In A. parasiticus and A. flavus (Yu et al. 1995a), a linkage of genes in aflatoxin formation, from early stage nor to late stage orta, was observed with that the entire pathway genes may be clustered.
