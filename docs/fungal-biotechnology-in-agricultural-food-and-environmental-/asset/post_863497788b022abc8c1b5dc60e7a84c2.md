## 1 INTRODUCTION

Lignocellulose are defined as plant or wood cell walls in which celluloses and hemicellulose are intimately associated with lignin. The role of lignin is to provide strength, serve as barrier against microbial attack, and as water impermeable seal the cell walls in the system tissue (Argyropulos and Massias 1997). Most aromatic monomers are synthesized from the phenolic precursors, coniferyl, sinapyl and $p$-coumaryl alcohols (Sarkanden and Ludwig 1971). Free radical condensation of these precursors initiated by plant cell wall peroxidases yields the formation of a heterogeneous, amorphous, randomly and highly branched polymer with at 12 different types of linkages such as ether- and carbon bonds connecting the aromatic nuclei. These structures impose unusual restrictions on the enzymatic attack (Hataka 1994; Higuchi 1990; and Farrell 1987). Most biological molecules such as cellulose are generally linear polymers whose subunits are linked by a repeating unit, and the mechanism of polymerization is generally centered around the common bonds. The complexity of the lignin polymer means, however, that any initial attack must be oxidative, non-hydrolytic, and extracellular (Hataka 1994; Higuchi 1990; and Farrell 1987). Lignin is particularly complex to biodegrade, the cellulose, hemicellulose, and other cell wall constituents with which it is intimately associated are protected from degradation and as a result their bioavailability is very low. Lignin degradation, therefore, plays a central role in the earth's carbon cycle, since most renewable carbon is either in lignin or in cellulose and hemicellulose. This chapter will focus on the biodegradation of lignin with particular emphasis on white rots, which are the most efficient degraders and the only organisms that can completely degrade lignin to carbon dioxide ($CO_2$) and water ($H_2O$) (Kirk and Farrell 1987).

## 2 BROWN AND SOFT ROT FUNGI

Only a few organisms are capable of degrading the aromatic polymer, the most efficient of which are fungi. Three groups of fungi are capable of lignin degradation (Eiksson 1970): White rot, brown rot, and soft rot (Table 1).

Brown fungi belonging to the basidiomycetes degrade cellulose and can only modify the lignin (Eiksson 1990). Demethylation is the obvious consequence of attack on lignin these fungi. The brown rot fungi grow mainly in the cell wall next to the sapwood (generalized 2000). The residual wood is brown and cracks into cubic pieces when dry. Brown rot fungi have an obvious preference for coniferous substrates, which are softwoods (gymnosperms); A survey of substrate reports reported reported that 19% of North American basidiomycetes are brown rot fungi, among 60 out of 71 (85%) occurs primarily on conifers, and that are mostly softwood degraders (Gilbertson 1980). They cause decay of timber in buildings (Blanchette 2000). One of the most destructive rot fungi is Serpula lacrymans, which is well adapted to attack service and can spread rapidly on wood and non-traditional surfaces. This type of decay has referred to as rot of decay, first apparently used to describe any deterioration of wood used in service is misleading. Because moisture must be present for the decay to occur.

Soft rot fungi are taxonomically classified in the subdivisions Ascomycota and Deuteromycota. They are active in environments that are too wet for brown-rot fungi, generally in wet environments, but they also degrade plant litter in soils (Blanchette 1995). Soft rots are relatively unspecialized cellulosic fungi in the genera Chaetium, Ceratopsis, and Philophora, etc., they degrade cellulose but only modify lignin.

Two distinct types of soft rot are also recognized (Blanchette 2000). Type 1 is characterized by lignocellululose formed within the secondary wall of wood and Type 2 results in erosion of the secondary wall. The modified cell wall is not degraded in cellulose to lignin by white rot fungi, but may be modified in advanced stages of decay. As decay progresses, cellulose loss occurs and lignin concentrations increase in the residual wood.

## 3 WHITE ROT FUNGI

White rot fungi are the only known that can completely break down lignin to $CO_2$ and $H_2O$ (Kirk and Harve 1987). Furthermore, lignin can be degraded as a soft source of energy and by rot fungus. Degradation of lignin enables them to gain access to cellulose and hemicellulose, as actual carbon and energy source. White rot fungi predominantly degrade from deciduous trees (angiosperms), containing hardwoods.

The many species that white rot are a heterogeneous group that may degrade more or lesser amounts of a specific wall component. Some species preferentially remove lignin from wood, leaving behind patches of decayed cellulose. This is known as selective delignification (Blanchette 1995; 1990). Other species degrade lignin and cellulose simultaneously which is referred to as non-selective delignification. The main white-rot fungi include Phanerochaete versicolor and Trametes versicolor which degrade lignin simultaneously.

Some fungi such as Ganoderma anatum and Hyphaetobasidium, which are capable of degrading cellulose (Blanchette 1990). The lignin-hemicellulose decay by a selective fungus can induce and even cause different strains of white rot fungi. The modified cell wall is not degraded in cellulose to lignin by white rot fungi, but may be modified in advanced stages of decay. As decay progresses, cellulose loss occurs and lignin concentrations increase in the residual wood.
