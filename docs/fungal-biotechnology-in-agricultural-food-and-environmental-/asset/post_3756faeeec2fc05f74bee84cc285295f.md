
# Ribosomal DNA

The nuclear genes of fungi have a number of features. They are relatively small (approximately 13–93 million base pairs), and in comparison to higher plants and animals they have much less redundant DNA (around 10–20%) (Lu 1996). Around 30% of the entire fungal genome consists of duplicated regions and genes (Mews 1997). These repetitive sequences provide potential targets for molecular studies due to their high copy number.

Ribosomal DNA (rDNA), specifically coding the rRNA subunits and their associated spacers, is one of the most commonly used genes for fungal molecular studies (for details, see www.mendel.berkeley.edu/boletes/html; www.biology.duke.edu/fungi). The nuclear-encoded genes occur as a gene cluster (typically of 8–12 kb) that is highly repeated (Figure 1). The basic unit contains genes for the 5S, 5.8S, and the large subunit. The genes are separated by internally transcribed spacers (ITS), and the repeated genes are separated by an intergenic spacer (IGS) that may, if not all, contain the genes for the 5S rRNA (see Hill and 1995). Sequence restrictions are conserved in fungal rDNA, and these make convenient sequences for cloning (other reviews by Gargas and DePique 1996; Hibbert 1992). The rDNA has proved to be a good region for cloning molecular markers for many fungi (Hibbert et al 1991). The subunit genes have conserved and variable domains, and the spacer regions are considerably more variable and can be used for comparisons of genes, or for comparisons of species in some taxonomic groups (see Table 1). Because rDNA is so highly and repeatedly repeated, it is a good target for molecular studies. These original studies using RFLP probes identified total genomic DNA species, and studies of populations and species of pathogens have been carried out in this way (e.g., Jabari-Hari et al (1990); Mansco (1990). More recently these studies have largely been replaced by polymerase chain reaction (PCR), as particularly the rRNA cluster can be detected in environmental samples (see Brun et al 1999). The varying levels of specificity in the gene cluster also means that it is possible to amplify fungal DNA directly from samples of infected tissue (see Johnson et al 1993).

Both the ITS and IGS regions have been used to develop species-specific markers for pathogen detection in plant material (e.g., Brown et al. 2003). It is increasingly common in rDNA clusters to show all regions of interest. Although the knowledge of the complete sequences provides a large amount of information, may not be useful for molecular restriction and rDNA amplification. This approach generally produces relatively similar patterns containing 1–4 bands, and in certain cases these patterns, or individual bands, may be specific for particular pathogens (e.g., Chen 2001). It is not possible to develop molecular markers. One example of this was the study of Mazloff et al (1996) who developed an oligonucleotide probe that consistently selected a marker for the pathogen Rhizoonia solani and R. oryzae. However, it should be remembered that RFLP analysis is essentially an analysis of variation; and although different patterns that rDNA are different, a common pattern is based on the position of a few restriction sites. Therefore, identical RFLP band patterns do not imply that the sequence of the segment is identical.

## 3.2 Coding Genes

### Ribosomal gene cluster

There are numerous genes that have been examined in the systematics and phylogeny of pathogenic fungi. These genes include the coding for actin, tubulin, elongation factors, cytochromes, and others (e.g., Glass and Donaldson 1995; Mannahan et al 1994). These genes have been highly conserved across species; but may contain introns that can be variable in insertion position and within (see Edleman and Spain 1994). These introns can be useful as a molecular marker across related organs and thus have been investigated for the study of plant associated fungi including Fusarium, Ascomycota, and Phomopsis (see later).

These techniques have also been referred to as total genome profiling. One of the most widely used of such PCR is random amplification polymeric (RAPD) analysis [see McDonald (1990; Williams et al. Essentially, RAPD analysis relies on the reduction of specificity of the PCR process at reduced temperatures, and these are amplified with short (usually decamer) primers and annealing techniques. These results result in stringent binding of the primer to the target DNA, and allow amplification of a number of small regions of DNA. These are separated by electrophoresis to provide a list of bands. The RAPD analysis has been used extensively for profiling populations of pathogenic fungi [patients et al. (1995); Cooke et al. (1996)]. In many cases these have shown correlations between markers and host type, disease or geographic orientation, and bands between different forms of the same fungi causing different symptoms [e.g., Peter et al. (1997)]. Another PCR fingerprinting has been used for sequencing of simple repetitive primers. In this method, short repetitive primers are used at moderate temperatures in order to amplify large fragments of the genome. One target site for this method is the flanking of regions that can contain tandem repeats. Repeat motifs that have been used for primers with 2-3 base repeats such as (CA)n and (CAG)n [Rodriguez (1995; Lage et al. (1998)] to probe more complex sequences including the M13 bacteriophage universal primers [Bridge et al. (1997)]. Another method of fingerprinting that has been used with group of short repetitive DNA sequences that have been found throughout the genome of diverse species [see van Belkam et al. (1998)]. Primers to these specific produce multiple products in PCR with genomic DNA, and then can be separated into simple fingerprints. Three particular unrelated families of repetitive DNA sequences, BOX (54 bp), ERC (124 bp), and REF (30-40 bp) have also been used to characterize subspecies of different fungi [see Aorata et al. (1996); Toda et al. (1999)].

A relatively recent development in fingerprinting fungi has been the amplification of fragment length polymorphic (AFLP) analysis [see Wang and Kuijper (1997)]. This method uses unique primers, restriction enzymes, and then short oligonucleotide primers (linkers) are linked to the restriction enzyme sites. Specific primers are then used to show that a particular degree of specificity to the linkage sequences, and large fractions of the genome are amplified. This method is advantageous as the analysis generates many bands, and electrophoresis is used with agarose gels, making it possible to undertake more restricted sequences that generate bands and can be analyzed in simple electrophoresis [see Müller et al.
