## 3.4 Antibiosis

Reports on the production of antimicrobial substances by AMF are not common. However, it was shown that some antimicrobial substances (unidentified) produced by the extracellular mycelium of the AMF species G. Itarides reduced fungal germination of F. oxysporum f. parasica inhibited by AMF (Filon et al. 1991). Budi et al. (1999) identified a Pseudomonas strain from the mycorrhizosphere of Sorghum grains inoculated with G. max. that exhibited considerable antagonism against P. parasitica. Regardless of the nature of these control activities, it is important to realize and utilize the significance of AMF in plant disease control. Research in this may prove to fruitful in the control of pathogenic bacteria and fungi.

## 4 CHALLENGES AND STRATEGIES TO ENHANCING ARBUSC FUNGAL EFFECT IN DISEASE CONTROL

## 4.1 Challenges

Although the efficacy of AMF species on plants has been assessed under the controlled environments and usually in the absence of AMF or other fungi (Budi et al. 1999); Kasya et al. 1996; Li et al. 1997), research suggests that the potential of AMF for control of plant pathogens is high. Limitations in AMF research pertaining to biocontrol of plant disease involve two worlds: (a) production of large quantities of pathogen is not feasible because of the biotrophic status of AMF, and (b) negative interactions between the AMF and indigenous microbial community introduced into field. The interactions between AMF and indigenous microbial community composition and environmental conditions limit the extent of AMF inoculation in plant disease control under field conditions. An appreciation of factors that influence AMF efficacy is needed to improve survival, competitiveness and effectiveness. For example, it is known that intracellular proliferation of AMF within roots is a highly regulated event (Bever et al. 1996). Therefore, a highly mycorrhizal host or control fungus may be more favorable for AMF and reproductive development than one that is not highly mycorrhizal (Ferdinand and Boyle 1998). ### 4.2.2 Superior Application Technology
Research shows that "priming" plants against pathogens using AMF (or fungi) helps plants by inducing a SIR response (Cordier et al. 1998). The inoculum may be applied to seeds, crops, or plantlets through tissue culture before being transplanted. Application of the agent prior to transplantation eliminates the need for costly "targeted placement," and increases the potential for AMF growth.

### 4.2.3 Improved Understanding of Microbial (AMF)
It is known that AMF functioning in natural ecosystems can be enhanced by various factors interactions with other organisms. However, specifics on the topic are lacking. Knowledge gained from studies addressing AMF effectiveness in a typical rhizosphere, community diversity and species richness containing extreme AMF levels, and the use of antagonizers is required for the development of effective AMF biocontrol.

### 4.2.4 Selection of Effective AMF
Bagaraj (1993) asserts that AMF species selection for a desired activity based on their ability for survival, aggressive colonization of host roots, and efficacy. Use of AMF species originally isolated from test host roots have proven advantageous for many plant species including agricultural crops, forest tree species and orchard crops.

## 5 CONCLUSIONS

Literature presents a variety of potential for AMF-mediated control of plant diseases. Although there are challenges in the noncolonizability of AMF and more limitations for agricultural crops, there is promise for non-seed crops, which are currently underdeveloped and underex. The AMF, increasing crop productivity using existing resources, avoiding resistance to chemicals, minimizing risk free from, and conforming to sustainable agricultural practices, offers more than plant disease control. In the future, mycorrhizosphere management must become one of the viable and environmentally friendly solutions for managing plant diseases and reducing pathogen inoculum.
