# Degradation of Hydrocarbons by Yeasts and Filamentous Fungi

John B. Sutherland National Center for Toxicological Research, U.S. Food and Drug Administration, Jefferson, Arkansas, USA

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Hydrocarbons are ubiquitous in the environment, but they are most abundant where coal and petroleum fuels are stored, processed, or burned (Tuhačková et al. 2001; Warashowski 1999). Because many hydrocarbons are toxic, their biological effects due to polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) in aluminum, coke, and steel has been linked to lung and bladder cancer (Mastangelo et al. 1996) and exposure to hydrocarbon fuels (Lindey 1992) has effects (Ritchie et al. 2011).

Mammalian metabolism of benzo[a]pyrene, the most important carcinogen in PAH mixtures, forms adduct with macromolecules and interferes with signaling pathways (Miller and Ramos 2001). PAHs are also volatile (Antiotogenic 1997).

The variety of yeasts and filamentous fungi to transform hydrocarbons suggests that they may be involved in the recycling of occurring hydrocarbons in the environment as seen in the degradation of lubricants (Lindey 1992). Their versatility in degrading hydrocarbons is due to their broad substrate specificity of (Cerniglia and Sutherland 2001).

Fungi are now being explored for the remediation of soils contaminated with hydrocarbons (Amelia Cerniglia 1999; 2001) and other species are used for the transformation of hydrocarbons to higher-value compounds (Trudgill 1994).

The recycling of hydrocarbons by fungi is probably common, usually unnoticed, in the environment. For instance, fungi are producing products in lakes (Wolf and Hanson 1979; 1980). Fungi that are pathogenic to insects can degrade the hydrocarbons found in the epic waxes of their hosts (Creso et al. 2000); Napolitano and June (1997). Many filamentous fungi and yeasts grow abundantly in soils, contaminated by petroleum residues (April et al. 1998; 2000; Okundayo and Obbecke 2009). Fungi degrade hydrocarbons in soils and lakes (Griffin and Cooney 1979; Romero et al. 2001) and in oil-polluted seawater (Cerniglia 1997; Jindarde et al. 1998). Filamentous fungi, especially Hormocinis Amphora resinae [the amazon of resin], may physically block aviation fuels when water is present (Parbery 1971). Yeasts are used for biodegradation at the interface with water and mycelium may physically block fuel lines of ships and aircraft (Lindey 1992). The ability to transform hydrocarbons is now being explored to clean up the environment. Several fungi are used to remove PAH from soil (Gerard et al. 2001; Liu et al. 1997) and yeasts are used to decompose residues in inulate (Nwachukwu 2000). Fungi have been used to inoculate soils to degrade hydrocarbons (Cerniglia and Sutherland 2001).

Many degraded hydrocarbons can be transformed to produce higher-value products. The yeasts can transform o- and b-pinene (Rama and Bhattacharyya 2006), 2-methylrene (Yamazaki et al. 1988), and limonene (Noma et al. 1992; de Oliveira and Strappasson 2000) as well as sesquiterpenes (Abraham et al. 1992; Miyazawa et al. 1995; 1998) have been used to transform metabolites.

(++)-limonene can be transformed in partial to alcohol, an anticancer drug (de Oliveira and Strappasson 2000). Ethyl-benzene and propylene are transformed stereoselectically to (+1)-phenethylalcohol and (+1)-phenylpropanol, which may be chiral building blocks for synthesis (Ueda et al. 2001). Some species are abundant in hydrocarbon deposits. The fungi that have been studied extensively for use in the transformation of hydrocarbons are the white-bodied myycetes Phanerochaete, Pleurotus, and the yeast Candida. Several fungi have been used in the bioremediation of soils contaminated by oil (Atlas and Cella 1995). Phanerochaete chrysosporium is a white rot degrading lignin (Pothuri et al. 1990; 1996). Another species, B. clostideo, oxidizes cyclohexylane (Daxelles et al. 1999). Aspergillus niger and A. niger both mobilize the β-methyl to dehydroxylated derivatives (Yamazaki et al. 1998). Another strain of this species oxidizes the PAHs of naphthalene, anthracene, and phenanthrene (Sack et al. 1990). Penicillium digitatum produces a cytochrome P450 that degrades benzo(a)pyrene (Venkateswari et al. 2001).
