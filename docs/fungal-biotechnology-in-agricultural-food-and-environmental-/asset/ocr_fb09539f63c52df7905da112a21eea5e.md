# ## INTRODUCTION

Explosive compounds are very significant environmental contaminants. Their production, use in conflict, and disposal has to contaminated soils, sediments, and water around the world are intrinsically toxic to microbes, fungi, animals, and man. In the United States, it is now a requirement that sites contaminated be remediated to acceptable site-specific goals (Jerger and Woodward 1990). Various remediation strategies have been adopted to date, and have involved physical, chemical, and biological approaches (Newcombe and Crawford 2002). Physical techniques such as incineration, excavation and filtration are effective for excavated contaminants. Chemical treatments have been tried, for example precipitation with using surfactants and solvent extraction (Kapurlan 1990) and although these approaches generate further residues that have to be disposed of. Biological treatments have included biodegradation by microorganisms and fungi as an exploration of degradation where microbial inoculum is added to the contaminated environment (Kapurlan 1990). Biological approaches appear to be cost effective although other methods. Success depends on microbial sensitivity to toxicity of explosive, toxic byproducts of incomplete degradation and the length of time biological cleanup of a contaminated environment can take (Randy 1995). This review seeks to summarize available information about the metabolic pathways involved in fungal degradation of explosives and to evaluate the effectiveness of these pathways across the taxonomic groups.

# ## 2 EXPLOSIVES AS SUBSTRATES FOR MICROBIAL DEGRADATION

# ### 2.1 2,4-6-Trinitrotoluene (TNT)

The nitro-aromatic compound 2,4-6-trinitrotoluene (TNT) is of greatest concern as an environmental contaminant (Figure 1a). It is intrinsically toxic to microorganisms as nitro-groups on the aromatic rings withdraw electron density and limit the activity of dioxygenase enzymes (Yin 1990; 2000). Moreover, TNT is poorly soluble in water (140 mg/l at 25°C), relatively stable and persistent in the environment (Gorontzy et al. 1994). Its partial breakdown products are hazardous, carcinogenic, and mutagenic, and may to produce cancer (Bennett 1994; Wang and Edwards 1984; Won et al. 1996).

Natural decomposition or result of bio-transformation and breakdown products include nitro-reduced amines (Yin Nitro). Nitro compounds such as TNT are rarely produced by Pseudomonas sp. via nitroreductase (Ohmori 1978) and oxygenase (Hahn et al. 1987). Streptomyces have been produced to produce phenolic, and benz derivatives by Fischer et al. (1996). Nitrophenol compounds such as aristocholic acid can be produced by plants (Williams and Barnett 1977) and this compound has been identified as a potent carcinogen, intercalating and forming adducts with DNA leading to oncogenesis (Arifi et al. 2000).
