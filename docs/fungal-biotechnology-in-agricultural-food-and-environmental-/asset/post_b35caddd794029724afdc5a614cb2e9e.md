## 3. MICROBIAL PRODUCTS AS FOOD INGREDIENTS

Major products used as feed additives are developed to complement the properties of other foods rather than as major sources. The techniques for yeast production are well established (Pepper and Stone 1976; Wahl 1992), most new developments deal with biomass treatments (Halaz and Lilly 1991). This will be covered in detail in this chapter.

### 3.1 Active Formulations

Categories adapted to maintain functional capacity are 'active dry yeast' (96.2) and 'yeast culture' (96.8) (Pepper 1983, Official Publication of Association of Feed Control Officials 2002). Use of protein concentrates and isolates in feed processing is recognized in many scientific papers to prove their nutritional properties. However, due to the high cost of protein isolates, the use of these formulations is limited to products, meat, and milk substitutes. They are produced by hydrolysis of protein to improve the physical properties (texturing properties) and nutritional value of food products under the definition of the FDA and Federal Regulations (2 CFR 101.22(3)).

Yeast autolysates and hydrolyates are used as natural flavors to enhance sausage, meat, and cheese manufacture (Halaz and Lilly 1991). Examples of these applications are the production of *Aspergillus* extracts by Diamond V as livestock feed, microbial extracts manufactured by Philip (http://www.beef.com) for use in different food systems, soups, marinades, and many other types. Lallemand, Lescafe, and Beldem produce a complete range of products for animal and human use. At this moment, the studies are mainly focused on new developments and more tailored formulations whose acceptance by the consumer will depend on their characteristics, although experience shows that the standard level fit below these limits. In products of fungal origins, chemical analysis of mycotoxins is considered (Scrimshaw 1985; Stringer 1985). Pathogenicity of the microorganism used for feeding has been evaluated by the injection of viable substance into the body cavity or fluids of mammals. In this way pathogenicity of a large number of microorganisms (*C. citria*, *U. minutus*, *M. oleaginosa*, Torulopsis) has been evaluated (Stringer 1985). The integrity of the original strain and absence of contaminants has to be proved by microbiological and immunological assays. Monitoring and control of the product properties (temperature, pH, aeration cell count) must be carefully monitored, and proof of such monitoring has to be presented. For example, the nucleic acids from microbial protein sources to the daily diet should not exceed 2g per day (Anonymous 1975; Scrimshaw 1985). This restriction does not apply to nonmammalian protein sources or other vertebrates (Stringer 1985). The required procedures for toxicity of microbial protein products are irrespective of whether the product is intended for human and feeding (Stringer 1985). The studies should include clinical and laboratory animal tests, with rodents and other species (e.g., broiler chicken and pig) and the species that will consume the product in its diet (Stringer 1985). The completion of a battery of biological studies including effects on growth, weight, health and clinical examination of the organs and tissues (Anonymous 1983a).

Tolerance studies in clinical trials. Tolerance levels are used to determine the acceptance and the frequency of allergy and other reactions. The content should be studied to identify potential intolerance, rash, and presence of other allergic reactions (Anonymous 1983b). The trials are conducted with a group of 50 to 100 individuals randomly assigned to groups, selected by sex, with careful report of all significant effects such as mood, appetite, or sleep patterns, monitored over a period of two-week study per series (Scrimshaw).

## SAFETY AND QUALITY OF MICROBIAL PRODUCTS

The safety and quality of microbial products has to be strictly controlled as in all foods. The concepts underlying the safety and quality of proteins were developed between 1970 and 1974 by the Protein Advisory Group of the United Nations (P), initiated by established quality standards. During the 70s the experience was accumulating with an increase of preparations of microbial protein with more extensive trials in animal foods and preclinical and clinical testing (Scrimshaw 1985). The main concepts consolidated in these guidelines (Anonymous 1975, 1983a) concerning the safety and quality control of microbial products are:

- (1) GRAS organisms. Generally recognized as safe (GRAS) must be judged by qualified experts based on scientific data which confirm absence of toxic compounds and non-pathogenicity, and in the case of a substance used before January 1, 1958, based on experience or use in food (Code of Federal Regulations, 21 CFR 101.30, 182, and 184).
- (2) No living cells derived from the fermentation source (original strain or contaminants) can be present in the final product. Medium components that may be health hazards must also be absent. If antioxidants, detergents, or preservatives introduced in the fermentation process to comply with minimum safety requirements must be removed completely.
