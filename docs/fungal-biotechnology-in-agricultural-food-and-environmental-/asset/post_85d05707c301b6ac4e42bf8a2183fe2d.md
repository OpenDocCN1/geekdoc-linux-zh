### 3.1 Extraction

The first step in metabolite profiling is to obtain the metabolites produced by the fungi growing on some substrate, see Section 2.2 and Table 1. Depending on the group of metabolites of interest, two main chains can be used to collect metabolites for a profile:
(a) an extraction approach used for volatile metabolites illustrated in Figure 3, in combination with HPLC/MS and 
(b) a headspace approach is used for the volatile metabolites illustrated in Figure 4, in combination with GC–MS.

Nearly all sample preparations start with either extraction based on distribution of the analyte between two immiscible phases or by a gas phase sampling. The former is the fungus (biomass) and/or growth medium, the latter solvent or a purge gas. Many physical (or mechanical) assistance may be needed to help the rapid distribution of metabolites between the two phases. There may be challenges in the compound of the two different approaches, e.g, some of the terpenes may be found in both extracts and by headspace analysis. The key is to select extraction methods for metabolites of interest with a high affinity. Adjusting pH can enhance the solubility of metabolites in the solvent. Furthermore, choice of solvent can also be used to favor extraction of a particular subgroup, and a selection of several solvents can be used to extend the range of metabolites.

Many extraction protocols use grams or milliliters of solvents, however these procedures are minimized due to the high sensitivity and selectivity of modern instrumentation. A simple method is the plug extraction method (Smeegaard, 1997) illustrated in Figure 3, where a few 6 mm plugs (0.5–1 cm²) are cut from the plates and the fungi are extracted with about 500 µl of solvent. The extraction is performed ultimately to improve efficiency and speed. If the extract is compatible with the subsequent analysis it can be injected directly; otherwise the solvent has to be evaporated and redolved in an appropriate solvent (Figure 3). Other analytical applications require a more elaborate sample preparation if GC–MS is required.

Sampling the volatile metabolites from fungi culture can be done by two approaches: either by one of different headspace (HS) methods using direct collection of volatiles in the gas phase above the growing fungi or by extraction of volatiles in the fungal biomass (sample). The chromatogram [e.g., plot of absorbance vs time] shows peaks representing compounds eluting from the column. This technique requires that the compounds have a chromophore light at the selected wavelength and that the solvent is transparent (or at least does not have any absorption). Fluorescence is measured with a selective pair of excitation/emission wavelengths in a similar fashion, if the compounds have a fluorescent, whereas others can be identified by refractive index detection.

Identification of fungi can be achieved by identifying specific metabolites produced, which requires reliable methods with high confidence, and usually requires either an authentic standard of spectral and chromatographic techniques.

### 3. HPLC

HPLC is by far the most analytical technique used for metabolite profiling by its sensitivity, relative ease of operation, and breadth of spectra that can be determined basically. HPLC is an integration of separation and detection system with separation in a liquid phase.

Metabolite profiling is almost always done using gradient elution on reversed phase material (C18) or polar phase. The mobile phase is water-acetonitrile or water containing some modifier, e.g, triluoroacetic acid (TFA). The flow rates used for profiling depend on the column size, generally between 2 and 4 mL/min. Smaller diameter column gives a better separation at cost of lower sensitivity, as a smaller amount of sample can be injected. This section of HPLC profiling metabolites can be detected by identification of specific metabolites or by the full chromatogram as a profile. In both cases it is important to analyze the analytical conditions as constant. In the first case, identification of metabolites is determined by the retention time and spectra with together. Possible spectral detection is clear. The effect of small wavelength, unnoticed in retention time can be overcome by using a series of alkyl alcohols to calculate the retention index of the chromatograms (Nielsen and Thane 1987). If the profiles are used to set constant conditions which would reduce the alignment needed (Nielsen et al. 1998).

Detection by a UV-detector (or PDA) is commonly used as these techniques easily enhance the information provided by structural information about each compound. In HPLC-UV detection, UV is collected at various lengths so that 10-20 wavelengths are collected as a chromatogram. The result is illustrated in Figure 5 and it is the standard method to fully exploit the data of a full HPLC-UV chromatogram. The UV traces obtained at selected wavelengths and a UV profile are presented at the same time which can be visualized as a chemical image of the sample and can be used for fungal identification (Nielsen et al. 1998; 1999; Thane et al.).

Figure 5 shows that different chromatograms of the same sample appear at different wavelengths. These wavelengths can be used to enhance the chromatographic resolution and to find specific metabolites, e.g, is at 18.60 on the 400 nm trace in Figure 5. These traces are all profiles which can be used in combination for identification or for detection. Quantitative detection is based on Beers law is for quantifying standards and compounds for each but it is not necessary to use the same wavelength for different compounds.

Identification or characterization much achieved from UV spectra if the fungus has a chromophore (body structure type particular to electrons systems), e.g, xanthogenic, which is a chromophore having absorption maximum near 410 nm. This is fortunately for many fungal metabolites but not for all (Cole and Cox 1998). The use of retention index and a chromatographic UV spectrum can be reliable for identification of many fungal metabolites (Thane and Thane 1993), however no standards are needed for confirmation. The use of UV-spectra in combination with other detectors, e.g., FLD can greatly enhance the sensitivity.
