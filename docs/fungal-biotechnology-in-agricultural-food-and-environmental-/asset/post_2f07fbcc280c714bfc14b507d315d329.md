# 3 Isozyme Analysis in Fungal Taxonomy, Genetics, and Population Biology

Stephen B. Goodwin
U.S. Department of Agriculture—Agricultural Research Service

# 1 INTRODUCTION

Isozyme analysis is a powerful technique for assaying genetic variation within populations. This versatile approach has been used on bacteria, fungi, plants, and many major groups of animals, from insects and other invertebrates to fish and mammals. The appeal of isenzyme analysis extends from its use, low cost, and speed to significantly lower cost compared to other techniques.

Isozyme analysis has a history in the fungi. Differences between major protein patterns were first noted during the early 1960s (Chang et al. 1962; Clare 1963). This approach developed into isenzyme analysis with the application of methods for detection of enzyme activities. Since mid-1960s this technique has been used in fungal taxonomy, protein biology, and species identification (Micales et al. 1982) but it is only over the past 15 years that the true potential of the technique has been realized.

The aim of this chapter is to introduce the concept of isenzyme analysis in fungi. Strategies for developing new isenzyme systems and for addressing particular questions about fungal taxonomy, genetics, and population biology will be discussed. Particular emphasis will be placed on identity profiles and patterns through successful implementation of new isenzyme systems, especially banding patterns, preparation of tissue samples, and methods and approaches to data analysis. Specific examples of isenzyme analysis for the major groups of fungi will be discussed to illustrate the many potential applications of this extremely useful and powerful technique.

# 2 WHAT IS AN ISOZYME?

Isozymes are different forms of a single enzyme that perform the same or similar function. Each form has a small change that allows it to be distinguished from other forms (isozymes) of the same enzyme. The changes usually result from mutations in the DNA that cause single amino-substitutions into the protein making the enzyme with mobility in an electric field. Such changes often can be detected by electrophoresis, and this forms the basis for iszyme analysis.

The "isenzyme" and "allele" are often used interchangeably and can be a source of confusion. However, the terms are not identical. Isozymes that have been genetically analyzed and can be alleles at a single genetic locus can be called allozymes. Therefore, all alleles are allozymes, but not all allozymes. isoenzyme is a general term and will be used when the genetic basis of the different enzyme forms are known for certain.

The basic approach is to obtain extracts of electrophoresis of total proteins, separate the proteins (usually a gel), that visualize the enzymes by some type of solid matrix, by use of chemicals that produce a visual reaction to specific enzyme activity. Virtually, any type of tissue can be used for isenzyme analysis. This can include spores, mycelia, or roots. The main requirement is that the tissue must be living.
