# 1 INTRODUCTION

Microorganisms are ubiquitously present in terrestrial ecosystems from which they are disseminated and contaminate plant communities. The ripening seed is no exception and is contaminated by a wide range of bacteria, yeasts, and fungi via the air, insects, rain splash, equipment, and agronomic practices. When seeds/grains are harvested they carry a wide range of microbial contaminants. Postharvest environmental factors determine the impact they may have on the seeds, including microbiology. It is important to remember that the harvested seeds/grains microorganisms are alive and spoiling slowly under dry, safe storage conditions.

Postharvest management can lead to rapid deterioration in quality and severely decrease microbial and nutritional value of the seeds. Microbial activity can also cause undesirable effects in grains including discoloration, utilization of carbohydrates as energy source, degrade lipids and proteins or alter digestibility, volatile metabolites giving off-odors, loss of germination and binding and making quality, as unfit as animal or human feed exhaustion (Christensen 1977). The spores of fungi can also cause respiratory diseases hazards to workers (Lacey and Crook 1988).

Extensive losses of seeds, especially cereal grains in store from all cases varies widely. They may amount to 10% worldwide (Anton 1979) but can reach 50% in tropical regions (Hall 1970), Vassan (1980) estimated losses of high moisture paddy in southern India exceeded 15–25% in only 9 days, with Rohani et al. (1985) found storage losses of paddy in West Malaysia of 1%.

## 2.1 Water Availability—The Concept

The extent to which seeds and grain are susceptible to spoilage depend, above all, on the presence of water in the system. Many authors have written about the fundamental requirement for water in microbial growth (Cooke and Whipps 1993; Griffin 1981; Scott 1957). Most stored products such as seeds and grain are hygroscopic in nature, i.e., they will take up or release moisture until they reach a dynamic equilibrium with the surrounding environment. It is straightforward to express this water in terms of the percentage moisture content, based on the dry weight to the weight (expressed on wet or dry basis), which implies that the stored material consists of dry matter plus a certain amount of “attached” liquid. In fact the situation is more complex than this. This is principally because the water in these substrates is not all equivalent, but held in a number of different “states”. Broadly speaking three means of water can be identified in hygroscopic material: (a) constituent water, water that is chemically linked to the substrate and forms part of its structural makeup, (b) adsorbed water, which is closely linked with the substrate by surface, and (c) free water, which is loosely associated with the substrate surface (Pixton 1967). In practice, there is probably no clear demarcation between these states and water type will depend on the moisture content of the substrate. The material may become more weakly bound as further layers of water accumulate on the surface (as the substrate becomes saturated), since the “available” water is the one that is most available for microbial growth, although its availability will be influenced by the presence of solutes. The implication here is that microorganisms respond to water content; therefore, the concept of water availability is of importance in relation to microbial growth and stability in the thermodynamics of systems, and considers the movement of water along gradients of potential energy. The term “water potential” is usually an abbreviation of “potential energy of water in a system relative to the free energy of pure water with a specified mass, or measured in MPa (Pa Papendack and Mullba 1985). The potential of free water is zero; while “constrained” in a system, the adsorbed (or less adsorbed) water is therefore, at a lower (negative) water potential, and any microorganism will increase the lower water potential to make water available. The numerical value of water potential may be related to water availability using the formula: $\Phi = (RT/V_w)\ln\alpha_w$ (Papendack and Mullba 1985).

$R$ is gas constant, $T$ is absolute temperature (K), and $V_w$ is molecular weight of water. $\alpha_w$ and $\alpha$ are nevertheless, however, measurement of the substrate contents that make up water potential cannot be carried out using water measurement, unless a sufficiently sensitive parameter.

### 2.1.1 Fundamental Principles

Water availability can be expressed in many ways, one of the most convenient being activity ($a_w$). The free water in a system is the proportion of the total water that is immediately available to reach equilibrium with the substrate. This will be reflected in the equilibrium vapor pressure or equilibrium relative humidity (ERH) exerted by the system. Water activity is defined as the ratio of the vapor pressure of water to that of pure water at the same temperature and pressure (Scott 1957). It is therefore, directly linked to the ERH by the: $a_w = p/p = ERH\%/100$ (Labuja 1974).

Water capacity, then, to be seen as a measure of the availability of water to evaporate from a substrate and humid environment, and is measured in the range of 0.- to 1.0 representing the percentage of water. It should be noted that $a_w$ depends on substrate content and temperature, and for a given substrate $a_w$ will increase with increasing moisture. This is the consequence of the general increase in moisture transport (Multon 1988).

### 2.1.4 Moisture Sorption Isotherms

The relationship between water content and $a_w$ in a given substrate may be expressed by moisture sorption isotherms. These are curves plotted by plotting water content against activity, or ERH at constant temperature (hence, the ‘isotherm’). They are specific to species and content but can generally show a sharp rise in the curve above 80% ERH. The importance of moisture sorption isotherms is that they establish the relationship between moisture content and water availability for specific products; and, therefore allow water availability to set safe levels from knowledge of moisture content.
