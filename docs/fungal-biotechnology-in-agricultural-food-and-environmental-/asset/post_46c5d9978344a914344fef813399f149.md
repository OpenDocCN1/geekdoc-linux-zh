## 4 BIOGENESIS

Ergot alkaloids are derived from tryptophan, mevalonic acid, and methiobine (Birt 1960; Groger et al. 1960). The origin of the ergoline part from tryptophan and dimethyllysergophosphate was established at an enzymatic level. The free biosynthesis of clavine and II, and lyssynclamine from precursors was catalyzed by Skall and Cavender and Anderson (1970). Tryptophan has also been found to be a factor in the induction and expression of enzymes catalyzing alkaloid formation (Krupinski et al. 1976). The first enzyme of alkaloid biosynthesis is dimethyltryptophan synthase (DMAT) shown by Tsai (1971) and the encoding gene was cloned from *C. fumiformis* and *C. purpurea* (Tsai 1995; Tudzensky et al. 1999). Recent data of Tudzensky et al. (2001) show that in *C. purpurea*, all involved alkaloid biosynthesis are regulated by feedback inhibition and the negative role of phosphate in the induction of enzymes catalyzing alkaloid synthesis (Krupinski et al. 1976) and on the inhibition of deamination of dimethyltryptophan by the addition of phosphate in the culture medium (Mehta 1984; Pazoninski et al. 1983). For gene for peptide synthesis homologous to that of *C. purpurea* in Neophytium species (Annis and Panacione 1998; Pazoninski et al. 1999) was reported.

The simple derivatives of lysergic acid and lysergic acid (including lysergic acid amide, shown in Figure 3) are either a small peptide or a simple alkylamide. Amides of lysergic and parasitic acids found in ergot alkaloids are ergometrine, lysergic acid 2-hydroxyethylamide, lysergic acid amide (ergine), parasitic acid, and 10-hydroxy-pasculamide (Flieger et al. 1993). Ergopeptides are derivatives of lysergic acid and are the only sclerotic phase or both (Mehta 1984).

### 5.2 Saprophytic Cultivation

#### 5.2.1 History

The first saprophytic cultivation of Claviceps on artificial nutrient media dates back to 1922 (Borys 1922). The first attempt for industrial production was the isolation of clav alkaloids from submerged *Claviceps spp.* (Abe and Yamamoto 1954). Later, for saprophytic production of simple derivatives of lysergic acid was developed using different strains of *C.a* (Abe et al. 1955). It took only 5 years to isolate *C. purpurea* to produce ergotamine (Amici et al. 1966). Since all types of ergot alkaloids for direct use as therapeutic agents or precursors for semisynthetic drugs can be obtained by formation.

#### 5.2.2 Strain

With other processes the key to successful production of ergot alkaloids is in obtaining the proper strain of fungus. There are three methods used for preparing Claviceps:
(a) Plating of mycelium tissue from the surface of decayed *Claviceps* fungus (Janardhan and Husan 1984; Pazoutova et al. 2002), and (b) Trapping of sexual associates ejected from fruiting bodies on sclerotia. (c) Monoscopic culture can be obtained (Vasarhely et al. 1980).

#### 5.2.3 Strain Improvement

The classical methods (selection pressure, mutagenesis, and recombination) used for strain improvement are, to some extent, more complicated with Claviceps due to incomplete information on cell nature (Dibek-Hruce et al. 1991; Mantle and Nisbe 1976). Recently it was found that chromosomes in *C. purpurea* are volatile so as well as mycelium may be affected by mutagenesis. Mutagen followed by selection is important for increasing the yield of alkaloid (Suzuki et al. 1987). An ergotrine producing *C. purpurea* showed 180-fold increase in alkaloid production after step mutation with different mutants (Kobel and Singh 1970). The results of sporulation and meiosis frequency the protoplasts formed from selected strains were used (Olave et al. 1982; Zalai et al. 1990).
