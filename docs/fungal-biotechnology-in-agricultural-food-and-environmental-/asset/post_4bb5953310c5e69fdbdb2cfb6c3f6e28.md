
# 5 CASE—1—DECOLORIZATION OF AN INDUSTRY

## 5.1 The Composition of the Waste

The initial problem was to decolorize the complex water from an industrial site. The waste originated in a plant and indicated that it contained low molecular weight (acyl) polyester esters derived from reaction of triglycerides with aliphatic anhydride, 비롯, amino acids and formaldehyde. Lesser quantities of higher molecular weight, from polymerization of vinyl, and methacrylate monomers were also present. The effluent had a total organic carbon content (TOC) of 270 mg/l and a total suspended solid (TSS) of 749 mg/l.

The strain of P. cinnamabaricus was inoculated into a nylon web cube support (0.5 cm³ of Scotchbry). The effluent was prepared in a defined medium (Tien and Kirk 1988) which was modified by increasing the concentration of diammonium sulphate to 0.8 g/l to a carbon to nitrogen ratio of 38:1. It stimulated decolorization; however alcohol (40 mM) was omitted in this trial because it retarded growth, inhibited glucose and nitrogen utilization and delayed decolorization. Addition of veratryl alcohol might interfere with metabolism (Tonon and Order 1988).

The medium was circulated through the reactor at 0.45 l/h and samples were taken daily and analyzed for decolorization, available nitrogen, and oxidative activity at this stage was termed peroxidase. A control reactor regulating the effluent was also used.

## 5.2 Decolorizing Organisms

In view of the numerous results of involvement in dye decolorization and lignin degradation (Sections 2 and 3) the white-rot fungi were considered the organisms of choice for this work. It was argued that the organism could either grow on or metabolize the effluent. Plates of malt extract (MEA) were prepared and the effluent diluted to contain 65, 1389, 2083, and 2270 mg/TOC, respectively. The fungi were first collected in western Victoria, Australia, and some other states. The fungi were grown under high humidity and examined daily.

The most appropriate organism was judged to be a test strain of P. cinnamabaricus according to Fuhring (1985) and Marshall and Western (1979). In the presence of a primary carbon source (and a TOC of 1385 mg/l) this fungus covered the effluent in 96 h. The TOC concentration was also used in bioreactor experiments.

## 5.3 Conditions in a Paed Bioreactor

Most degradation studies have been carried out in solid cultures or in low agitation or shaker cultures (Anders and Eriksson 1977; Hataka 1985). Paed bioreactor configurations have been used to study the ligninulosic and degradative properties of P. chrysosporium (Lewandowski et al. 1990; Linko 1988). In view of this it was decided to examine the effect of the fungus on the industrial waste in a 21-packed reactor.

## 5.4 Decolorization in the Reactor

The differences in the spectra at stage of decolorization of the effluent and some of these are shown in Figure 1. In this experiment decolorizing three stages were observed. Firstly, some of the colors adsorb to the biomass of veratryl alcohol. Secondly, the enzyme acts to degrade soluble components of the effluent and then the peroxidase on the adsorbed color of the mycelium in situ. Evidence that the peroxidase was most likely a cause is reported in Section 6.

## 6.1 Structure of the Dye

Chicago Sky Blue (2) is a dye substance used in dyeing cotton and cellulosic fiber. It is often incorporated into inks, for dyeing of leather and paper. It belongs to the largest class of commercially produced water-soluble dyes that are characterized by three azo links which link phenyl and naphthyl groups containing combinations of functional groups (sulfonic acid, salt; chloro; hydroxyl; methyl and nitro) on the ring.

Decolorization and degradation of azo dyes have been demonstrated by P. chrysosporium (Cripps et al. 1990; Ollikka et al. 1993; Pasti-Grisby et al. 1992; Paczynski et al. 1997; Spadaro et al. 1996). The mechanism of this organism characterizes the degradation of azo, hetero-cyclic, triphenylamine, and polymeric dyes of azo.

Much less is known about the laccases in the degradation of synthetic dyes, although several rotting fungi produce laccase as well and peroxidases have shown to colorize a range of different structures (Krapp et al. 1995). A mechanism for degradation of phenol dyes (Chivuka and Rangan 1995) is similar to the one proposed for degradation of phenol dyes by lignin (Chivuka et al. 1995). In the later case formation of novel sulfhenyl hydroperoxides was postulated and the elimination of the azo linkage as molecular nitrogen. In the case of lactose, electron-rich phenolic rings adjacent to the azo link appeared necessary for oxidation and radical formation. Mass spectral (MS) and electpray ionization (EI-MS) analyses confirmed the release of azo linkages as molecular nitrogen.

## 6.2 Decolorization of CSB Using P cinnamabaricus

The isolate of P. cinnamabaricus was examined for its ability to decolorize CSB on plates, initially in shake flasks and subsequently in 1 L packed-bed bioreactors. Every the dye was decolorized at relatively high concentrations (500 mg/l).

## 6.3 Enzymes Involved in the Degradation

The decolorization of the dye by P. cinnamabaricus led to a search for the responsible enzymes. In the experiments, solutions had been tested in the presence of catalase as Aspergillus niger. This involvement of laccase in the degradation reaction and a search was undertaken to see whether it acted alone or required other mediators.

## 6.4 Production and Purification of Laccase from P cinnamabaricus

In the 10 L-bed, P. cinnamabaricus used glucose at a constant rate. The ammonia content of the medium was exhausted by day 4. Laccase activity was first detected on day 4 and had reached 0.28 U/ml by day 10 when the culture broth was harvested for laccase purification. The isolation procedure follows that of: 9 ml of fluid in ultracentrifugation by centrifugation, and column chromatography on Sephadex 75. Fractions at 614 nm were analyzed for laccase activity, pooled, dialyzed against 20 mM histidine buffer pH 6, and concentrated. The concentrate was chromatographed on Mono anion exchange column with 5 mM NaCl and new peaks at 614 nm appeared and stood at 8 nm in the presence of protease inhibitor. A 45-fold increase in specific activity was observed during capillary electrophoresis purity greater than 95% with minor peaks (areas 7.9 and 4.96%) at longer elution. It was irreversibly inhibited by 0.1 mM sodium azide and ascorbic acid at 1 mM.

## 6.5 Characterization

The enzyme contained 510 amino acid residues and was a single polypeptide of molecular size of 63 kDa. It contained 11% carbohydrate that is consistent with other lases (Coll et al 1993). The first 10 amino acids at the N-terminal were A, G, V, A, B, L, T, and D differed from the enzyme from P. cinnababar as isolated from Australia (Eggert et al 1996). It is also comparable to the lase of P. ostreae (Bourbnais et al 1995), and T. reticulatus (Bourbnais et al 1996).

In degradation, the absorbance at 618 nm (25 mg/mL) was monitored after heat treated. The rate of decolorization was linear (0.28–25 µ/min), and protein concentration equilibrium was reached after 2 h. The major peak at 618 nm and at 32 min disappeared and new peaks appeared with retention times of CBS. The day had retention time of 22 min on HPLC C14 (reversed phase column) (Coll et al 2000). After 2 day the retention 22 min disappeared and new peaks appeared with retention times of 1.28 and 3.96 min, respectively. decolorization magnitude lower than CBS has been clearly degraded by the activity of the lase. The products were not characterized so far.

A major band and two bands were observed at pH 3 and 2.65, respectively, isometric focusing (IEF). Each band stained the lase substrate glucose (10 mM). One band, according to lase activity, was observed on electrophoresis (4% gradient nondaturing conditions). This band, stained with 10 mM guaiacol, coincided with the co-electrophoretic protein band.
