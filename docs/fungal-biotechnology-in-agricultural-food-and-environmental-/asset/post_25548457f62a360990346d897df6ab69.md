## 4.3 Biocontrol of Powdery Mildew

Powdery mildew fungi are parasites of plants that draw nutrients and water from their host, reducing growth and yield through the action of photosynthesis. The fungus penetrates the epidermal cells and establishes a relationship with the plant host causing the formation of haustoria nutrient-absorbing structures. Mycelial growth and sporulation on the surface of leaves and stems, resulting in a fuzzy mildew appearance.

Over years, powdery diseases have been managed through the use of chemical fungicides and genetic resistance, but reports have highlighted the potential of biological control methods. Yeast fungal agents have been shown to reduce sporulation and growth of mildew, minimizing their damaging effects on hosts. The fungal biocontrol agents are mostly mycoparasites, while the yeasts produce antibiotics and enzymes that help control mildew hypha and to collapse and be rendered invisible.

Verticillium has been described as a mycoparasite of powdery mildew fungi as an agent of plants and has been developed as a control agent on cucumber crops V. lecani in level of antagonism against the powdery mildew fungus of cucumber Sphumerpora laboratory conditions (Askay et al. 1998). Application to cucumbers reduces prior infection and increases humidity under high (> 95%) reduced disease severity (Verhar et al.). The high humidity required for growth of this mildew was reduced by the addition of an oral formulation (Verhar et al. 1999). Infection of S. fuliginea by V. lecani resulted in disorganized cytoplasm and possible colonization due to enzyme activity

Another mycoparasite, Ampelomyces quisalis, has been extensively studied as a biocontrol agent of powdery mildew of cucumber. The mycoparasite inhibits the mildew fungus and pycnidia in association with associated mycelium, reducing growth and sporulation of the pathogen. Cells of the mycop grow inside the mildew hyphae, gradually causing them to degrade. High levels of β-1,3-glucanase activity were reported in A. quisialis (Rotem et al. 1999) and exposure of hyphae to the enzymes caused them to degrade.

A commercially viable formulation of A. quisialis AQ10 has extensively used against powdery mildew. On cucumbers grown in the greenhouse, AQ10 was very effective in reducing mildew (Elad et al. 1998). On field-grown cucurbits, AQ10 also suppressed mildew development and increased yield compared to untreated plants (McGrath and Shishhoff 1999). Molecular techniques have been used to characterize strains of A. quisialis. Sequence analysis of the nuclear ITS region among a worldwide collection of isolates revealed considerable intraspecific variation (Kiss and Nakaisen 1997). Isolates of the same genetic background were found in widely different areas and genetically different isolates could be found in a given area.

### 4.3.2 Yeasts As Biological Control Agents of Powdery Mildew

Sporothrix floculosa is a yeast-like fungus with demonstrated biocontrol activity against mildew. Biochemical investigations have shown that the yeast induces collapse of mildew spores and hyphal cells. Extracellular acids with antifungal properties were produced by P. flocculosa and reported to be the principle mode of action in biological activity against mildew (Benayag 1999), disrupting the cytoplasmic membrane in a range of fungi (Benayag and 2001). A fungus-acid-resistant strain with chemical control methods could have been used in conjunction to reduce mildew development (Benayag and 1995). In a comparative study of three biological control agents, powdery mildew of cucumber was shown that P. flocculosa has disease control (Dik et al. 1998). Electron microscopic studies have revealed mildew hyphae and spores after treatment.

Molecular methods have been used to identify strains of Pseudomonas. Ribosomal DNA sequences and amplified primers were used to distinguish among different strains of this species and to develop isolate-specific markers to monitor and confirm genetic fidelity of the strains. A strain of P. fluorescens was formed commercially under the name Pseudomonas for use in control of powdery mildew on many crops grown in greenhouse conditions. Species of Tilletopsis are yeast-like fungi that occur on the leaf surface of various plants and have been demonstrated to have biological control activity against mildew diseases (Hijwegen 1992; Knudsen and Skou 1993; Urquart et al. 1994).

## CONCLUSIONS

The numerous reports of the utility of fungal and yeast biological control agents to reduce fungal diseases on vegetable crops illustrate the potential of this approach for disease management. In addition, the applications of techniques in biotechnology are providing numerous examples of effective biological control agents isolated and monitored in more depth. However, there are unique requirements in working with microbial control agents that must be recognized if this approach to disease control is to be successful.
