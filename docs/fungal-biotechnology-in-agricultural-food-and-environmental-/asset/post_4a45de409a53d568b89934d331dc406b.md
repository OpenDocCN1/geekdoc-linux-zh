# 1 HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

Microorganisms have long been used in the elaboration of foods for human consumption. Practically, every civilization has developed fermentation processes of one or other as the bases of their culinary traditions. However, the culture of food transforms, came rather than the advent of industrial fermentation, political and economic isolation. The first recognition of the value of surplus yeast as a feeding supplement for animals by Max Delbrück (1910) was rapidly followed by the production of food yeast during the First World War. The novel microbial foods have appealed to the conservative palate, but this was outweighed by the logistical advantage of a high productivity of aerobic fermentations in compact facilities. This offered variety many military strategies to draw plans to produce large amounts of food during the two World Wars, as well as the opening of worldwide hostilities in the second half of the twentieth century could have meant an interest in microbial protein production for food. However, preoccupations regarding importation in third world economic isolation, in the case of the Soviet Union and China, made the scope of microbial fermentations as a practical solution for the production of food, at least in emergency situations.

Special developments in the field of agriculture, later accompanied by important changes in international relations leading to the opening of the world food markets, overshadowed the worries of limited foods which rapid development rendered mass microbial food production competitive against traditional agricultural food crops, which became readily available at comparatively lower prices. This also led to a progressive decline in Single Cell Protein research studies in the literature (Ugalde and Castro 2002).

Notwithstanding this decline, biomass production technology nudged away from microbial protein as bulk food, towards new specialties, leading to a new revitalization in the field (Figure 1). This company produces fungal from Fusarium venenatum (formerly F. venenatum). The resulting product is manufactured to create a texture and taste that are reminiscent of meat products, creating a market as a alternative for vegetarian formulations.
