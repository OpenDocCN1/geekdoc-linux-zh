## The Application of Molecular Markers in the Epidemiology of Plant Pathogenic Fungi

Paul D. Bridge* Birkbeck College, University of London, London, and Royal Botanic Gardens Kew, Surrey, United Kingdom

Tanuja Singh / Dilip K. Arora* Banaras Hindu University, Varanasi, India.

## INTRODUCTION

Fungi are present in a variety of forms, in almost every habitat, where they are specific in their occurrence on particular types of host (or substrate) and ecological niche. Fungi may also become pathogenic with higher plants and enter complex biological relationships with the host (Clay and Koch 1996; Thrall and Burden 1997). The term pathogen is defined as "a fungus able to cause disease in a host or range of all plants (Host, 2001), and in this definition of pathogenic fungi will be limited to those that cause diseases of living plants, and therefore not include the fungi in wood cells or decaying matter." The term "pathogenic fungi" consist of a large group of fungi and species from diverse areas of the fungal kingdom. Recent developments in the development of evolutionary genomics have meant that a number of important plant pathogenic organisms are well identified, and some include fungi sensu stricto. These include genera of Phytophthora, Phytium, and Oomycetes, that are now placed in the Stramenila (Dick 2001). Pathogenic fungi play a significant role in crop productivity. Devastating fungal diseases such as corn smut, potato blight, and black and stem of wheat can destroy many economically important crops. This situation becomes more critical when the interaction between pathogen and the host invokes relatively long periods of intimate interaction without apparent damage to the host, and where the pathogen can persist in hosts for many years (Stanooz et al. 1997). The effects of fungi on living plants vary considerably. At one extreme, damage may be limited to small lesions on leaves [e.g., caused by some Alternaria species, see Ellis (1968)], while the other extreme may be rapidly killed [e.g., by some Vertices (1984)].

Much has been done to control fungal disease through selection and breeding programs, the genetic modification of both host and pathogen, and the introduction of resistant varieties (see Stutely and Kraus 1994). The success of these depends largely on the genetic variety in the fungal population and monitoring this variety. Many aspects of the biology of fungi have important considerations at the population level. This applies to mode of reproduction (including, sexual and asexual and dispersal mechanisms), and to anastomosis between genetically different individuals (Brass 1991; Kuldau et al. 1996). A further consideration is that some fungi are predominantly haploid in their reproductive stage but diploid, and some are asexual. In the case of pathogenic fungi, genetic variability can be introduced through a variety of mechanisms, either sexual reproduction or independently of it (Kistler and Miao 1992). Such variability is significant as it influence the host-pathogen interaction with flexibility allows the fungi to adapt to changing environmental conditions, including the introduction of new host genotypes.

Understanding the epidemiology of pathogenic fungi depends upon the ability to unambiguously identify sexually produced individuals and asexual clones. Classification traits, as morphological, physiological, and disease characteristics, lacked the required to identify identical individuals within a population and has prevented population studies for many years. Recent developments in molecular techniques now allow population studies on pathogenic fungi, which can be performed with great accuracy. The analysis of polymorphic loci allows for the direct assessment of genetic variation in a given population. Application of molecular markers has also allowed the investigation of evolutionary processes in a natural context (Muller and Brasier 1994; Milgrom et al. 1996; Vallen and Cuban 1991; Walagis and Cubeta 1994) and the number and scope of these studies is rapidly increasing.

In addition to knowledge of the modes of transmission of a pathogen, it is also important to determine when and where it is transmitted in terms of fungal populations. In cases a single fungal species may consist of a number of different host populations. An example of this is the wheat pathogen F. oxysporum, where around 170 different forms (referred to as species) have been identified. Each of these forms shows specificity to different hosts, and F. oxysporum will cause damage on wheat but would not have been expected to cause significant damage on oil. In order to monitor what fungi are present, and pose a risk to a crop, it is necessary to identify pathogens. Identification becomes complicated if there are subpopulations within the pathogen that show differential pathogenicity, in terms of degree of damage or particular cultivars. Cultivated populations are generally described as having differentiation to a significant difference to their crop. A further factor in considering populations in plant pathogens is whether the comprises of mimetic or mitotic forms. Pathogenic fungi, such as Fusarium species, occur exclusively in imperfect or mitotic forms. The variability within the population can be as relatively low, if the disease is of a single introduction. Some fungi, such as Phytophthora and rusts, however present in perfect and imperfect forms, and this allows for variability to be introduced at each generation [as seen Duncan et al. (1998)].

## REASONS FOR DETERMINING Epidemiology

As is the case for human and animal diseases, the epidemiology of plant disease can provide information for its treatment and control, and can lead to the development of breeding systems for resistance (Sachs and Stolorsky 1970). In the case of plant diseases, particularly those affecting crops, the two main areas that need to be determined are the mechanism of spread of the disease, and specificity and host range of the infecting fungi. Are transmitted to plants by many mechanisms. Many fungi spread through the soil, which may come from spores and other sources. Others can be transmitted directly or through water droplets. Pathways may also exist on secondary hosts, such as Fusarium oxytorum (Armstrong and Armstrong 1958) or on seeds [see Terry and Parker 2001]. If these forms are not present in a crop, they may act as reservoirs that allow a crop to be carried over between seasons. Insects and other vectors, such as Cladosospora gramicola (Shetty 1980) are in relation to infected plants, and cause damage in subsequent generations. The mode of transmission may be important in the development of control or treatment strategies. Such knowledge is particularly important in the selection of planting material, the preparation and maintenance of planting areas, and the establishment of crops successions and rotation [as seen Mause 1999].

## 3 TYPES OF MOLECULAR MARKERS

Detection of fungi by the visual examination of morphology is highly specific and species-specific identification of fungi species is difficult. Molecular techniques present several advantages over the traditional ones, and more importantly, nucleic acid sequences are used to identify them. They do not rely on phenotypic identification and is much faster. The molecular markers can be derived from both universal and conserved regions of the genome, and have been used to define populations at all levels from an individual isolate upwards. Some methods have the potential for the detection of specific genomic DNA sequences from initial sample, eliminating the requirement to culture the fungi. Molecular markers, and primers have commonly been developed from a variety of DNA fragments and specific regions such as genes ribosomal RNA (rRNA), virulence factors, and insertion sequences.
