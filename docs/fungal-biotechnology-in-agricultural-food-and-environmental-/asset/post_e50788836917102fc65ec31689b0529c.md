## 1 INTRODUCTION

Human society today demands the production of high-quality food in a sustainable way capable of causing least damage to the environment. Expected benefits include increase in the efficiency of crop production, reduction in agrochemical inputs, and an evaluation of the safety and biological effects in agriculture. Typically, fertilizer and pesticides have a high cost in terms of energy and investment in intensive production of plants. More than 150 years of cultivation with synthetic fertilizers and pesticides have left soils depleted of nutrients needed to facilitate the growth of crops. A less costly and environmentally friendly alternative enables the establishment of a low-input farming system. However, to implement such a plan, we must develop plants that can efficiently utilize and soil nutrients at present levels. The use of nature's own bio-biotechnology could be a possible answer to this problem. Role of mycorrhizal in sustainable agriculture can offer a great help towards modern agriculture improvement. In the present review, we have discussed the various aspects of biotechnology with special relation to mycorrhizal biofertilizers, so far reported synergies, mycorrhizal potential and methodologies for its multiplication, different constraints in its commercialization and its future role in achieving sustainable agriculture.

Biotechnology has been defined as the integrated use of biological, physical, and engineering sciences to achieve technological application of biology. The goal of technological application implies a direct relevance to commercialization for such multiplication of the invented bio-product so as to reach the masses. It is encouraging that both, government and industry are becoming more responsive to natural approaches to growing environmental problems. Individuals and organizations worldwide are coming to realize that excessive use of chemical fertilizers can negatively impact water quality and the environment as a whole. The goal, if addressed properly, may lead to a healthy environment for future generations.

Biofertilizers include environment-friendly fertilizers with organic organisms such as: (a) Rhizobium strains for legumes, (b) VAM strains for cereal crops, horticulture, and (d) phosphorus solubilizing bacteria (PSP)-phosphorus dissolving bacteria. Most among these are symbiotic mycorrhiza, Rhizobium members and phosphate group which deliver plant nutrient, nutrition and resistance to adverse environmental conditions. Biofertilizers also include microbial agents which may be involved in soil and associative microbes with higher plants. They are nature mini-fertilizers that are economical and safe source of plant nutrients through agricultural practices to improve soil fertility. They have great potential as a sustainable, renewable, and environmentally-friendly source of plant nutrients and are important components of an integrated nutrient system. Research on biofertilizers has identified biological N fixation, plant growth promoting bacteria (PGPR) and phosphorus solubilizing bacteria (Hge et al. 1999). Research and development activities involved in this demanding unexplored field include microbiological, biochemical, molecular, and ultrastructural techniques, by extensive field trials for testing before releasing them for agricultural use. Farmers have also realized the benefits of biofertilizers. Further research is critical as it will provide the latest information on the implementation of these technologies, including under different agro-climatic conditions and help in standardizing handling and storage norms. ## MYCORRHIZAL ASSOCIATIONS

Mycorrhizal associations include many fungi belonging to Zygomycota, Ascomycetes and Basidiomycetes and Deuteromycetes. A feature of these fungi is that they are generally dispersed in soils, exhibiting a strong benefit to hosts, and rarely saprophytic. Different types of mycorrhiza have been classified into different groups based on the extent of root penetration. Most arbuscular mycorrhizas are the most common and have gained tremendous importance in plant growth. The AM associations are formed between the fungi of Glomerales (Glomeromycota) and root plants (Harley and Smith 1983). Conservative estimates predict that this ancient symbiosis, dating back to the early Devonian (390 million years ago), occurs in approximately 90% of the Earth's land plants (Remy et al. 1997), gaining recognition as an integral component of elemental nutrition throughout the world. The AM association is a mutual benefit; the fungi supplies the plants with nutrients (from fixed positions) while the plant fungi improve the uptake of nutrients such as phosphate uptake and also converts unavailable various nutrients to available forms for the plant. This bidirectional exchange of nutrients takes place through extensively branched hyphae, commonly called arbusculae. In addition to the nutrient uptake, mycorrhiza improve the performance of other beneficial microbes, by resisting root pathogens, and increase the tolerance to extremes of environmental and biological stress.
