## INTRODUCTION

Explosive compounds are very significant environmental contaminants. Their production, use in conflict, and disposal have led to contaminated soils, sediments, and water around the world that are intrinsically toxic to microbes, fungi, animals, and man. In the United States, it is now a requirement that sites contaminated be remediated to acceptable site-specific goals (Jerger and Woodward 1990). Various remediation strategies have been adopted to date, and have involved physical, chemical, and biological approaches (Newcombe and Crawford 2002). Physical techniques such as incineration, excavation and filtration are effective for excavated contaminants. Chemical treatments have been tried, for example precipitation using surfactants and solvent extraction (Kapurlan 1990) and although these approaches generate further residues that have to be disposed of. Biological treatments have included biodegradation by microorganisms and fungi as an exploration of degradation where microbial inoculum is added to the contaminated environment (Kapurlan 1990). Biological approaches appear to be cost effective although other methods. Success depends on microbial sensitivity to toxicity of explosive, toxic byproducts of incomplete degradation and the length of time biological cleanup of a contaminated environment can take (Randy 1995). This review seeks to summarize available information about the metabolic pathways involved in fungal degradation of explosives and to evaluate the effectiveness of these pathways across the taxonomic groups.

## 2 EXPLOSIVES AS SUBSTRATES FOR MICROBIAL DEGRADATION

### 2.1 2,4,6-Trinitrotoluene (TNT)

The nitro-aromatic compound 2,4-6-trinitrotoluene (TNT) is of greatest concern as an environmental contaminant (Figure 1a). It is intrinsically toxic to microorganisms as nitro-groups on the aromatic rings withdraw electron density and limit the activity of dioxygenase enzymes (Yin 1990; 2000). Moreover, TNT is poorly soluble in water (140 mg/l at 25°C), relatively stable and persistent in the environment (Gorontzy et al. 1994). Its partial breakdown products are hazardous, carcinogenic, and mutagenic, and may produce cancer (Bennett 1994; Wang and Edwards 1984; Won et al. 1996).

Natural decomposition or result of bio-transformation and breakdown products include nitro-reduced amines (Yin Nitro). Nitro compounds such as TNT are rarely produced by Pseudomonas sp. via nitroreductase (Ohmori 1978) and oxygenase (Hahn et al. 1987). Streptomyces have been shown to produce phenolic, and benz derivatives by Fischer et al. (1996). Nitrophenol compounds such as aristocholic acid can be produced by plants (Williams and Barnett 1977) and this compound has been identified as a potent carcinogen, intercalating and forming adducts with DNA leading to oncogenesis (Arifi et al. 2000).

### 2.2 Nitrate Esters and Methylene Nitrate Cycles

Nitrate esters, including glycerol nitrate (nitroglycerin) and pentatethyl tetrahydro nitrate (PETN) are chemically active at low and at high concentrations they are acutely toxic (Geronty et al. 1994). Other nitrate esters, like nitrocellulose (Figure 1c), are non-toxic and relatively stable. Naturally occurring, biologically generated, or de-nitrated esters appear very rarely. There is a single report of an insect that contains a high level of nitrate esters (Figure 1d).

Another group of compounds that are significant environmentally contaminants are cyclic ethers of methyl nitramine (cyclotrimethylnitramine). This group includes the most powerful explosive, RDX and HMX. It also has a limited aqueous solubility of 2.3 mg/l at 20°C (Geronty et al. 1994). HMX is used in shaped charges or as a rocket propellant and is therefore a common environmental contaminant.

### 2.3 Origins and Evolution of Degradation Pathways

The xenobiotic nature of these compounds and their poor solubility may explain why, to date, they have accumulated in the environment. However, within the last decade after considerable exposure, bacteria are now being isolated from contaminated environments that are capable of mineralizing some of these explosives. For example, a TNT has been isolated from contaminated water samples by nitrobacterium (Parales 2000). Examination of the genes associated with this bacterium indicates that the evolution of the pathways is a recent event. This is suggested by the fact that the pathway is not optimized, there are vestigial genes, a lack of co-ordinated regulation, and incomplete (Parales 2000).

## 3 BIODEGRADATION OF TNT

In contrast to the newly evolved pathways we see in the bacteria, fungi have the capability to biodeg explosives using existing, co-metabolic, pathways that lead to mineralization. Parrish (1971) published the results of a screening for TNT degrading capacity among 190 fungi. This study showed that fungi were capable of TNT degradation, but the sensitivity of the fungi to levels exceeding 20mg/ppm was such that Benuri (1994) reported that the fungi used in biodegradation were inhibited. Results of this work have led to more de novo exposure of these explosives for some years. However, more recent screens have shown that the ability to degrade TNT to some degree was demonstrated by fungi genera such as Asoderma, Mycomdesporium (Schreiber et al. 1997b). Recent data suggests that the fungi are capable of achieving mineralization of TNT at rates far higher than Hawari et al. (2000a).

The co-metabolic reactions increase the electron density of the aromatic ring and facilitate electrophilic attack by lignin-degrading enzymes (Field et al. 1993). TNT degradation occurs by a series of co-metabolic synergistic reactions involving manganese peroxidase and lacase. The peroxidase enzymes are heme-containing glycoproteins that require hydrogen peroxide to catalyze single electron oxidations that generate free radicals. Hydrogen peroxide is generated by oxidase enzymes including oxygen, which is a better electron acceptor than phenol oxide, which uses molecular oxygen as its terminal electron acceptor (Fritsche et al. 2006). Laccase can catalyze polymerization and demethylation via oxidation (Harvey and Thurston 2001).

The hydroxy alcohol to aldehyde conversion limits the production of free radicals that are involved in the oxidation of primary substrates of TNT. This biodegradation of TNT may be inhibited if levels of HADNT accumulate.
