
# Decoloration of Industrial Wastes and Degradation of Dye Waste

Kirsten Schliephake / Warren L. Baker / Greg T. Longer Environment and Biotechnology Centre, Swinburne University of Technology, Melbourne, Australia

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Large quantities of toxic and intensely colored waste effluents are produced by a range of industrial processes. Effluents are treated by processes ranging from aerobic and anaerobic physico-chemical techniques. More innovative technologies using white-rot fungi have also been studied to deoxymethyl pollutants, generally referred to as biological methods (Kahmark and Unwin 1996). These methods have the potential to minimize pollution or remediate it if it occurs.

White-rot fungi and their ligninolytic enzymes have wide industrial potential. Phanerochaete chrysosporium and Coriolis versicolor can degrade a number of related organic compounds. These fungi have been shown to efficiently decolorize and degrade such compounds (Archibald 1990; Fukui et al. 1992; Sundmann et al. 1988). Effluent color from sulfite pulping effluents has also been observed with C. chrysosporium (Bergueau et al. 1991). Furthermore, these fungi are showing significant potential to biodeg a large number of xenobiotic compounds and hydrocarbons (Field et al. 1993; Glaser 1990; Hammo 1989). Lignin degradation, manganese peroxidase, and laccase are the three main enzymes (Jones 1995; Schoemaker et al. 1998) have been well-studied but several other enzymes have been utilized in further degradation of the products resulting from their action.

This chapter describes the rationale and results for three related studies on whether fungi (a) treat effluent wastewater to reduce color, (b) to decolor dye solutions used in industrial process, and (c) to determine the enzyme involvement in the decolorization process.

## 2 ORGANISMS DEGRADING LIGNIN

Lignin is an amorphous, highly aromatic heterogenous, 3-D branched polymer (Buswell and Olerik 1987; Higuchi 1985) with its substituents composed of carbon–carbon and other linkages. There are different lignin structures consisting of various amounts of the three phenyl alcohols present: p-coumaryl, coniferyl, and syring alcohols. These give rise to hydroxy, guaiacyl, and syring rings (Higuchi 1985; Kirk and Farrell 1987). The lignin alcohol compounds have various radical structures.

White-colored is degraded by the bleached color of added wood decay (white-rot fungi) and other organisms. White-rot fungi can degrade most varieties of polymers, including lignin (Crawford and Crawford 1980; Kirk 1971). The lignin degradation follows two pathways (a) simultaneous attack on lignin and polysaccharides or (b) sequential attack on lignin and polysaccharides after extensive depletion of cellulose (Buswell 1991).

Other degrading lignin include the brown-rot fungi, the soft-rot fungi, which seem capable of some demethylation and partial degradation of side chains and aromatic rings (Ander and Eriksson 1978; Buswell 1991) and some bacteria associated with the Actinomycetes and Eubacteria (1971). Bacteria may extensively degrade, demethoxylate, and modify aromatic rings and side chains.

## 3 DECOLORIZATION OF DYES BY MICROORGANISMS

Synthetic azo, anthraquinone, triarylene, and other dyes are extensively utilized for textile dyeing, paper printing, and color photography. A necessary criterion for the application of these dyes is that they resist photodegradation and leaching. These dyes are also resistant to microbial degradation and are not easily removed from water by conventional biological treatment (Mayber 1981; Shaw 1991). The removal of dyes from water is achieved by elimination of the dyes using adsorption, chemical oxidation, and flocculation.

Azo dyes and aromatic rings are soft to nature and highly resistant to oxidative degradation. Partial degradation of azo dyes during activated sludge treatment, has been observed. Significant removal of color from the water was attributed to adsorption of the dye onto the substrate (Aftabzadeh 1991). The dyes are susceptible to anaerobic degradation. The reduction of azo dyes to colors produced by bacteria has been reported (Mayber 1981). The initial step in this transformation is the reductive cleavage of the azo group, resulting in the formation of aromatic amines. The decolorization of a number of textile dyes and effluents by an immobilized anaerobic consortium has also been demonstrated (N-Nagam 1996; Osseping 1996).

The decolorization of chrysosporium-induced dyes, such as Poly-B41, Poly-R8, and Poly Y-606, has been correlated with its lignin degrading degree (Sle and Gold 1983). Strains of Postreat have also been known to effectively decolorize Poly-B41 (Plat 1983); P. pulvinaceous has increased Poly-B decolorization, as well as enhanced laccase production. Laccase is produced by Pleotus sp. as part of its ligninolytic system by dye decolorization by this fungus appears to be induced due to laccase secretion (Masapy and Lennon 1992).

The utilization of the white-rot fungi and their nonspecific ligninolytic enzyme system for the biodegradation of other industrial dyes has increased interest, since it also became known that P. chrysosporium is able to decolorize and decolorize a range of sulfonated dyes, heterocyclic, and triphenylamine dyes (Bumpus and Brock 1988; Cripps et al. 1990; Paczynski et al. 1992; Sadler and Evans 1992). The triphenylamine dye, crystal violet was decolorized by P. chrysosporium. These metabolites were identified and the dye N-demethylated by fungal providing evidence that the enzymatic system is least effective in oxidative biodegradation (Bumantag 1998). A number of azo, triazine, and heterocyclic dyes can also be decolorized by strains of P postreat (Table 1). This further demonstrates the effectiveness of the ligninolytic system in biodegrading and decolorizing wastewater with dyes from textile and other industries (Cripps et al. 1990); Fluorescent acid (Gogya et al. 1992) and triazine dyes (K and Neto 1991) were also decolorized. Peroxidase was used by P. chrysosporium, although H2O2-enhanced decolorization by P postreat has also been noted (Vyas and Molinar 2015). Knapp et al. (1995) suggests that more work is needed on the effect of the decolorizing capacity of other members of Basomycetes.

A number of white-rot fungi and their enzymes have been used successfully in detoxification and degradation of selected xeniotics. The most studied wood-rot fungus is F. chrysosporium. Most of the oxidative reactions are presumed to be one-electron oxidation of suitable substrates to phenolic radicals. Nonphenolic substrates undergo radical intermediates. The result is the formation of oxygen-ring products, small aromatic fragments, and quinones these can be further metabolized.

### 4.1 Lignin Peroxidase

A lignin-degrading enzyme from F. chrysosporium was identified by three independent research teams (Glenn et al. 1983; Tien and Kirk 1983). This enzyme was also generally known as lignin peroxidase, it can depolymerize lignin and cleave Co-O linkages in sites suggesting that the polymerization and cleavage ability of lignin occurs during wood degradation (Chen and Kirk 1988). The enzyme has also been found in other fungi Versicolor (Dorschel et al. 1987; Johnson et al. 1987; Wagner et al. 1988), Praduta (Hataka et al. 1987; Kautinen et al. 1988; Niko-Paavola 1987; Niko-Paavola et al. 1988) and P. treemulus (Bas-Hawkes et al. 1987).

### 4.2 Manganese Peroxidase

One year after discovery of lignin peroxidase, Kuwahara et al. (1984) reported the isolation of another enzyme from F. chrysosporium ME 346. This formation showed Mn(II), H2O2 and lactate dependency on increased peroxide concentration. The enzyme can oxidize a variety of substrates, including phenol, d-oxidation, and Poly R. Purpose characterization of this enzyme is presumed de manganese peroxidase.

A similar enzyme, vacuoxidase was found in BKM-1677 (Paszynski et al. 1985). This enzyme oxidizes low-molecular weight phenols and aromatic acids produced by Mn(II) and H2O2. It does not oxidize phenol red and is activated by NADPH rather than glutathione, providing a link to xenobiotic metabolism. Generation of peroxide was formed in lignin with manganese peroxidase from GSH under oxidative conditions.

Manganese accumulation in the decayed residue of wood (Blanche 1984) supported the hypothesis that manganese dependent peroxidases participate in lignin degradation (Paszynski et al. 1985) via oxidation to a higher oxidation state (Glenn et al. 1986; Paszynski et al. 1986). Manganese peroxide oxidative reduction of Mn(II) to Mn(III) was subsequently demonstrated (Glenn et al. 1986).

### 4.3 Laccase: Role and Function in Lignin Biodegradation

Laccases as polyphenoloxidases are widely known as p-diphenoloxidases (EC 1.10.3.2) (Tuer et al. 1994 and 1995). The enzyme is found in many plant species (Mayer and Harel 1979) and is widely distributed in fungi (Mayer 1997) wood-rot fungi. It is often associated with lignin degradation, manganese peroxidase, or Nordic (Nerud and Misurová 1996).

Laccases are blue oxidases and participate in electron transfer in biological systems by catalyzing the reduction of oxygen to water with various organic substrates (Reinhardt and Malminen 1994). The protein contains four copper atoms, which facilitate efficient catalytic sites. The Type I (blue) copper center has a strong absorption and gives a characteristic blue color. The Type II (normal) and the Type III involve a multiple of coupled ions (Fer and Peri 1981; Morpopo et al. 1993). Studies into the mechanism show that the His bridge between the binuclear center and the Type II forms a trinuclear cluster as the active site (Solomon 1988).

Phase mechanism has been investigated on Red versicolor. It is found that the enzyme has a protective function, causing oxidation of a natural polymeric lignin in the case of tree damage (Reinhardt and Malminen 2001). A plant laccase was involved in lignification (Ba et al. 1993) and the role of laccase has been extensively reviewed by O'Malley et al.

Fungal laccases oxidize phenol and phenolic-structures of lignin with subsequent polymerization or degradation (White et al. 1997). They are considered to proceed via phenolic pathways. The unit of lignin biodegradation remains largely untried, since most of the extensively studied wood-rot fungi, F. chrysosporium, and other Basidiomycetes do not produce lignin in lignolytic conditions.
