# 1 INTRODUCTION

Mycorrhizae are mutual symbioses between plants and fungi. Such, mycorrhizal fungi play an important role in structuring plant communities by improving the performance of individual plants. Improved growth and survival of host plants is mostly attributed to the ability of fungi to improve plant nutrition and rooting structure (Allen and Allen 1986; Allen et al. 1989; Lewis 1973) and enhance resistance to environmental stress (Williams 1992). However, a variety of mycorrhizal types differently affects their host plants. If the appropriate group of fungi is unavailable to form the type of mycorrhizal association to which plant belongs, groups of plants may not recover from disturbance. Disturbance alters fungal recovery typically occurs after initial plant disturbance. As a consequence, the formation of functional mycorrhiza may decay or may occur.

The challenge of mycorrhizal restoration of arid plant communities has received considerable attention, notably because establishment of mycorrhizae involves rates and patterns of plant succession (Allen and Allen 1990). It is critical, to understand the factors that regulate the nature of mycorrhizal rhizae (Allen et al. 1992), and determine whether mycorrhizal inoculation is a viable alternative to managing existing fungal populations or manipulating their natural revival. Although the specific factors that regulate mycorrhizal reestablishment are not entirely known, they involve survival of residual fungal propagules, dispersal of propagules from adjacent disturbed areas or from local sites of residual, and available microbiomes where plants and fungi can survive and contact each other

inoculations of a disturbed soil (Allen et al. 1992). Alternatively, inoculations with native or exotic fungi facilitate formation of mycorrhizae in a shorter period, given certain circumstances. The simple application of inoculum, however does not guarantee the formation of mycorrhiza. The potential benefits of inoculation could be outweighed by cost and efficiency limitations, if the probability of plants benefiting from mycorrhiza is low (Findlay and Kendall 2001). Understanding mycorrhizal function and limits of mycorrhizal fungi is key to studying ecosystem disturbance and recovery from disturbance (Allen et al. 1999).

In examining the role of mycorrhizal fungi in ecosystem restoration, has been considerable interest in determining the influence of fungi fungi on revegetation of polluted soils. The use of mycorrhizal fungi in bioremediation of metal-polluted soils has received increasing attention. Recent studies suggest that mycorrhizal fungi exhibit high metal tolerance and as a result, carry heavy-metal tolerance to which mycorrhizal associations benefit, in terms of alleviating metal toxicity, largely unchanged (Allen 1997). We will address in detail the response of mycorrhizal fungi to disturbance and the factors that inhibit or facilitate their natural recovery. We will also discuss management options for enhancing fungal reestablishment and the drawbacks of inoculum. We will briefly discuss the potential role of mycorrhizal fungi as bioindicators and their application to bioremediation.
