While the batch culture is most frequently used, continuous cultivation of fungi can have some advantages producing the yeast yield (Table 5). As shown in Table 5, the maximum biomass yield of 70 g/l was produced by S. cerevisiae in starch medium with respect to the dilution rate when the rate was low (Yung and Tung 1996). Summarizing the results of conversion of both, two-step cultivation resulted in 17 g/l biomass and 89% COD reduction. In general, it was observed that the combination of yeast fungi resulted in improvement of COD reduction and wastewater purification. On the other hand, dilution rates of 0.56 with cell yield resulted in a productivity of 0.4 g/l biomass and COD reduction of 18-22% (Wang et al. 1980). In contrast, COD was most effectively reduced by K. marxarius (Braun and Mayreh 1981). A series of yeast and fungi in two stages give the most promising results with respect to efficient consumption of organic matter (Bottara Castilla et al.1994). The continuous culture yields a higher protein content in the biomass and enhance the stability of the culture as well as its resistance to contamination (Banker 1982). Microorganisms rich in protein is the main product of fungal conversion. Some information is available about cell material. That of utilis composition is different from that of other yeasts grown in carbohydrate media. Microbial protein has a high content of essential amino acids such as cysteine and methionine; the content of the latter amino acids is generally lower in microbial biomass (Cabb et al. 1983; Quinn and 1987; Yazacioglu et al. 1980). Compared to the recommended level of FA standards (Quinn 1997), yeast biomass from fungi is rich in nitrogen, vitamins, and also contains important active substances.

Nutritional evaluation of yeast biomass grown in wheat distillery starch showed that it was suitable for human consumption with a protein utilization value of 0.90 and a digestibility of 0.67. The biomass was not toxic, as confirmed by toxic tests (Banker et al. 1982). When molasses were used, K and Mg contents had low leaching effects (Araujo et al. 1977). The nutrient content in cell should not be as low as in filamentous fungi which have an advantage over other yeasts in respect (Araujo et al.1977). It has been observed that levels of DNA and RNA were lower in batch than in continuous cultures (Quinn and Marchant 1979b). Feeding experiments of Aspergillus in a biomass for diet, demonstrated excellent acceptability, good gain, and no toxicity; the biomass had a very good protein efficiency ratio, comparable to meat and soya (Araujo et al. 1977). A 1% methionine increased the biological value but Candida yeast biomass was lower than that reported (Cabb et al. 1983).

With the use of fungi for bioconversion of wastwaters, not only fungi but also other products such as ethanol can be produced by filamentous fermentation, if the process is performed at low vacuum (Teramato et al. 1993; Ueda and Teramato 1995; Ueda et al. 1991). Microfacial polysaccharides can be produced, such as cellulose (Lee et al. 1990) or chitosan by A.pergillus or G. bacteria strains (Yoko et al. 1998). Hydrotic enzymes can be produced by filamentous fungi. Cellases and other glucases can be produced by growth in filamentous medium (Sato et al. 1986; Guider-Cuzman). Some ascomycetes, A. avavari kawaki, are able to produce starch enzymes from wastewater (Morimura et al. 1991). Products are produced with Aspergillus species (Morimura et al. 1994) and barley (Morimura et al. 1994a) stalls. The produced is a yeast product, astaxanthin, from P. rhodlla (Fontana et al. 1997) and plant growth hormones, by growing white fungi, such as F. g. trogi and V. Yire et al. (1999).

## 6 ECONOMIC CONSIDERATIONS

Little is available concerning the economics of the bioconversion process. Only rough estimates of costs were given by Maera et al. (1983) and Tomlinson (1976). The main costs are investment in equipment, energy needed for heating, and cooling (Maera et al. 1983), biomass separation (Tomlinson 1976), nutrient retention, solubility limits, protein production, and irregularities in raw material. However, capital cost may not be as much, as shown in some studies on molillage treatment methods, where aerobic yeast growth was estimated to reduce the capital (Maera et al. 1983).
