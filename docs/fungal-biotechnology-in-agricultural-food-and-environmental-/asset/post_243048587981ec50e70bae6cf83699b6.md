Plant fungi significantly contribute to crop yield and damage, lost by plant pathogen services. The potential of AMF to control plant fungi has been clearly demonstrated (Becker et al. 1999; Bodker et al. 2002; Boyetchko and Tewari 1996; Duchesse et al. 1989; Kapoor et al. 1996; Kiasandari et al. 2002; Kelger and Gould 1983). There are reports that AMF inoculation did not have any effect on disease severity (Gullin et al. 2002; Larsen and Bodker 1995; Weiss et al. 1991; Zamolino and Schenck 1993). In order for practical and routine use of AMF as protectors against pathogenic fungi, AMF performance must be consistent, specific, and effective.

Specificity of AMF for crops is crucial to mitigate any detrimental effects on beneficial micro-organisms. However, there are conflicting reports on the specificity of AMF. For example, propagation with Glomus intraradices and Glomus species induced reduced necrosis and external symptoms caused by Fusarium spp. accompanied by other mechanisms of biocontrol. Using an in vitro system, Filion et al. (1999) demonstrated that extraction from the extraradical mycelium of G. intraradices reduced the conditional germination of F. oxysporum sp. f. specifically. Alternatively, the chemical equilibrium of the mycorrhizosphere may have affected pathogen control.

To enhance AMF efficiency, researchers have used AMF species or mixture of AMF against pathogens. For instance, co-inoculation with G. formae, Gigamospora marina, Aciculpora lacus, and Sclerocyst eliminated the damaging effects of Sclerotinia fungi (Kulkarni et al. 1997). Also, tobacco inoculation with a mixture of AMF and Trichoderma effectively controlled damping-off caused by Fusarium aphanodermatum and black shank disease caused by P. pariticum. Neerarium (Steerlann et al. 1998). In cereals, chemical microbes act synergistically with plants to suppress disease. A combination of wheat, carbandizim, and Glomus fungus protected seedlings from the root pathogen Mycelporum macropholina, in 100% survival (Pashley et al. 1997). AMF effectively managed growth and symptoms caused by V. twisted.

Newelman et al. (1995) showed that pre-inoculating the root with Glomus marina reduced the infection of F. oxysporum. The requirement of a fully established AMF community has been debated. For example, a correlation was found by Fieldman (1998) between the degree of AMF colonization and susceptibility to powdery mildew. However, although the importance of AMF is not described, this study investigated the effectiveness of a single species of AMF, indicating that AMF control was satisfactory.
