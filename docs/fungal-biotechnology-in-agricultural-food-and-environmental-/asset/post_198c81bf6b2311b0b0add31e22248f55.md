## Cellulose Degradation by Fungi

Justine N. Niamke / Nam Sun Wang University of Maryland, College Park, Maryland, USA

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Cellulose and hemicellulose comprise about 30 and 30%, respectively, of the biomass nature produces through photosynthesis and are by far the most abundant carbon polymers in nature. Lignin and minerals make up the remainder of the biomass. In addition to that already exists in natural settings (especially in trees), cellulose/hemicellulose is a large component of wastes originating from municipal, agricultural, forestry, and certain industrial sources. Its abundance also supports the use of renewable resources for fuel, energy feedstock. Although a small portion of the waste is presently converted into useful products, this picture will likely change in the future, as the world demand for energy will increase (Millet et al. 1976). For directly burning wood, most cellulose-products first require degradation of cellulose to sugars before conversion, usually through fermentation of the biomass to yield useful products, including ethanol, acetic acid, lactic acid, and alcohols. Besides various chemical products, degradation of cellulose also removes the waste associated with forests (Katzen and Moreau 1995). After several decades of research worldwide, there are two main routes to cellulose degradation: chemical (alkaline) and biological (enzymatic or microbial). Of the latter, fungi and anaerobic bacteria are excellent candidates. In this chapter, we specifically focus on cellulose degradation by fungi and compare the economics of alternative processes.

## 2 BIOMASS AS A SUGAR SOURCE

Cellulose is a long polymer ranging from 1000 to 10000 d-glucose units. Glucose monomers are linked together with $\beta$-1,4-glycosidic bonds to form stable chains, and these chains further aggregate. Together they hydrate to a rigid crystalline structure that is water-impermeable, water-soluble, and resistant to hydrolysis (Linko 1987). The boundary and sequence of the molecules determine the chemical properties of cellulose (Allen and Sjöstrom 1985). On the other hand, hemicellulose forms a highly branched structure of both six-carbon and five-carbon sugars. The branched structure allows it to exist in an amorphous form that is more susceptible to hydrolysis. Compared to cellulose, which is similar across all biomass sources, hemicellulose is highly variable in structure and composition, depending on the source.

The most intensely studied fungi used for cellulose degradation is Trichoderma reesei, which produces an efficient ensemble of major classes of enzymes that work synergistically to cellulose:
- endoglucases, which attack at random locations in the cellulose chain,
- exoglucases or glucanohydrolases, which liberate dimer cellulose from the end of the cellulose chain, and
- β-glucosides, which release glucose monomers from cellulose and soluble cellobiose. Although T. reesei is efficient in degrading cellulose, it cannot convert cellulose into a useful product, unless fungi happen to be the target. In fact, only a few fungi are known to convert efficiently into useful products. The following are filamentous fungi that are capable of hydrolyzing cellulose as well as converting the sugars liberated from hydrolysis to ethanol or acetic acid:
- Neurospora
- Fusarium oxysporum
