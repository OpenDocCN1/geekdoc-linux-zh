## 1 INTRODUCTION

The practice of monoculture in modern agriculture enables us to continue to provide foodstuffs for the world's ever increasing population. Monoculture is, however, an unnatural situation, which is inherently unstable and offers considerable opportunity for the development of biological control. Agriculture has therefore become heavily dependent on fungicides to control the growth of fungal pathogens that threaten agricultural crops. With intensive fungicide use, the destruction of crops by fungal pathogens is a severe problem worldwide that leads to losses of up to 50% (Larsen and Shell 1993). The use of pesticides in particular, has also resulted in significant costs to public health and the environment. Studies aimed at replacing pesticides with environmentally safe measures are currently being conducted at many centers. In this context, control by biological agents provides an alternative to the use of chemicals. It is generally recognized that biological agents are safer and environmentally friendly than reliance on the use of high volumes of fungicides and other alternatives. The scientific interest in biological control of plant pathogens is a response, but there is an equally greater need for biological control agents that presently go uncontrollably controlled by these “traditional" means (Cook 1993). But, biological control should and be justified on its own merits, without giving it importance at the expense of chemical controls.

Biocontrol must be effective, reliable, consistent, and economical before it becomes an important component of plant disease management. To meet these criteria, we must increase our understanding of the biology of the biocontrol agent in question, in which most cases are extremely limited. Furthermore, superior strains, with delivery systems that enhance biocontrol activity, may be developed (Harman et al. 2006). Many agents could be modified to enhance their attributes. In addition, we can now think of microorganisms with inhibitory activity against plant pathogens as potential sources for disease resistance.

## 2 TRICHODMA AS A BIOLOGICAL CONTROL AGENT

The potential for the use of Trichoderma as biocontrol agents was suggested by Weidling (1932) which was the first to demonstrate the parasitic activity of this genus towards pathogens such as *Rhizoctonia solani* (Chet 1990. Weidling 1932); then, several of Trichoderma species as biocontrol agents have shown an attack on ecologically important aerial and soilborne pathogens (Chet 1987). In many experiments, Trichoderma was found to be a necrotrophic mycoparasite (Boosalis 1964; Chet and Elad 1982; etc). Mycoparasitism is defined as a direct attack on a fungal thallus, followed by nutrient utilization by the parasite (Chet et al. 1997). Necrophic mycoparasites, such as Trichoderma, are those that kill the host cells, or just after invasion and use the nutrients released. The antagonistic nature is due to the production of antibiotics, toxins, or hydrolytic enzymes in such proportions as cause death and destruction of the host (Machman and Sakai 1993). In summary, Trichoderma includes: (a) competition, (b) parasitism, (c) antibiosis, and (d) induction of defense in host plants, or by combination of some. Parasitism is a complex process including: (a) host recognition, (b) secretion of hydrolytic enzymes; (c) hypha penetration and invasion (Figure 1), and (d) lysis of the host.

### 2.2 Host Invasion

Analysis by electrochemistry has shown that the interaction of Trichoderma spp., with either S. rolii or R. colonum, the parasite hyphae contacted their host and perforated cell walls. These observations led to the suggestion that Trichoderma produced and secreted hydrolytic enzymes for the partial degradation of the host's cell wall. Interestingly, the fungal species within this clade of the phylogenetic tree, all of them are either parasites and the corresponding genes have been implicated in their parasitic activity. However, the chitinous system of Trichoderma is much more complex. Two genes showing similarity to the one D33 produced by Kim et al. (1992) have been cloned from T. viride (Kim et al. 2002). These two genes are closely related, and belong to family 18, class of glycosyl hydrolases. Further, two genes of N-beta-1,4-glucosidase belonging to different isolates have been identified in Trichoderma (Draborg et al. 1995; Kim et al. 2002). Chit 36 protein shows no significant homology to either chitinase (Lee et al. 2001). Recently, this enzyme was proposed to play a major regulatory role controlling the expression of chitinases upon contact of Trichoderma with its host (Kubick et al. 2001).
