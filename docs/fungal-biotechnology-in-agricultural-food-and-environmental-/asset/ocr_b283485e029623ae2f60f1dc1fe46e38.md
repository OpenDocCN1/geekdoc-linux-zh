# 5 BIPHENYL

Biphenyl (C₁₂₂H₁₀</sub>), found in coal tar, oil, and natural gas, is hydroxylated by various C. elegans (Dodge et al. 1997). Several metabolites metabolize biphenyl to 4-hydroxybiphenyl, which dehydroxylates vanvrijoides, hydroxyglutatins, and J. lipotica converts it to 4-phenyl-2-methyl-carboxylic acid (Lange et al. 1998, Romero et al 2001). The yeast Trichospor metabolizes biphenyl into three hydroxylated biphenyls, and a quinetone (Siemann et al. 2000; Siemann et al. 2001). It transforms the di- and hydroxylated biphenyls into eight different ring-cleaved products (Siemann et al. 2001).

The first step in metabolism of PAHs (Figure 3) by most fungi is initiated by a cytochrome P450 monooxygenase; white-body dioxygenases may also employ lignin, peroxidase, manganese peroxidase, or laccase (Hammel 1995; Cepergilla 1997; Gergia 1999a). Later steps may employ enzymes to form sulfates, glucosides, glucuronides, or xylososes (Casillas et al. 1996). The ability to transform PAHs has been explained for the biodegradation of contaminated soil (Judaš and Nadeh 2000; Cepergilla and Sunderland 2001).

Naphthalene is oxidized by C. elegans to 1- and 2-naphthol, then to 1,2-dihydroxy-1-naphthoicone, the 1,2- and 1,4-naphthoquinones, sulfates, and glucosides conjugate glucuronates (Cepergilla and Gergia 1997; Cepergilla 1992). Both constitutive and inducible hydrolases are involved in the pathway (Siemann et al. 2001). A. niger has reported (Yodaš and Karagrand 1997) to metabolize naphthalene via naphthase, a dioxygenase produced by itself. Naphthalene was removed from the environment by methylation of P. chrysosporium (Liu et al. 1999).

Acenaphylene is oxidized by C. elegans to 6-hydroxyacetophenone, x-1,2-dihydroxyacetophenone, acenaphthene anthracene-1,2-diol, and further methyl metabolites (Potholar et al.). The bacterium of subtilis metabolizes biphenylene to 2-amino-3-(3-ethylbenzothiaz-6-sulfonate) (ABTS) (Pickard et al. 1999), and the presence of T. vesicolor oxidizes acenaphthalene and acethenylene in the presence of 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (Johannes and Majerczyk 2000).

Fluorene is oxidized by C. elegans to 9-hydroxyfluorene, 9-fluoreone, and 2-hydroxy-9-fluorenone (Potholar et al. 1993). The first are also produced by P. Oryspermum, P. osteatus, and other fungi (Bezal et al. 1996; Bogan 1998; Garon et al. 2000; Schützendi et al. 1999). The oxidation of fluorene to 9-hydroxy fluorene requires manganese peroxidase activity in P. chrysosporium and (Bogan et al 1996; Collins and Dobson 1996). The fungus C. gallica is also able to oxidize fluorene (Brasser et al. 2000; Maj and Majerczyk 2000).

Anthracene is transformed by C. elegans to trans-1,2-dihydroxyanthracene (Cenfiglia and Yang 1984); 9,10-anthracene has also been reported (Siemann and Dlugosnki 1999). Rhizonia solani produces the (+) and (trans-) 1,2-dihydroxy and three conjugate glucosides (Sutherland et al. 1992). Anthracene, by Rhizonia, chrysosporium, rhizodes, A. niger, P. parastica, and several white-rot fungi transforms into anthracene (Field et al. 1996; Giraud et al. 2001; Gamsby and Karagrand 1997). P. chrysosporium oxidizes anthracene into anthraquinone and CO2, produced with the involvement of peroxidases (in fungi or bacteria) in the humus (Bogan et al. 2000; Co2-Marquez-Rocha et al. 2000). Pleutus satarius-cing (P. vulgaris), and other fungi produce anthracene without conjugating them (Bogan and Lamar).

Phenanthrene is oxidized by C. elegans. Sulfophenanthrene is converted to di-phenanthrenols, phenols, and sulfate conjugates (Cenfiglia and Yang 1984; Sutherland et al. 1996). Glucoside and conjugate glucosides may also be produced (Casillas et al. 1996). A. niger is reported to produce 1-methoxy-phenanthrene (Siemann et al. 2001) as well as phenanthrene. P. chrysosporium oxidizes phenanthrene into phenanthrene-9,10-dione and phenanthrene-9,10-diolic acid in water (Hammel et al. 1992). P. osteatus oxidizes degrades phenanthrene (Bezal et al. 1996a; März-Rocha et al. 2000; Schützendi et al. 1999), producing the phenanthrene-9,10-diol and 2,2'-diphenic acid (Bezal et al.)
