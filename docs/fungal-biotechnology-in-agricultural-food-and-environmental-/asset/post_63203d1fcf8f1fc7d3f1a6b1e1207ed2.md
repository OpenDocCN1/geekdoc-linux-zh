One of the major difficulties with Trichoderma biocontrol strains is their genetic instability, which is poorly understood at present. This is partly due to the fact that there is little known about the genome organization and plasticity of Trichoderma. Not even the number of chromosomes is known with certainty: Fekte et al. (1996) reported six chromosomes in the Trichoderma genome seemed to lack the smallest chromosomes ranging from 7 to 7.7 Mbp. When fractionated chromosomes of the five species were analyzed in the presence of the ech42 (endonuclease-binding), strong hybridization signals were displayed, but their physical position on the chromosomes remained unknown. Herrera-Estrilla (1993) compared the karyotype of T. reesei with T. atrotvirides (named erroneously T. harizianum in its system) and T. viride, and detected similar genome organization among them. Satie et al. (1993), when comparing three strains of Trichoderma (one and two mutants derived from it), found that the smallest chromosome was not present in the mutants. The degree of chromosome polymorphism at the species levels, the karyotypes were relatively constant. A report to the company (Gemma 1999) was proved by the fact that “T. harizianum” strains consist of several different karyotypes (CP Kubick). Thus, as expected for an asexual fungus, chromosome instability is unlikely responsible for the genetic instability of Trichoderma biocontrol strains.
