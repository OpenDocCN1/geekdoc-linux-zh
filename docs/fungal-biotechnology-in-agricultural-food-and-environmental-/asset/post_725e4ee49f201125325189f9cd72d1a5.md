
## 2.2 Temperature

Fungi differ widely in the range of temperature over which growth can occur and those optimum for growth (Magan 1997). For example, Penicillium species P. aurorangensis and P. verrucosum grow a wide range – 4 to 35°C while Aspergillus fumigatus has a wide tolerance of 10–55°C. The majority of fungi in stored grain grow with optimum in the range 25–35°C. Lowering temperature reduces the metabolic activity of fungi, and enabling longer-free storage. However, most grain can be protected by slowing the growth of Alternaria, Penicillium, and Fusarium, in which each is able to grow at low temperatures. Pockets of moisture are critical indicators of quality storage. Pockets of moisture can allow initiation of fungi which produces metabolic heat resulting in a success of fungi becoming dominant and with spontaneous heating and dominance by thermolerant/thermophilic fungi and actinomycetes (Lacey and Magan 1991).

The fungal community can be very different; colonization of grain pre- and post-harvest are described separately.

## 3 POSTHARVEST FUNGAL COLONIZATION OF CEREAL GRAINS

### 3.1 Factors

Conditions in the stored grain are more stable and controlled than those in field. Stored cereal grain offers a good ecological niche for fungi. The $a_w$, $O_2$ level, interactions with other organisms, insect preservatives, and storage time are the most important factors influencing the growth of fungi in grains but cannot be isolated. All these factors interact with each other. For instance, fluctuations in temperature can cause condensation of water that increases the water activity in stored grain. ### 3.2 Myofora

The number of fungi that can colonize stored grain is very wide. These represent a high number of genera and include polytrophic, mesophilic, thermophilic, and xerophilic species (Hill 1983; Lacey 1980; Magan and Lacey 1984a,b). The most characteristic aspect of these species being stored in the grain are the Aspergillus and Penicillium. In addition, it is possible to find species of Eotroph, Fusarium, Rhizopus, which include Talagus species, Mucor, and parasites (Figure 2). All species are widespread throughout the world and they cover almost the whole range of environmental conditions likely to be found in stored grain.

While involved in biodeterioration of grain are considered to be aerobic, there are many other species which cannot grow in sand and thus dominate specialized grain ecosystems. In many cases, decreasing $O_2$ to <14% is required to be substantially reduced. Reducing $O_2$ to >50% is required for inhibition of growth (Magan and Lacey 1994). Some species, e.g. P. roquefort are able to grow in $O_2$ provided at 4%. The use of integrated postharvest systems for prevention of deterioration entails modifying $O_2$ and controlling simultaneously the use of $O_2$ free. The tolerance to low $O_2$ and high $CO_2$ is also important. Interactions with grain water are critical factors. The most effective treatment is if the grain is dry. Control of insects in stored systems may not, however, be effective against some insects which survive and grow with high relative humidity. From one however, from the range of grains that can affect the colonization of microorganisms, $a_w$ is the most important factor limiting their activity. Each microorganism has a specific range over which they can develop. The most organisms manage to colonize seeds because specialized groups grow at intermediate and low $a_w$ levels. Fungi contribute greatly to seed losses, either alone or together with insects.

Water and temperature are very important factors that determine the fungi present in stored grains. The fungi have species that need high levels of $a_w$, grow in importance when the grain is stored and subjected to processing directions that deteriorate. On the other hand, Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Europot have a low importance in the field, become very important during the storage of the grains. Another factor that determines the moisture in the grain is the oxygen concentration, because fungi grow very well under oxygen concentrations. Under these conditions Magan and Lacey (1984) reported Fusarium fumorum, P. roqueforti, and A. candidus are the most consistent, which is with the fungal community found on cereals under storage.

Both climatic and geographical location can explain the differences in the mycofora of different grains in the field. During storage, however, the mycodora is very similar for all grains (Phelle 1988). The number of fungi found on maize, rye, and other cereals in tropical climates are the same as those found in temperate regions except for small number of additional species, and a greater frequency of Aspergillus and different Penicillium.

Penicillium, Aspergillus, and Fusarium spp. isolated from maize grain have been found to be significantly influenced by $a_w$, temperature, and their interactions. The effects of $a_w$ and temperature on germination can be quantified in two: (a) the minimum $a_w$ for germination and (b) the lag time before germination.

Fusarium normally found in stored grain can germinate at lower $a_w$ levels than the fungi found in the field, especially in changes to lower $a_w$ when changing temperature level. $A. niger$ germinate to 0.96 at 10°C, but down to 0.80 at 25°C. $P. hordei$ germinate to 0.90 at 9°C, but down to 0.85 at 25°C. $P. verticillatum$ germinate only to 0.96 at 5°C, but down to 0.88 at 25°C.
