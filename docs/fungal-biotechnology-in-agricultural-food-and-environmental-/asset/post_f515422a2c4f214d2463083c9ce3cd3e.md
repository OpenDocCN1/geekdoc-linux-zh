## 4.1 Lignin Peroxidase

### 4.1.1 Properties

Lignin peroxidase is a set of glycosylated isozymes with masses ranging from 3.2 to 4.0 and molecular masses from 30 to 43kDa, each containing one mole of protein (Ferrell et al. 1989; Gold and 1993). It possesses a high redox potential and a lower pH optimum than that of any other peroxidase (Caro et al. 1997; Kirk and Hammel et al. 1986). Like other oxidases, LP is capable of oxidizing phenolic compounds through the generation of phenolic radicals. However, due to its exceptionally high redox potential and low pH optimum, it is able to oxidize nonphenolic substrates, typically not oxidized by other perases including the phenolic unit of lipid (Hannel et al. 1986; Hataka 1994; Have et al. 1998). Stable cation centered radicals formed during the oxidation of nonphenolic aromatic nuclei may serve as mediators for LIP-catalyzed oxidation, effectively extending the substrate range. The ability of LIP to attack a variety of linkages suggests that it plays a key role in lignin degradation. The physiological role of which is believed to protect LIP from H₂O₂-dependent inactivation to the native state. This is of particular significance; since during oxidation of cationic chemicals, LIP is shown to accumulate, indicating that they are either substrates for LIP or they lack the ability to revert LIP to the native state (Caro and August 1995; Harvey and Palmer 1990). The oxidation of such chemicals is inefficient at high H₂O₂ concentrations.

### 4.1.2 Catalytic Cycle

The catalytic cycle of LIP is similar to that of other peroxidases (Perangan and Gold 1986; Tien et al.).

[img/51691e3be3fcbe0a14a6eae361d5da7_386_0.png]

Reaction of reactive enzyme species by LIP (Fe³⁺, Fe²⁺, porphyrin) forms a complex of hexacoordinate porphyrin cation (Fe⁴⁺ = O₂, P). One-electron oxidation of a reducing substrate (SH) by LIP yields a radical (S•) and the one-electron-oxidized intermediate, LIP-III (Fe⁴⁺ = O₂, P). A single one-electron oxidation of the radical returns the enzyme to LIP, completing the catalytic cycle.

However, in the case of suitable reducing substrate or at high H₂O₂ concentrations, LIP is further oxidized by H₂O₂ to LIP-compound III (LIPIII; Fe⁴⁺ = O₂, P), a species with limited catalytic activity.

### 4.1.3 Role of Veratryl Alcohol

LIP-catalyzed oxidation

The LIP has been shown to readily return to ferric state in the presence of H₂O₂ and veratryl alcohol (3,4-dimethylbenzyl alcohol; VA) (Barr and August 1994; Cai and Tien 1998; Warish and Gold 1990). Nonphenolic aromatic compounds other than VA, such as 3,4-dimethylamino, 1,2-4-dimethylbenzene have also been shown capable of mediating oxidation (Teunissen and Field 1998; Ward). The peroxidation may be driven by the potential (OP) and site-binding affinity (possessing higher OP values and higher affinity) and the substrates (possessing lower value and lower affinity) (Ward et al. 2002).
