## 2.1.2 PGPF in Mycelial Fungi

Plant growth has been obtained from isolates of mycelial fungi that do not produce spores. Some identified fungi have been reared from sterile black fungi (SBF), sterile dark fungi (SFDF), and red fungi (SRF), and have been isolated from root crops, wheat, and grass varieties. Isolates identified to be SBF (Spekman and Kruger 1984) and similarly identified isolates to SRF increased showed shoot growth in weight in wheat (Dewan and Sivasanthamaram 1989). Growth promotion by these identified fungi has been reported on other plants and crops, and resulted in typical weight in barley (Spekman and Kruger 1984). PGPF considered as SRF have been reared to promote growth of rye, rice, grass, lettuce, median, nowadays, and all of which were isolated and measured as shoot weight (Dewan and Sivasanthamaram 1989). Isolates used in these studies have not been identified, although isolates of SRF are thought to be Basidiomycetes because of the presence of clamp connection. Strains of SBF and SRF were isolated from herbal plants and all with their relationships to endophytic fungi are being considered.

The duration of the plant-growth promoting effect of PGPF in treated plants is an important factor for the evaluation. Increased growth responses were observed in wheat during the seedling stage (2 weeks after sowing), vegetative stage (4 weeks), pre-flowering stage (6 weeks), and seed stage (8 to 14 weeks) (Yamakuchi 1994; Shivanna et al.). All isolates used increased plant height, and also increased the ear-head length, weight, seed number, and biomass (Table 1) (Shivanna 1994).

In various applications, it is important to isolate PGPF that have high affinity for plants and can colonize their rhizosphere; (b) high levels of plant growth, and (c) consistent performance in fields trials. An ideal isolate is a requirement for isolates that show specific effectiveness with an individual plant host.

## 2.2 Mechanisms of Plant Growth by PGPF

Several hypotheses have been put forward for the mechanism of plant growth by PGPF, including (a) hormone production, (b) substrate degradation (mineralization), and (c) suppression of deleterious microorganisms.

## 2.1.3 PGPF in Rhizosphere

A particular strain of R. solani has shown growth promotion and significantly increased yield for varieties in field experiments (Snec et al. 1986). Included included wheat and weight of radish roots and carrot roots, which increased weights of cotton fiber and wheat grains. In experiments with potato, although increases were observed in shoot and tuber when 63-70 days after transplanting, there was no increase in yield at the time of harvest. Some isolates of binucleate Rhizonia have been found to be PGPF (Harris et al. 1993; Vilaguban et al.).

Close relationships have been shown between the reduction of barley grain weight by PGPF, the subsequent growth promoting effect of PGPF, and their cellulase and degradation activity (Yamakuchi 2000). Effect of MH-N and NG-N also were increased by amendment with PGPF-infected barley grains.

The total amount of nitrogen in PGPF infected-barley grains remains the same despite when PGPF isolate is used; however, the amount NH-N varies depending on the isolate, with the highest level seen in grains infected with Phoma. The NH-N levels decreased in the order Phoma > Penicillium > Trichoderma > content. The amount of NH-N was 7.8 times higher in Phoma-infested barley grains than control (Hayumaguchi 2000). ## 2.2.3 Subversion of Deleterious Microorganism

A remarkable growth promotion effect has been reported for field-green cucumbers, and this was attributed to the endophytic spp. in the soil by PGPF (Hayumaguchi 1994). The suppression of deleterious microorganisms by PGPF may be one of the mechanisms of plant-growth promotion.

## 2.3 Mycorrhiza

As a definition of mycorrhiza, Smith and Read (1997) proposed "a system in which an external mechanism of mycorrhizal fungi supplies nutrients to a plant root." Some plants mycorrhize in order to control their life cycle.
