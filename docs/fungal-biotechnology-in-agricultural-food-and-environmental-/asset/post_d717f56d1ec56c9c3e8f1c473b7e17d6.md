## 7.1 Protecting Soil

If disturbance event can be prevented, in the case of mining or logging, it may be possible to minimize the adverse effects on mycorrhizal fungi through topsoil protection. In work by Miller et al. (2009), fungi persisted for years after the trees were felled; new seedlings could tap soil if it remains. Since most mycorrhizal fungi are present in the upper soil profile, topsoil serves as a reservoir for invading inoculum, as well as a hospitable environment for invading roots. The soil should allow for intense inoculation effects after disturbance. For example, when disturbance to be solved by minimizing or eliminating activities that affect mycorrhiza-associated soil and organic matter to stabilize soil and reduce erosion. This protects intact fungal networks that can repair mycorrhiza on uncolonized roots of neighboring plants (Francis et al., 2006; Smith and Harvest). In situations where soil is disturbed, the environment to preserve mycorrhiza may be salvaging the topsoil before disturbance and transferring it away from the site to another area (Allen et al. 1999). However, the benefits of topsoil are linked to the length of time, the soil without plant cover necessary to sustain symbiotic relationships. Soil may be protected for several years, during which organic matter can recover (Schwartzman, 1990; Kundu and Gove, 1997), and mycorrhizal fungi can grow greatly (Miller et al. 1985). After soil recovery, or poor recruitment may further hinder mycorrhizal establishment. Protecting the cover of organic and soil stockpiles could ensure the survival and establishment of mycorrhizal fungi on restoration sites.

## 7.2 Soil Amendments

Improving the quality of organic matter is an important management option for enhancing mycorrhizal development (Allen et al. 1999). Compost, bark, or some other carbon source provides a source of nutrients while keeping soil moisture, facilitating infiltration and reducing soil compaction. These conditions are not only beneficial for mycorrhizal fungi, but also for many microorganisms that improve nutrient availability and stability. For AM particularly, the absence of organic matter following severe disturbance limits the establishment of mycorrhizae despite adequate dispersal to the site (Allen et al. 1992; Read 1984). The use of soil amendments could therefore alleviate the need for mycorrhizal inoculation.

## 7.3 Maintaining Source Areas

The ability of mycorrhizal fungi to colonize sites from relatively long distances supports the need to maintain source areas with diverse and abundant mycorrhizal populations. The nature of mycorrhization was presumed to be a limited process, relying heavily on soil redistribution of fungi from disturbance edges. However, Allen et al. (1989); Warner et al. (1987) demonstrated that both wind and animals can disperse spores as far as 2km across disturbed landscapes. On Mt. Helens, widely dispersed AM inoculum, animals were necessary for dispersal of AM (Allen et al. 1992).

## 7.4 Maintaining Natural Reinforcement

Of diverse array of AM and EM fungi that potentially inhabit a site, relatively few are used to replace the that lost under disturbance. Maintaining a natural reinforcement may be critical for the restoration process. In arid regions, mycorrhizal fungi colonize across sites by wind and animals (Allen et al. 1987). Evaluating and maintaining these factors could potentially enhance the rate of mycorrhizal recovery. For example, Allen et al. (1989) found that wind and animal dispersal of spores depends on understanding the physical and biological characteristics of a site. Knowing potential source and sink areas, it is possible to prevent the trapping of blowing spores using artificial barriers such as fences or fancing the distances between individual plants by creating islands of fungal inoculum (Allen et al. 1999).

Animal activity during sites is also important to mycorrhizal recovery. Animals disperse fungal propagules, either by eating (Allen et al. 1987; Blache and Rabbit 1998) or by moving soil and root material carrying propagules (Allen and Mac 1988; Fries et al. 1993). Numerous animals consume the hypogeous bodies (truffles) of EM fungi and disperse these new colonies introduced to the soil of many habitats (Allen et al. 1997). Pocket fungi (Tommy alphotes) and Ant (Pogonomycetes mycelium) move substantial amounts of soil, spores, roots, fragments, and propagules to the surface (Allen et al. 1998). This method establishes refuge and favorable conditions for late-seasonal obligate mycorrhizal plant species (Allen 1987). The dispersal of mycorrhizal propagules via animal activity could play an influential role in determining rates and patterns of plant reestablishment success.

Despite the benefits of mycorrhizal associations, although has not been used on a commercial scale. For commercial development, large quantities of inoculum must be produced. Since AM are obligate biotroph, must be produced and grown on living plants. This is problematic because of the high risk of plant pathogens and contaminants entering the culture system (Jasriel and Silva 1997). In contrast, EM fungi may grow on agar media in the base of a host root or the vegetative material of fruiting bodies collected directly from plants (Molina and Palmer 1982).

The first step in cultivating mycorrhizal fungi is producing the stock of individual fungal isolate on host plants. For AM fungi, spores or colonized roots from the host are used to produce larger quantities of inoculum for growth on suitable substrates (Fritz 1990). Although large amounts of EM fungi can be easily collected in the field, spores are rarely used to isolate EM fungi. Instead, many inoculation programs use the EM vegetative material for its effective growth and storage on agar (Molina and Palmer 1982).
