## 4.2 Adaptive Evolution of Wine Yeast Strains

Although the origin of S. cerevisiae is a matter of controversy, its genome has been subjected to selective pressures since its uncontrolled use in fermentation processes. Useful phenotypic traits, such as fast growth in sugar-media, high alcohol production and tolerance, good flavor production, which have been selected over billions of generations, have had strong influence on the S. cerevisiae genome (Pérez-Orín et al.; 2002). We will now analyze the molecular mechanisms that explain this yeast adaptation to vinification.

### 4.2.1 Stress Adaptation

During the fermentation phase, yeast cells are exposed to a number of stresses (Attfield 1997), the most important being osmotic and ethanol stresses. Osmotic stress is due to the high salt content in which yeast cells must resist.

### 4.2.2 Gene Expression in Wine Yeast

The molecular basis of the technological properties of yeast strains are largely unknown. However, the obvious possibility is that the adaptation of these strains to the environment depends on selection profiles of the genes (for a review see Pérez-Orín et al.; 2002). Comparative analysis of gene expression induced by environmental stresses in industrial strains shows that different industrial strains involved in the process of the strains in industrial environments. To date, the study of gene expression during wine fermentation has focused on genes induced in the stationary phase to express specific activities at the end of the process (Pug et al.; 1996; Pug et al.; 2000; Riu).

Due to the differences of the particular yeast strains, it seems of great interest to study real commercial wine strains. Pérez-Orín (2000) determined the level and expression patterns of genes in wine vinifications using a commercial strain (T3, Lindenberg). Genes such as POT1, HSP1, and SSA3, which are expressed late in laboratory cultures, are expressed in wine cells only during the first few vinifications. The reason for this could be the very different conditions used in vinification (and therefore in real verifications) compared to laboratory conditions with high vent content (20–25%) versus the usual laboratory conditions (1–3), and highly aerobic conditions (Puig et al. 1996).

The sequence of the S. cerevisiae revealed the development of tools for the evaluation of the expression of genes encoded in the genome (transcriptome). The DNA microarray analysis allowed us to investigate how expression changes during fermentation process; several attempts have been made with yeast (Cavalleri et al. 2000; Hauser et al. 2001). Knowledge of genetic features well as the specific expression profiles of yeast strains under different conditions could help us understand better the biological process at the molecular level and how the gene expression is regulated in relation to changes in the physical and chemical properties of the medium. It is important to note that the genes SUC1/SUC2 (sugar efflux, SUC1) upregulated in resistant strain (Hofmann et al., 2000) were found to be overexpressed in wine strains. They investigated in detail the possible mechanisms of the regulation of the LSU1 gene of the T3 yeast strain. A rearrangement of the promoter of LSU1 was detected, leading to up-regulation expressed as a result of the selective pressures imposed by winemaking (Pérez-Orín et al. 2002).

### 4.2.3 Wine Yeast Genome

The flexibility of the genome to adapt to introduced environmental changes has been demonstrated in several experiments. It has postulated that an duplication of the entire yeast genome has been instrumental in its evolutionary adaptation to anaerobic conditions by providing redundant gene functions (Wolfe and Sloeh 1997). Yeast strains have the ability to adapt their rhizomes during mitotic growth (Long and Venzinet 1993). In contrast to laboratory strains, wine strains seem not to generate uniformly under genetic pressures. A reason for this is the ability of many "natural" strains to change their mating type when haploid (homothalling). Using this capability, reduction, mating type of the daughter cells and conjugation with any of the same-spore colony (Mortlier et al. 1994). This process (called "genome renewal") would produce highly homogeneous strains with deleterious mutations by natural selection. Yeast strains, are usually aneuploid and heterozygous for many loci (Bakalinskaya and Novo 1990; Guijo and Benson 2000), which is not observed during genome renewal (Puig et al. 2000). There are other ways in which yeast genome can change after point. Some mutations, which occurs at low rates, mitotic chromosomal transitions (Rachidi et al. 1998; Sox and Lee 1985) will provoke further adaptation to environmental changes (Puig et al. 2000). Extra copies of chromosome XIII, which contains the alcohol dehydrogenase genes ADH2 and ADH3, are found in wine strains, which are of interest to observe. Martin et al. 2007 found that the SUU1-R allele, which confers resistance to yeast cells, is the result of a reciprocal translocation between chromosomes VIII and XIII under gene-overcrossing-mediated by recombination between the 5 upstreams of the LSU1 and ECM4 genes, which contain the PSU1 coding region of the ECM4 promoter. They showed that this translocation is present in wine strains, suggesting that the use of such a preservative in wine production might have favored its selection. This is the first that a chromosomal rearrangement has been shown to in adaptive evolution of S. cerevisiae.
