## INTRODUCTION

The world of mushrooms, owing to their sudden appearance in numbers, groups, rings, bunches, and also in isolation as a single attractive and imposing structure, has fascinated the man since time immer and references are available in the scriptures of many ancient civilizations. Theofastrus wrote about food of mushroom when the Greek and Roman emperors found it in the royal dishes before the Homo sapiens, as evident from the fossil records of the late Cretaceous period, is about 130 million years ago; it is said that the primitive man collected and consumed mushrooms from the wild in many regions of the world and the scenario changed after successful cultivation of mushrooms. The Chinese are reported to have cultivated specialty mushrooms such as Auricularia, Flammulina and Lentinus between 6000 AD, but it was the edible mushroom (Agaricus bisporus) in France around the year 1650 transformed the world of mushroom production.

Commercial production of fungi represents exploitation of technology wherein wastes (agricultural, industrial, and household) are efficiently converted into nutritious food. Indoor cultivation of fungi is arguably the highest producer per unit area and almost 100 times more than conventional agriculture and husbandry. Without pressure to meet the demand for the human population, the rate of mushroom production would be slow. About 2000 types are being cultivated and abundant in marketable quantities:
- Common mushroom (Agaricus bisporus)
- Oyster mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus)
- Shiitake (Lentinus edodes)
- Black ear mushroom (Auricularia)
- Silver mushroom (Flammulina)
- Liver mushroom (Tremella)
- Hairy mushroom (Hypsotus)
- Medicinal mushrooms such as Maitake (Grifola) and Reishi (Ganoderma lucidum).

## WORLD PRODUCTION OF MUSHROOMS

The rapid development of mushroom technology from primitive culture to the last century is a success story which has kept pace with the ever-increasing demand for this commodity and there is every reason to be optimistic about its future (Verma, 1997). From 2 million tonnes in 1980, the mushroom production has registered a threefold increase in a decade and was about six million tonnes in 1997, namely A. bisporus, Pleurotus spp., V. volvacea, and Auricularia spp.

It is clear that human mushroom (A. hostarius) is the leader, contributing 31.8% to the total production but that share was 56.2% in 1986 has decreased over the years. China is the biggest producer of Lentinus, Pleurotus, Auricularia, Flammulina, Tremella, and India are also major producers. Japan produces quantities of Agaricus and production is concentrated in three geographical regions—Europe, North America, and East Asia. In Europe, the UK and Italy are other major producers and in East Asia, the giant India and Taiwan are all other major producers.

It is quite interesting that six countries (Germany, UK, Italy and France) consume more than 80% of world consumption; per capita consumption in these countries is very low (2–3 kg). One indicator of demand for mushroom is the income level of population in the G7 nations. Rising income level is a global phenomenon and the demand for mushrooms is expected to grow at a faster rate for specific groups suffering with physiological disorders or ailments like obesity, diabetes, hypertension, hyperuricemia, constipation, etc. (Rai, 1997).
