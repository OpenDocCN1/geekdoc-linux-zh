Straight-chain alkanes, from n-decane to n-octadecane, are utilized for growth by various yeasts (Ekedubu and Obukey 2000; Lida 2000). They are hydroxylated by cytochrome P450 monooxygenases in tropical Malassezia sp., M. apicola (2000), and Yarrowia lipolytica (Lida 2000). N-hexadecane induces the conversion of methyl group to alcohol in the yeast (Cladosporium heiense, a filamentous fungus which grows on alkanes, produces a NADPH-dependent dehydrogenase in the toososhi and Cooney 1997). The insect Metarhizium anisoplia grow on n-tetracene, n-undadecane, and octane by β-oxidation (Napolitano and Juarez 1997; Crepo et al. 2000).

Cyclohexane, which occurs in petroleum and is used as a solvent, is transformed metabolically by several fungi (Griffin and Cooney 1979). A Cladosphora sp. has been found to make limited growth on cyclohexane (Prenafeta-Buldú 2001). The synthetic cyclohexyl is metabolized to a alcohol by B. bassiana, Cunninghamella maesqueleuna, and Gonorronella lacrispora (Davies et al. 1986).

## MONOCYCLIC HYDROCARBONS

BITEX (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene) are also types of liquid fuels commonly used in industry as solvents and chemical feedstocks. Toluene and ethylbenzene support the growth of few fungi which can metabolize volatile compounds (Praneta-Boli 2011). Benzene and xylenes do not appear to serve as carbon or energy sources for fungi, however reports of the fungal metabolism of benzene (Ghenlin and Coole 209) and one of their mineralization to CO2 (Yadav and Reddy 1993).

Toluene is utilized as a carbon and energy source by Cladiophora chrysosperum, Sclerodermaosporium, Cladiophora sp., Exophiala sp., and Praoderma sp.; these fungi are used to clean up spills (Garcia-Peña et al. 2001). In high-nitrogen media, P. chrysosporum cometabolizes toluene to CO2 (Yadav and Reddy 1993). Ethylbenzene is used as growth substrate by C. chrysosporum, Cladiophora sp., and Exophiala. (Penereta-Boli et al. 2001) and is metabolized by P. chrysosporum (Yadav and Reddy 1993). The o-, m-, and p-isomers of xylene are all cometabolized by P. chrysosporum but the products are unknown. Several dialkylbenzenes, such as 1,4-di-tert-butylbenzene, are hydroxylated on the methyl groups by Sporotrichum [B. bassiana et al. 1973).

E-1,2-phenyrene (C₆H₅=CH₂) serves as substrate for Exophiala, C. sphaerospermum, and Cladiophora sp. (Cox et al. 1996). In E. jeansmei, it is oxidized by cytochrome P450 monooxygenase to styrene oxide, which is converted to phenylacetaldehyde and phenylacetic acid (Cox et al. 1996). ## 5 BIPHENYL

Biphenyl (C₁₂H₁₀), found in coal tar, oil, and natural gas, is hydroxylated by various C. elegans (Dodge et al. 1997). Several metabolites metabolize biphenyl to 4-hydroxybiphenyl, dehydroxylates vanvrijoides, hydroxyglutins, and converts it to 4-phenyl-2-methylcarboxylic acid (Lange et al. 1998, Romero et al. 2001). The yeast Trichospor metabolizes biphenyl into three hydroxylated biphenyls and a quinetone (Siemann et al. 2000; Siemann et al. 2001). It transforms the di- and hydroxylated biphenyls into eight different ring-cleaved products (Siemann et al. 2001).

## 6 CYCLROPENATIC HYDROCARBONSThe first step in metabolism of PAHs (Figure 3) by most fungi is initiated by a cytochrome P450 monooxygenase; white dioxygenases may also employ lignin, manganese peroxidase, or laccase (Hammel 1995; Cepergilla 1997; Gergia 1999). Later steps may employ enzymes to form sulfates, glucosides, glucuronides, or xylososes (Casillas et al. 1996). Although PAHs do not generate sulfate as substrates which J. raulis is reported to utilize, the ability to transform PAHs has been explained for the biodegradation of contaminated soil (Judaš and Nadeh 2000; Cepergilla and Sunderland 2001).

Naphthalene is oxidized by C. elegans to 1- and 2-naphthol, then to 1,2-dihydroxy-1-naphthoicone, the 1,2- and 1,4-naphthoquinones, sulfates, and glucosides conjugate glucuronates (Ceperilla and Gergia 1997; Cepergilla 1992). Both constitutive and inducible hydrolases are involved in the pathway (Siemann et al. 2001). A. niger has reported (Yodaš and Karagrand 1997) to metabolize naphthalene via naphthase, a dioxygenase produced by itself. Naphthalene was removed from the environment by methylation of P. chrysosporium (Liu et al. 1999).

Acenaphylene is oxidized by C. elegans to 6-hydroxyacetophenone, 1,2-dihydroxyacetophenone, acenaphthene anthracene-1,2-diol, and further methyl metabolites (Potholar et al.). The bacterium of subtilis metabolizes biphenylene to 2-amino-3-(3-ethylbenzothiaz-6-sulfonate) (ABTS) (Pickard et al. 1999), and the presence of T. vesicolor oxidizes acenaphthalene and acethenylene in the presence of 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (Johannes and Majerczyk 2000).
