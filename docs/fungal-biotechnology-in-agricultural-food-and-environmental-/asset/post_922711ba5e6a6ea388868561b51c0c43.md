## 3 WHAT IS A NEMATOPHAGOUS FUNGUS?

Nematodes constitute a food source for the nematophagous (nematode-destroying) fungi. Life stages of nematodes (eggs, juveniles, adult, and feeding females) can be attacked, penetrated, and digested by several types of nematophagous fungi (e.g., Booth et al. 2001). Nematodes of vermiform morphology (adults and root-knot nematode females) may also be invaded by nematodes as either (saprophytic) fungi, but the latter may not be regarded as nematode-destroying but as nematode-trapping fungi, by the fungi enter the natural openings (mouth, anus, etc.) of the nematodes and do not penetrate their cuticles (Nordberg-Hertz and Stålhammar-Carlelm 1997), and thus represent the saprophytic source of these fungi. Most nematophagous fungi are divided depending on their mode of infecting nematodes: nematode-trapping (Figure 2A), endoparasitic (Figure 2B), egg- and female-parasitic (Figure 2C), and toxin-producing fungi. The result is always-a complete digestion of the host. Nematode-trapping fungi capture vermiform in special organs formed on the hypha (Barron 1977). The traps have either an adhesive function to catch the nematode or a penetrating function e.g., Arthrobophthora spp., characterized by low host specificity and parasitic ability. e.g., Drecheria coniopora spp., use their spores to infect the nematode hosts. The spores of these fungi are produced as conidia of various fungi. The nematophagous fungi have high specificity and are mostly obligate parasites. The toxin-producing fungi, e.g., Ple러스 ostrea, immobilize their victims using toxins prior to penetration. Females of fungi all attack vermiform nematode stages.

## ## HABITAT OF NEMATOPHAGOUS FUNGI

### 4.1 Soil

The nematophagous fungi are inhabitants of most soil types from tropical to arctic areas of the planet, although they are most frequent in organic soils. One to five species of nematophagous fungi are fully recovered from a single sample. Their abundance ranges from 1–180 per gram of soil (Parsons et al. 1996). These variations are due to “environmental factors,” *e.g.*, soil pH, organic matter, water content, and presence of nematode hosts. Most nematophagous fungi are facultative parasites that have the capacity to live in soil matter. Therefore, soil should obviously be seen as the ultimate sink of nematophagous fungi. The nematophagous fungi, in spite of their saprotic ability, are not good competitors in soil. Therefore, the capacity to invade other organisms—important for their survival in the soil. The interactions with these potential hosts will be discussed in the following sections.

## 5 MODE OF ACTION OF NEMATOPHAGOUS FUNGI

The rhizosphere is a habitat with high microbial activity due to release of nutrients from root exudates and decaying cells. Like other micro-organisms, nematophagous fungi are frequent in the root zone, with up to 19 times more propagules in the surrounding rhizosphere soil (Persmark and Jansson 1997). Numbers of nematodes are also higher in the rhizosphere than in bulk soil. Since plant-parasitic nematodes attack roots hosts in the rhizosphere biology of nematophagous fungi is important in order to use nematophagous fungi for biological control of such nematodes. Nematodes are generally attracted to hosts by specific or unspecified compounds, e.g., CO2. Green nematodes like *A. oligospora*, grow chemotropically towards plant roots (Bordallo et al. 2002). Egg-parasitic and nematode-trapping fungi have been found to grow epiphically in epidermal and cortical cells of plant roots (C) (Bordallo et al. 2002; Lopez-Lorca 2002). Root colonization by nematophagous fungi will be further discussed in “Fungi–Root–Interactions.”

The interactions between nematophagous fungi and their hosts involve several steps from recognition (attraction phenomena, contact), production of adhesives and lytic enzymes, differentiation of infection structures, e.g., appressoria and trapping organs, to host penetration and digestion of the host cells (Tuiled et al. 1992). These events have been studied using a variety of techniques (microscopic, biochemical and molecular). In the following section we will discuss some of these interactions with view of the hosts of nematophagous fungi: the nematode and the plant.
