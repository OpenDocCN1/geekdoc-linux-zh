The proportions of C:N:P of 50:1:5 were used in growing filamentous fungi in sausage silage (Araijo et al. 1977; Le and De Mendezes 1978), while 40:10:1 was optimal for A.ioides in the sandy substrate (Rosemael et al. 1985). Cells from other plants can inhibit substrate fermentation, or the microbes may be suppressed under suboptimal conditions. The quality of inoculum for fermentation is 10% of the broth growth. Previous addition of the microbial strain to silage may have a positive effect on biomass produced in submerged shakes and flasks of laboratory experiments. Large-scale experiments using volumes of 9m³ (Caleb et al. 1983) and 80m³ (Wang et al. 1980) have been reported. Batch, fetch, continuous or continuous operations were used in trials fermenting single fungi, mixed cultures of microorganisms or a two-stage cultivation with successive cultivation of different microorganisms have been used. Cell recycling proved be beneficial for higher yields of biomass as well as for better production of organic acids (Wang et al. 1985; Wang et al.). Cultivation temperatures were kept between 22 and 38°C, suitable for microorganisms; pH controlled at the level often proved to be beneficial to the microorganisms during fermentation. Aeration rates of 0.5–1 vah were generally employed, being the very high aeration rates in materials or lower aeration rates of 1 vah (Wang et al. 1980) in large scale experiments with dilution rates of 0.01h⁻¹ (Yang and Tung 1990) to 0.38h⁻¹ (Wang et al.). The dilution rate could be caused by the cell-recycling (Wang et al. 1980). In processes the fermentation time is dependent on temperature and ranged from several days to two years, with days and months, and up to 12 days with mushrooms. After fermentation the yeast biomass is generally harvested by centrifugation, with filamentous fungi silage filtration is possible.

The level of reduced COD in terms of relative values and the absolute reduction is dependent on the initial value.

Filamentous fungi are cultivated mostly in batch processes. Compared with yeasts, filamentous fungi are more effective in consuming substances, since COD reduction of 80% and 90%, respectively, have been reported (Araijo et al. 1977; Ruiz et al. 1975) in sausage silage, Penicillium strains in wine mash (Gay and Marchant 1980) to be the most promising in this process. The highest amount of biomass was obtained by A. niger in distillery waste (Morimura et al. 1994). It was also observed that the protein content in the biomass of filamentous fungi was lower than of yeasts. Candida, however, was found to produce the highest protein content; the level of protein was determined by the biuret method. Overview is given in Table 4.

In general, mixed cultures were the most effective in substrate utilization, based on COD. A value of 10–75 g/L produced, in high yeast molasses silage of 17.5 g/L in baseline. While dilution produced the highest amount of biomass, the biomass yield was much lower (Huyard et al. 1986). A successful approach seems to be two-step cultivation of C. utils and P. paridis by a total amount and a COD reduction of 99%. During the first stage, C. utils was used for SCP production, whereas P. paridis in the second step consuming the organic substances in the liquid phase (Azzam and Heikel 1989; Bottaro-Castilla et al. 1984). Similar results were observed in two-step cultivation of C. utils and A. niger separately (Nidel).

Bioconversion of waste was made by means of substrate, and in addition, it is possible to obtain useful products, such as protein-rich biomass, ethanol, etc. Results of bioconversion by yeasts is given in Table 5.
