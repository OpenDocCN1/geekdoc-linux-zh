## Yeast in the Dairy Industry

T. K. Hansen / M. Jacobsen The Royal Veterinary and Agricultural University, Frederiksberg, Denmark

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Yeast have been isolated from all kinds of dairy products (Fleet 1990; Fleet and Mian 1987; Jacobsen and Narhus 1996; Tilbury et al. 1974). Yeast are used as important starter cultures in food products such as beer, wine, traditional fermented foods (Fleet 1990; Fleet and M 1987; Jacobsen and Narhus 1996) and seem to be the most important micro-organism exploited by man. The role of yeast as micro-organisms, which can have a positive influence on the quality of dairy products has not been widely accepted (Fleet 1990; Jacobsen and Narhus). Instead, yeasts have often been seen as spoilage organisms originating from poor hygiene and sanitation (Fleet and Mian 1987; Jacobsen and Narhus 1996). The nutritional requirements of yeast to grow at low temperature, low pH, moisture content and high sugar or salt concentrations together with their enzymatic activity may outperform a natural part of the microbiota in dairy products. In 1991 the first symposium on "Yeasts in the dairy industry" was organized and held by the International Dairy Federation (IDF) group F47. As reflected in the published literature the interest in yeast in the dairy industry has increased and the knowledge of the positive and negative aspects has become more detailed. The key areas have been addressed: (a) study of important technological characteristics of yeast, e.g., proteolytic and lipolytic activity, aroma formation and fermentation and assimilation of sugars and lactate and specifically related to different kind of cheese; (b) properties affecting both the negative role of yeast as spoilage causing quality defects as well as the potential of yeast as highly controlled starter cultures; (c) yeast classification and taxonomy based upon molecular methods. The present review will deal with the positive and negative aspects of yeast in the dairy industry.

A variety of yeast species has been isolated from milk and dairy products (Fleet 1990; Fleet and Mian 1987; Gadaga et al. 2001; Rohlin et al. 1992; Roostita and Fleet 1996; van Tempel and Jakob 1998; Tudur and Booth 1993) but a few species are frequently used in dairy products. These yeasts are representatives of the genera Debaryomyces, Kluyveromyces, Yarrowia, Candida, and Saccharomyces (2001). The yeast occurs both in aerobic and anaerobic state depending on the yeast's ability to produce acidity. Most common yeasts used in dairy products mentioned by their anaerobic state are:
- Debaryomyces hansii
- Geotrichum galactiae
- Geotrichum lipolytica
- Kluyveromyces marxexii
- Kluyveromyces sphericalis
- Saccharomyces cerevisiae
- Saccharomyces kefirii

To describe yeast used in dairy products and select at subspecies level is a necessity. The taxonomy of yeast is mainly described in the first and third chapters of the edition titled "The Yeasts" edited by RJ (1984) and Kurtman and Fell (1988). The criteria include micro-morphology in standard media, assimilation and fermentation of carbohydrates, growth at different temperatures and under osmotic pressure, development of pseudo or true mycelium, formation of ascospores as well as other phenotypic traits takes time to correct identification. Former systems such as APIZYM (France) are widely used. The API-test is based on assimilation profiles of yeast. The API-kit was originally made to offer fast identification of medical importance. Several investigations have found related yeasts were not included in the kit (Deak and Beauch 1998). The enlarged version of the test, API-kit, has been found to be reliable and acceptable for rapid identification of yeasts as well as in combination with classical methods, especially when a reliable selection of the strain is required, such as volatile profiles of yeast (Magan et al. 2001), volatile profiles of yeast by an electron nose (Cummer et al. 1998) and identification based on Fourier-Transform infrared spectra for identification at specific yeasts.

The number of yeast and species has increased 100% or more since 1970. The first edition of "Yeast" was published in 1980. The second time yeast was published different genera which have been proven to belong to the same species. The complexity of the yeast taxonomy but also the growing interest in identifying yeasts using molecular methods including whole chromosomes, the classification of yeast is based more on genetics than phylogeny. Several methods have been reported for taxonomic use, e.g., molecular techniques using SDS-gel electrophoresis of whole cell protein, pulsed-field electrophoresis (PFGE) (Martinez et al. 1998; Molin et al. 1999; Jensen and Knudsen (2002, 2003), gel fragment length polymorphism analysis of mitochondrial DNA (DNA-FLP) (Petersen et al. 2002; Romano et al. 1996; van den Appel and Jakobsen (2000)), internal genomic sequencing of ribosomal DNA (rDNA) sequence analysis, PCR and enzyme-restriction fragment length polymorphism (PCR-FLP) of the transcribed spacer regions (ITS) (Caglia et al. 2001; Petersen et al. 2001). However, for many of the yeast related to dairy products the methods are not fully developed or sensitive enough to distinguish species or subspecies.
