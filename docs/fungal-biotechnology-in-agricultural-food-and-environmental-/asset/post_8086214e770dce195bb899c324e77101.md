## Control of Postharvest Diseases of Fruits Using Microbes

Wojciech J. Janisiewicz Appalachian Research Station, U.S. Department of Agriculture–Agricultural Research, Kearnsville, West Virginia, USA

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Losses from postharvest diseases of fruits have been substantial at the storage, retail, and consumers level. The levels are very difficult to establish because research has generally considered one or two levels, and little work has been done to determine losses at the consumer level. Nevertheless, in the United States, losses are estimated to reduce from 5% for citrus to as much as 20% for strawberries (Ebert and Obama 1985). Most of the fruit becomes infected through wounds made during harvest and postharvest handling, but for some, infections take place in the orchard during the growing season, and remain latent. As fruit matures the pathogens become active and again Various methods exist to reduce postharvest decay, including pathogen control by fungicide to reduce wounding (Summer 1982), physical treatments such as hot water and hot air treatments that kill pathogens (Falla et al. 1996), low temperatures or modified atmosphere that stop or reduce growth of the pathogens (Summer 1982), treating with chemicals that enhance natural resistance (El-Gaughman 1998), with synthetic fungicides (Ebert and Ogawa 1985), and more recently, with biocontrol agents (Droby et al. 1998; Janisiewicz and Korsten 2002; Kristen et al. 1995; Using et al. 2001). Fungicides are, by far, the most widely used agent against fruit decay because they are easy to apply and generally effective against most of the pathogens on a specific crop. Storage of some fruits for extended periods, e.g., citrus fruits, is totally dependent on the use of fungicides. Postharvest use of synthetic fungicides has been increasingly curtailed by the perceived hazard to humans and the environment. This has resulted in new regulations restricting or eliminating their use in this country to find and register new fungicides to replace those which postharvest pathogens have developed (Gallino and Kuipers 1994; Ragsdale and Sister 1994). Thus, there has been a need for alternative fungicides. Recently, biological control has emerged as an alternative (Janisiewicz and Korsten 2002). The full potential of biocontrol has not yet been realized because the mechanisms have not been fully understood. Understanding of the mechanisms will eventually help manage and improve the biocontrol system. While some methods have limitations, these may be addressed by combining biological control with other methods (Davidson et al. 2001; El-Gaughman et al. 2000;2000b; Smilanik et al. 1999). In this chapter, the key elements in the development of biological control of postharvest diseases (BCPD) of fruits, the current status and future prospects of BCPD using of fungal and bacterial antagonists are discussed.

## 2 PATHOGENS TARGETED FOR BCPD OF FRITS

The BCPD of fruits can be approached from the perspective of the host plant (different fruits), habitat for the microorganisms (wound, intact surface), and the pathogen's strategy used to infect fruit. The pathogen's strategy has been emphasized most frequently because may economically important postharvest diseases of fruits are caused by necrotrophic pathogens (Denis 1983). These pathogens invade through wounds, and require moisture for spore germination and initiation of the pathogenic process, which makes them vulnerable to competition from surrounding microorganisms. Other mechanisms of biocontrol described later in chapter may also be involved, but revealing evidence suggests that secondary fungi (Janieszk and Korfen 1997) or latent infections that occur in the field are areas to provide biological control against the pathogen that has established a parasitic relationship with the host.
