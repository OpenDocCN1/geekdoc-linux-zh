Soft rot fungi are taxonomically classified in the subdivisions Ascomycota and Deuteromycota. They are active in environments that are too wet for brown-rot fungi, generally in wet environments, but they also degrade plant litter in soils (Blanchette 1995). Soft rots are relatively unspecialized cellulosic fungi in the genera Chaetium, Ceratopsis, and Philophora, etc., they degrade cellulose but only modify lignin.

Two distinct types of soft rot are also recognized (Blanchette 2000). Type 1 is characterized by lignocellululose formed within the secondary wall of wood and Type 2 results in erosion of the secondary wall. The modified cell wall is not degraded in cellulose to lignin by white rot fungi, but may be modified in advanced stages of decay. As decay progresses, cellulose loss occurs and lignin concentrations increase in the residual wood.

# 3 WHITE ROT FUNGI

White rot fungi are the only known that can completely break down lignin to CO2 and H2O (Kirk and Harve 1987). Furthermore, lignin can be degraded as a soft

source of energy and by rot fungus. Degradation of lignin enables them to gain access to cellulose and hemicellulose, as actual carbon and energy source. White rot fungi predominantly degrade from deciduous trees (angiosperms), containing hardwoods.

The many species that white rot are a heterogeneous group that may degrade more or lesser amounts of a specific wall component. Some species preferentially remove lignin from wood, leaving behind patches of decayed cellulose. This is known as selective delignification (Blanchette 1995; 1990). Other species degrade lignin and cellulose simultaneously which is referred to as non-selective delignification. The main white-rot fungi include Phanerochaete versicolor and Trametes versicolor which degrade lignin simultaneously.
