## 6 FOODBORNE PATHOGENS AND MYCOTOXINS

It is estimated that 400 fungi and many of the pathogenic ones threaten the health and availability of food safety. From the above, there are at least 200 confirmed of related pathogens and mycotoxins (see this volume, Chapter 6). Many vegetables, fruits, and seeds lose their color and other quality values due to moisture, infection of spoilage microorganisms. These types of wastage that occur during transport, handling, and redistribution. Food crops and their products can be infected with single or multiple species of toxins and contaminated with levels of toxic metabolites—often ranging in PPB to PPM levels (Jones et al.; 1995; Koshinsky and Khattarioutains, 1992; Koshinsky and Khattarioutains). Advances in diagnostic biotechnology have revolutionized the procedures in the identification of fungi. Biochemical assays have been made faster, reliable, and affordable. Rapid identification of yeasts from years ago has been made less cumbersome because of the cost of sequestering of target fungi from the ingredients and interfering compounds. Addition, biochemical tests which have traditionally been used in the identification of yeasts and filamentous fungi have been made possible through the use of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) technology.

Exposure to these metabolites whether in in combination results in toxicological manifestations and even death. Even by multiple species, their interactions with multiple targets and others produce devastating synergistic effects on living cells and whole (Koshinsky and Khattarioutains, 1994; Koshinsky and Khattarioutains, 2000). The historical significance and still proving fungal consumption threat related to public health comes from the fungi that prefer to grow in cool climates. Claviceps purpurea has been found to spread throughout North and South America, Australia, and Africa (Bandopadhyay et al. 1998). Sourdough is the most fungal threat to the crop in the cool climates where it grows.

Research and development work on understanding fungal presence in food products and processes are rapidly advancing. For the most part of the research in this area is descriptive. However, future work will improve health and safety related of foods such as better shelf life, decreased threats to human health, and increased perseverance of the food.

Huge challenges are posed by mycotoxins such as aflatoxins, trichothecene, and fumonisins that are produced by fungal growth. Identification of mycotoxins and their metabolism and transformation is continuous value. So, far however, breeding of bean and corn cereal plants with various conventional strategies for resistance has been attempted but unsuccessful (Meidner 1997). However, it is possible to understand the nature and source of these toxins and hence prevent nature of reality (Khattarioutains, 1998; Koshinsky and Khattarioutains et al. 1997). With the isolation of target species a better and better relief could be achieved.
