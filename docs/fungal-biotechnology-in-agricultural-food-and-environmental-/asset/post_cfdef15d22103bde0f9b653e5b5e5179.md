# 8 EDIBLE FUNGI AND RECYCLING OF THE WASTES/RESIDUES

Mushrooms are highly perishable and have short shelf life ranging from hours to days depending upon the species and the storage environment. Weight loss, blanching, and microbial spoilage are common undesirable postharvest changes besides many biological and chemical changes (Bao 1997; Saxa 1996; Sax and Rai 1989). Mushrooms require urgent postharvest care like handling, packaging, cooling, transport, and storage under condition. Modified postharvest packaging (MAP), controlled postharvest packaging (CAP), and modified humidity packaging (MHP) of the mushroom have been discussed by Amaswaran and Ghosh (1999). Individual Quality Freedom (IQF) are gaining popularity with other vegetables in the same markets.

Postharvest technology of mushrooms has been dealt with by many authors (Bao 1997; Lal Chaudhary and Sharma 1995; Saxa 1996 and Rai 1989). Mushrooms are delicate, contain 90% water, rich in nutrients and have very active oxygen oxidase and protease activities. Their protective covering of skin is delicate, unlike fruit flesh which is fragile. Postharvest physical and biochemical changes occur at very fast rate. Storage in film packages, sodium coating, cabinet preservation, lactic acid fermentation, irradiation, preservation have been attempted to various levels of success for storage. Dehydration and long-term storage and trade are the most common forms of mushroom; while the most common method for mushroom button, drying is required for storage and is highly efficient. Mushroom mushrooms are also freeze-dried in limited quantities. Improvements in storage delay, cabinet drying, and tunnel technologies of mushrooms are needed to produce better product. For many attempts have been made to produce value-added products like chips, powder, ready-to-use mushroom curry, nuggets, biscuits, etc.; in future we may manage more of such products.

There are very few wastes of lignocellulosic nature of agro-forestry, which cannot be used for growing one or the other mushroom. Poppe (2000) has compiled the information on various-wastes, which have been used for growing mushrooms. Residues left after obtaining the main product (e.g., grains, corn, sugar) of these products may provide many to prevent environmental hazards. Cultivating mushrooms on fungi wastes is one of the unique mechanisms whereby waste is left in one form or the other. The substrate left after growing the mushrooms is often called as "substrate" which is a misnomer and "post-mushroom substrate" (SMS) is a more appropriate term because it is not "spent" and can be further decomposed by set of organisms. Many efforts have been made towards profitable utilization of the PMS. The substrate has been used in agricultural crops (Almaswari and Danai 1997). SMS of mushroom button using casing fraction is being used for growing other mushroom suitable treatments. The PMS of the oyster mushroom is used to feed cattle and is then used for biogas production; slurry of fungus is used in the system of composting which has been used and adopted by the (Levanon and Danai 1997b). SMS, especially of the mushroom button, after suitable treatment like composting, has proved very good measure for cultivation of agricultural crops; As mentioned earlier, the PMS of the oyster mushroom is a very good feed with improved protein content and digestibility. (Rai 2002). Cultivation of edible fungi has the mentioned advantages; it represents the production of food (mushroom) feed, fuel (biogas), and fertilizer (many) by complete recycling of the lignocellulosic wastes of agriculture or forestry origin.

# 9 CONCLUSIONS

Mushroom cultivation is the most important microbial technology, after yeast fermentation, it provides us with food protein products from lignocellulosic wastes of varied species. In future, new varieties are likely to be added to the diversity of the cultivated mushrooms and the production of presently consumed mushrooms will increase with the genetic improvement of the strains. Modern tools will continue to break barriers. Many of strategies in which mushroom cultivation is likely to spread over all world. New methods of culture, preservation, and substrate preparation have long-term usefulness for food, feed, and fertilizer.
