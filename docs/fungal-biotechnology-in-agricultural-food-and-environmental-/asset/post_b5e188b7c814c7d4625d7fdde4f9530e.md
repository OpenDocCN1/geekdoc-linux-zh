
# 2 PRODUCTION OF MYTOXINS BY TOXICOGEN FUNGI

## 2.1 General Condition

Outbreaks of toxicoses associated with the ingestion of moldy foods and feeds by humans and animals have also been recorded in last century. Deaths of livestock were reported from consumption of moldy corn in horses, Illinois, Russia and swine in the United States (Christians and Kaufman 1990). A well-documented example is the disease caused by alimentary toxic aflatoxin (ATA) that has resulted in more than 5,000 humans in the USSR during the Cold War, and the case was due to trichothecotoxins. Modern toxicology was not developed until the discovery of aflatoxin in the 1960s as the causative agent killed more than 10,000 yearly with feeds that fed contaminated. Aflatoxins are types of highly carcinogenic compounds produced by frequently occurring *Aspergillus* species and *A. parasitic*; research has focused attention on mycotoxins for the last forty years which have been identified and characterized, and their biosynthetic origin in various fungi. It is estimated that at least 25% of the world's agricultural product is contaminated with mycotoxins and certain deserts linked to contaminated feeds. Recent evidence shows that indoor air pollution from toxic fungi may play a more widespread role in chronic disease than was thought possible (CAST 2003).

Invasion by fungi and mycotoxins in commodities can occur under favorable conditions in the field (preharvest), at harvest, and during processing, transportation and storage (Bhatnagar et al. 2002). Fungi are frequently found in the field: *A. flavus*, *Alternaria*, *Claviceps purpurea*, *Fusarium*, *F. clavioides* (called by numerous species), *F. gramineum*, and a number of *Fusarium sp.* Most likely invasive species include *Fusarium sp.*, *Alternaria*, *Botryospha*, *Aspergillus*, *M. verricosum*, *Trichotrichum*, as well as *Alterna*. These include *Penicillium*, *P. citrinum*, *P. versicillum*, *P. palitatum*, *P. puberulam*, *P. expansum*, and *P. roefletti*, all which are capable of producing mycotoxins in grains and foods. Other toxicogenic fungi are: *A. parasitic*, *A. flavus*, *A. versicolor*, *A. ochraceus*, *A. fumicilloria*, *A. rubrum*, *F. verticillioides*, *F. tricordei*, *F. nivale*, and other *Fusarium sp.* It is apparent, that the mycotoxin producing fungi belong to three genera: *Aspergillus*, *Fusarium*, and *Penicillium*. However, not all species in these genera are toxicogenic.

## 1.3 Economic Impact of Mycotoxin Contamination

The most obvious negative impact of mycotoxins is an outright loss of crops and affected animals, particularly when a severe outbreak occurs. However, even lower levels of mycotoxins in feed affect animal health by feeding reduction and increased susceptibility to infection. Crops contaminated with mycotoxins at levels far unfit for human consumption in countries, which have various regulatory frameworks for acceptable levels of these compounds. However, humans and animals may encounter severe health or high mortality rates in countries with less regulation or monitoring as a result of consuming mycotoxins in feeds and foods. In addition, costs of medical analysis, quality and regulatory programs, research development, services, lawsuits, and the cost of treatment of illness must also be considered in the overall burden of mycotoxin contamination worldwide. Thus, the economic impact (crop cost) burden is very significant (CAST 2003), and estimated to be $932 million annually from aflatoxins, fumonisias, and deoxynivalenol contamination of crops.

## 2.2 Factors Affect Mycotoxin Production

Genetic and nutritional factors affect the formation of mycotoxins. Depending on the susceptibility of the crop, genetic and seasonal factors, as well as cultivation, harvesting, storage and transportation practices, mycotoxins are found worldwide (D'Mello and McDonald 1997). In the field, weather conditions, plant stress, insect vertebrates, species and load of infected fungi, competition within plant and fungal species, and microbial competition all affect mycotoxin production. Factors such as time of exposure, temperature during exposure, and extent of insect or damage to the commodity prior exposure affect mycotoxin production during field or storage. Chemical factors including the nutritional status of the crops or chemicals (such as fungicides) during crop management could affect fungal populations, and consequently mycotoxin production. The temperature and humidity affecting mycotoxin production may differ from that supporting fungal growth. In general, mycotoxins are optimally produced at 24–28°C, but some toxins such as T-2 toxin is maximally at 15°C. Contamination during crop storage may be affected by changes in temperature and water activity, allow ecological succession of different fungi as water activity and temperature of stored grain changes. During storage and transportation, water activity (aa), temperature, crop damage, time, blending with moldy component and a number of physical and chemical factors, such as aeration (O$_2$ cycles), types of grains, pH, and presence or absence of specific nutrients and inhibitors are important.

## 1.3 Economic Impact of Mycotoxin Contamination

The most obvious negative impact of mycotoxins is an outright loss of crops and affected animals, particularly when a severe outbreak occurs. However, even lower levels of mycotoxins in feed affect animal health by feeding reduction and increased susceptibility to infection. Crops contaminated with mycotoxins at levels far unfit for human consumption in countries, which have various regulatory frameworks for acceptable levels of these compounds. However, humans and animals may encounter severe health or high mortality rates in countries with less regulation or monitoring as a result of consuming mycotoxins in feeds and foods. In addition, costs of medical analysis, quality and regulatory programs, research development, services, lawsuits, and the cost of treatment of illness must also be considered in the overall burden of mycotoxin contamination worldwide. Thus, the economic impact (crop cost) burden is very significant (CAST 2003), and estimated to be $932 million annually from aflatoxins, fumonisias, and deoxynivalenol contamination of crops.
