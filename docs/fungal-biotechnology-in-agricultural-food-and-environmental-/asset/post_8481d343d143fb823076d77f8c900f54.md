Zamir K. Punja Simon Fraser University, Burnaby, British Columbia
Raj S. Utkhede Pacific Agri-Food Research Centre, Agass, British Columbia

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Vegetable crops may be produced as both fresh market and processed commodities and be grown under field conditions, such as greenhouses. A wide range of fungal diseases (Howard et al., 1994), thereby reducing crop yield and quality. Methods for disease control have included the use of chemical agents to reduce pathogen incidence, development of resistant cultivars, as well as the application of fungicides to limit the potential of diseases to damage vegetable crops.

In this chapter, examples of recent successes in biological control of fungal diseases of vegetable crops using fungi and yeasts, by which pathogen control was achieved. In addition, the utilization of biotechnology in the implementation of biological control strategies for disease are reviewed. The rationale for development of strains with enhanced efficacy through genetic manipulation, monitoring characterization of fungal genes and the diseases to be considered in this chapter for biocontrol strategies are described include those caused by pathogenic fungi that infect the seed and damping-off. Examples of these fungi are *Rhizoconia solani*, Pythium sp., Fusarium sp., and Sclerotinia rolfsii, Dymosella bryoniae, and Erysiphe sp. (powdery mildew).

Many fungi and yeast biological control agents have been evaluated against the above pathogens and have been formulated to provide disease control options for vegetable crops. The use of biocontrol agents may be particularly attractive for vegetable crops due to the sensitivity of these crops and the possibility for control of environmental parameters, particularly temperature and relative humidity (Paulitz and Belanger 2001). These are important variables that can significantly influence the efficacy of biological control agents under field conditions. Chemical fungicides provide a level of disease control in the absence of genetic resistance, and control practices to minimize the impact of these diseases and reduce residues in food. For example, chemical fungicides have provided adequate control of many pathogens. However, fungicide resistance, particularly regarding pesticides, and revocation of widely-used fungicides have led to increased activity in the development of biocontrol agents against fungal pathogens.

## 2 CONTROL OF SEED ROT AND DAMPING-OFF DISEASES

Germination of seeds is accompanied by the excretion of host nutrients into the soil environment, which frequently attracts damaging fungi such as *Pythium*, *R. solitary*, and Fusarium. These fungi use the seed and root exudates source for germination and growth, and subsequently colonize the seed and root, causing rot and damping-off of seedlings. These fungi are favored by cool (15–20°C) and moisture. Fungal biocontrol agents have been described which when applied to the surface of seed, to the planting substrate, or when applied shortly after treatment, can utilize host nutrients and exclude the seed and root to compete and exclude the pathogenic fungi. Many of these biocontrol agents secrete hydrolytic enzymes and antibiotics that inhibit the development of the pathogenic fungi. The most widely-researched of these biocontrol agents are antagonistic (*Trichoderma* and Glaucidium species) and nonpathogenic, closely-related fungi (*Pythium*, Fusarium, and Rhizoconia species), as well as mycoparasitic fungi such as Taras, Sordotrichium, and Verticillium (Table 1).

## 3 BIOCONTROL OF ROOT AND CROWN ROTS AND WILT AGENTS

Pathogenic fungi which infect the root system and crown through root hairs, natural openings, or wounds, can colonize these tissues and enter the vasculature, causing decay and death of the plants. Most of these pathogens infect during early stages of plant development, although disease may only be manifested. Fungal biocontrol agents have been described which when applied to planting media can colonize the root, occupy infection sites, and compete with the pathogen. In addition, these agents enhance resistance in the plants through induction of various enzymes and antibiotics that inhibit growth and development. For a potential biological agent to the stage of commercial deployment, numerous criteria must be satisfied and considerable data needed to demonstrate aspects of efficacy, survivability, and scale-up. Aspects of these agents are reviewed in Avis et al. (2001a; Harman 1995; Lyman et al. 1996) and will not be discussed in this chapter. Several chemical companies and numbers of companies with specialized products have invested in the development of biocontrol agents to complement pesticides for the control of diseases on horticultural crops. These products are targeted to markets where they have the best of performance and where there is the most, e.g. for control of seed and root-infections on seedlings. Some commercially available biocontrol agents for plant control are now available (Farrell) and more are likely to be brought to the market. The molecular methods have been described that can be adapted to ensure quality control and monitoring of the biocontrol agents (Avis et al. 2011a).

### 3.1.1 Trichoderma Species Identification

The genus Trichoderma contains species that occur throughout the world. Most species are fast-growing saprophytes with the ability to survive under a range of environments by utilizing different substrates for growth (Hjelestad and Toorn 1998; Samuels). The most common biological agents in the genus have been reported to be strains of *Trichoderma virza*, *T. viride* (Harman et al. 2000).
