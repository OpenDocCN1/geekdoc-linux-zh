By providing a direct link between soil and plants, mycorrhizal fungi could be of great importance in heavy metal soils (Leyval et al. 1997), such as there is increasing interest in the potential for mycorrhizal fungi to be used as remediation agents or as indicators of heavy metal pollution.

It is generally believed that heavy metals inhibit the growth of mycorrhizal associations. Recent studies have shown the viability and activity of EM fungi in soils with different heavy metal concentrations. Richards and Söderström (1990) reported that the number of fruiting bodies and species decreased along a heavy metal gradient. Diversity collected from old mine sites expressed aluminium tolerance and high mycelial growth compared to rehabilitated and forested sites (Earthburton-Wright and Griffin 1995). For AM fungi, there is relatively resistance to high metal concentrations. Gildon and Tinker (1980) reported that plant roots growing on zinc and cadmium-contaminated soils showed high AM colonization. Davies et al. (2001) reported high rates of AM colonization at the most contaminated sites in soils. Rao and Tak (2001) found a significant improvement in root growth and density of EM fungi isolated from mine species when used in such soils.

The mechanism that confers metal tolerance in mycorrhizal fungi is largely unknown. For AM and EM fungi in polluted soil may depend heavily on the density of the hyphae. Other possible mechanisms for heavy metal tolerance in fungi may include metal chelation (Martin et al. 1994) and the sequestration of metals in vacuoles. The stability of metal tolerance in both AM and EM fungi remains to be examined (Leyval et al. 1997).
