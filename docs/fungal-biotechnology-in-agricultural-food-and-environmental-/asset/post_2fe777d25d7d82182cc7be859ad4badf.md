Recently, the ability to synthesize a large number of chemical libraries from core structure was greatly enhanced by the advance of rapid combinatorial/parallel synthesis method (Caporale 1995). The diversity and number of distinct compounds in combinatorial library enhance the possibility of finding a chemical structure with the desired properties. Combinatorial libraries can be synthesized in many ways as reviewed by Rolle (1999). However, most of the successes in combinatorial chemistry were accomplished by using libraries to improve the properties of a specific target. The optimization of azole oxazole compounds suggested a promising future for combinatorial chemistry in biosynthesis (Tritsch 2001).

A number of useful targets have been discovered from the fungal metabolism related to nucleic acid, protein, steroid, and cell-wall biosynthesis. The recent successful example of sordinanol shows that better understanding of biochemistry in fungi could involve more targets for antifungal leads. Approaches in chemical and biosynthesis were suggested to discover the chemical library of microbial metabolites, which can make it easier to discover the compound with desired chemical and biological properties. Trends in developing new biocides will be facilitated and strengthened through multidisciplinary approaches in chemistry.

As another approach to discover the chemical library of microbial metabolites, combinatorial biosynthesis is proposed to generate “natural products, which use genetic information and DNA recombination techniques to alter the pathway of producing chemical structure. This can be done by either enzyme induction or heterologous biosynthesis within the machinery of others (Copies et al. 2002). New structures were generated by inserting enzyme subunits into type I polyketide synthases (PKSs) (Kim et al 2002; McDonald et al 1999; Yoon et al 2003). Most of the microbial biosynthesis was generated by PKSs, in Streptomyces. The recently discovered gene clusters of antifungal antibiotic and 2,4-diacetylphinol were also identified from plant-associated pseudomonads, the well-known beneficial bacteria. These clusters are expected to be used for the future of combinatorial biosynthesis for fungicidal development, although a number of questions about their enzymatic foundations remains to be elucidated.
