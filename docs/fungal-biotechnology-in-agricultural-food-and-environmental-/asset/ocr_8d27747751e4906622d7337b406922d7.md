Most of the oxidative reactions observed are presumed to be one-electron oxidation of suitable substrates to phenolic radicals. Nonphenolic substrates undergo radical cationic intermediates. The result is the formation of oxygen-ring products, small aromatic fragments, and quinones and these can be further metabolized.

# 4.1 Lignin Peroxidase

A lignin-degrading enzyme from F. chrysosporium was identified by three independent research teams (Glenn et al. 1983; Tien and Kirk 1983). This enzyme was also generally known as lignin peroxidase, it can depolymerize lignin and cleave Co-O linkages in sites suggesting that the polymerization and cleavage ability of lignin occurs during wood degradation (Chen and Kirk 1988)

The enzyme has also been found in other fungi C. Versicolor (Dorschel et al. 1987; Johnson et al. 1987; Wagner et al. 1988), Praduta (Hataka et al. 1987; Kautinen et al. 1988; Niko-Paavola 1987; Niko-Paavola et al. 1988) and P. treemulus (Bas-Hawkes et al. 1987).

# 4.2 Manganese Peroxidase

One year after discovery of lignin peroxidase, Kuwahara et al. (1984) reported the isolation of another enzyme from F. chrysosporium ME 346. This formation showed Mn(II), H2O2 and lactate dependency on increased peroxide concentration. The enzyme can oxidize a variety of substrates, including phenol, d-oxidation, and Poly R. Purpose characterization of this enzyme is presumed de manganese peroxidase.

A similar enzyme, vacuoxidase was found in BKM-1677 (Paszynski et al. 1985). This enzyme oxidizes low-molecular weight phenols and aromatic acids produced by Mn(II) and H2O2. It does not oxidize phenol red and is activated by NADPH rather than glutathione, providing a link to xenobiotic metabolism. Generation of peroxide was formed in lignin with manganese peroxidase from GSH under oxidative conditions.

Manganese accumulation in the decayed residue of wood (Blanche 1984) supported the hypothesis that manganese dependent peroxidases participate in lignin degradation (Paszynski et al. 1985) via oxidation to a higher oxidation state (Glenn et al. 1986; Paszynski et al. 1986). Manganese

peroxide oxidative reduction of Mn(II) to Mn(III) was subsequently demonstrated (Glenn et al. 1986).

# 4.3 Laccase: Role and Function in Lignin Biodegradation

Laccases as polyphenoloxidases are widely known as p-diphenoloxidases (EC 1.10.3.2) (Tuer et al. 1994 and 1995). The enzyme is found in many plant species (Mayer and Harel 1979) and is widely distributed in fungi (Mayer 1997) wood-rot fungi. It is often associated with lignin degradation, manganese peroxidase, or Nordic (Nerud and Misúrcová 1996). They are not remarkably specific to reducing substrates and are common with other copper-containing enzymes, tyrosinase (molybdoxidase, EC 1.14.3.1).

Laccases are blue oxidases and participate in electron transfer in biological systems by catalyzing the reduction of oxygen to water with various organic substrates (Reinhardt and Malminen 1994). The protein contains four copper atoms, which facilitate efficient catalytic sites. The Type I (blue) copper center has a strong absorption and gives a characteristic blue color. The Type II (normal) and the Type III involve a multiple of coupled copper ions (Fer and Peri 1981; Morpopo et al. 1993). Studies into the mechanism show that the His bridge between the binuclear center and the Type II forms a trinuclear cluster as the active site (Solomon 1988).

Phase mechanism has been investigated on Red versicolor. It is found that the enzyme has a protective function, causing oxidation of a natural polymeric lignin in the case of tree damage (Reinhardt and Malminen 2001). A plant laccase was involved in lignification (Ba et al. 1993) and the role of laccase has been extensively reviewed by O'Malley et al.

Fungal laccases oxidize phenol and phenolic-structures of lignin with subsequent polymerization or degradation (White et al. 1997). They are considered to proceed via phenolic pathways. The unit of lignin biodegradation remains largely untried, since most of the extensively studied wood-rot fungi, F. chrysosporium, and other Basidiomycetes do not produce lignin in lignolytic conditions
