More recent studies have attempted to examine and accommodate the complex and coordinated behavior exhibited by mycelium. A stochastic cellular automation for modeling the dynamics of two-species microcommunities revealed the significance of local and nonlocal interactions in generating the behavior of mycelial systems (Bown et al. 1999). Furthermore, the experimental system allowed for detailed spatial analysis of the communities which revealed that interaction outcome depends on patch size, fine scale dynamics were highly dependent on nonlocal interactions (Bown et al.; Syrock et al. 2002). Furthermore, experimental studies indicate that fungal competition is more intense in smaller domains occupied by the same species, and in vitro where growth may be less than that growth (Holmer and Stahl 1953). Consequently, in in vitro studies for understanding the factors that influence the dynamics of fungi, a major limitation exists in how small-scale studies relate to the behavior at larger community scales. At larger scales it is probable that contrasting emergent behaviors arise because individuals in a group behave differently than individuals are isolated. Processes such as modification of the environment, translocation and typical anastomosis (Rayer 1996) may influence community development. Issues relating to scale can be a central problem in ecology over the last fifty years. Nevertheless, the importance of appreciating the relationships across different scales should be emphasized, as under- and large-scale ecosystem events, Levin (1992). Therefore, in the development of a complete understanding of ecology it is necessary to address issues relating to scale and adopt a hierarchical framework (Levin and Safif 1997).

In vitro mycelia studies, particularly those of spatial interaction, offer the map of predicting or ex situ fungal community dynamics. Furthermore, these have focused on the center of the patch rather than limited patches under experimentally defined environmental conditions (Boddy 2000). Data produced from competitive interaction are used to rank species in order of their competitive ability hence indicate sequences of fungal communities in the field. Correlation between fungal behavior in artificial and the natural environment is mixed, and may be related to the spatial or temporal scale at which the data are collected or aggregated. Nevertheless, studies represent a valuable approach to understanding fungal ecology, that that limitations are recognized qualified and considered. According toget hierarchies may be transitive (following a strict linear order of hierarchy) or intransitive (no strict linear hierarchical order) (Boddy 2000). Consequently, predicting the interaction of more than two species may become extremely difficult, if not impossible, if the species under investigation display an intransitive hierarchy (White 1998). Thus, the expansion from two to more species may allow for the emergence of 'higher-order' interactions involving ever different biological phenomena, resulting in shifts in interactivity (Culver 1992). Furthermore, the initial partition configuration of three-species community has been shown to impact community development and recover, which may contribute to the persistence of individuals (based on pairings) to which otherwise would be expected from the system (Figure 3; Sturrock et al.). In addition, individuals display variety of results when subjected to the same set of conditions (Figure 3; Haley et al. 1996; Rouse 1995). Stochastity may be interpreted as an ecological advantage in hazardous environments. The complex stochastic and sensitive nature of community interactions is particularly evident within experimental systems involving more than two species, which demonstrate heterogeneity with the initial arrangement compared to binary-pairings (Sturrock et al 2002; White 1998). Agreement between based studies and those in natural strata is also likely to be influenced by environmental heterogeneity (Boddy 2000). The gaseous regime, temperature, and resource have all been shown to influence the outcome of interactions. Furthermore, the sensitivity of the interaction may not be associated solely with colony size, and that other environmental regimes are not conducive to superior competitive phylogeny (Sturrock et al 2002).
