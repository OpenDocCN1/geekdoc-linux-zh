# 1 INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 Mycotoxins, Mycotication, and Mycotology

Mycotoxin is a generic term describing secondary metabolites produced by fungi. "Myco" means fungal and "toxin" represents poison. They encompass a considerable variety of low weight compounds with diverse chemical structures and biological activities. Some mycotoxins could also be toxic to plants or other microorganisms; but these are not classified as antibiotics. In the context of effects of mycotoxins on animals, it is important to distinguish between "mycotoxins" and "mycotoxic". Mycotoxic is used to describe the action on various organs, notably the liver, kidneys, the nervous system. On the other hand, "mycotoxic" refers to a general invasion of living tissue by fungi (CAST 2003). Due to their diversity, they exhibit a number of biological effects, including both acute and chronic effects such as carcinogenic, mutagenic, genotoxic, teratogenic, and immunotoxic effects (Bhanagar et al. 2002; Chu 1998; Wogan 1992). Modulation of the immune system can cause immunosuppression or immunostimulation, so also plays an important role for the overall toxic (Bondy and Pestka 2000). The interaction of mycotoxins with cellular macromolecules plays a dominant role in their toxic actions (Chu 2002; Hussein and Brasel 2001). Recent studies on the effect of mycotoxins on apoptosis have further revealed their mode of action at the cellular level (Chu 2002). The complexity of the biological effects has led to a scientific discipline named "mycotology" for the study of various issues that are encountered with groups of mycotoxins.

Mycotoxins have been the subject of many reviews, and several comprehensive reviews have discussed various aspects of this topic (Bhanagar et al. 2002; CAST 2003; Chu 2002; Scourdore 2000). Due to a limitation of space, in this chapter we have tried to cover, as much as possible, the most recent advances achieved in the field, and referred primarily to listing all the previous references in the field.

# 1.2 Historical Background

Although mycotoxins have been known for a long time, identification of a mycotoxin as a causative agent for these illnesses was unknown in most of the incidences [reviewed in Bhanagar et al. (2002)]. For at least one example, the ancient Greek ergot-infected rye fever (and as early as 30 B.C.) could have been associated with mycotoxin-contamination of food (Van Dongen and DeGroot 1995). Since the ninth century, in Europe, where ergot was called *ignis sacer* (sacred fire) or St. Anthony’s fire, because it was believed that a pilgrim to the shrine of St. Anthony would bring relief from the intense burning sensation caused by the mycotoxin.
