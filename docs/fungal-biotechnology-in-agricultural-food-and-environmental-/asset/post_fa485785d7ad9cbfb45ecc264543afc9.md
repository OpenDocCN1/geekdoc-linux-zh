## CHALLENGES FOR FOOD SECURITY THIS CENTURY AND BEYOND

Today we face many critical issues in agriculture: (a) an exceptionally growing human population; (b) recurrent famine; (c) the destruction of natural landscapes such as tropical forests to extent to previously unused lands; (d) the exodus of human population from rural communities; (e) the demand for environmental quality resulting from exposure to agrochemicals, erosion of soils and salinity of soils as well as exhaustion and contamination of fresh water resources; (f) the loss of biodiversity through monoculture and the destruction of natural habitats; (g) the resilience of agricultural systems stress on food security; (h) the acquisition and concentration of agricultural water by multinational corporations; and (i) a lack of knowledge by a growing proportion of human population how to cultivate, and preserve food.

The United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization estimates that the world will be able to sustain the growing human population by 2030 but hundreds millions of people in developing countries will remain hungry and environmental problems caused by agriculture will require resources (FAO 2002). 2025.3 predicts that the global proportion of hunger will be in the world (United Nations 2002). The social consequences are obvious. Food is a basic human and right. How can we sustain the food needs of the earth's biotic community in the 21st century and by what we we preserve environmental quality and the diversity and quantity of life on earth (Time, August 26, 2002)? What solutions can biotechnology provide to address these problems (Khush and Bar 2001)?

In the last century, the Green Revolution addressed the food needs of the human population through the development of high yielding and early maturing varieties that performed under favorable conditions of nutrition and moisture (Khush 2001). Prior to this time, increased production was dependent on adequate water for crop production. In recent years, yield gains have not kept pace with population growth (Seragalin 1999). Furthermore, non-abiotic and biotic factors also contribute to losses in yield pre and postharvest. As in these technical constraints to contribute to production and population growth (1996). Socioeconomic constraints also contribute to practices that affect yield. The world's rice is grown in Asia with limited resources. Thus, decisions are based on economics rather than achieving targets. Despite this, the application of biotechnology to these technical constraints has limited impact on these reductions (Hussain 1997). This was due to resistance to the use of genetically modified organisms (GMOs) in most countries in Asia. 

China is the only country that has embraced biotechnology on a scale as to technical constraints (Huang et al. 2002; Pray et al. 2002). For few millions seek to increase yield and reduce pesticide costs and adverse effects of pesticides by using transgenic Bt-cotton (Pray et al. 2002). Another constraint relates to the complex-of of property rights that can delay disclosure and the scientific application of biotechnology discovered and their transfer is limited (Kowalski 2002). Finally, public concern about the safety of using GMOs has left of of food aid containing transgenic corn untouched in Zimbabwe and Zambia depict the tion that 13 million people are at risk of famine due to drought in Southern Africa (Parkerberg 2002). Organic agriculture worldwide has rejected GMOs as an agricultural technology and disease management. Light in potato is still very difficult to control in farming systems (Mader et. 2002).

Biotechnology in plant protection against fungal pathogens has seen phases of development over the last 20 years including:
a) the use of molecular markers to assist breeding and the map-based cloning of genes associated with resistance and plant response as we study fungal pathogenesis and host recognition;
b) the development of methods for transformation of genes;
c) the application of New Biology to study plant growth and development and plants respond to abiotic stresses and fungal pathogenesis.

The purpose of this review is to provide a critical assessment of these biotechnological approaches to plant protection on fungal pathogens. maps were developed for key pathogens such as Magnaportothe grisea (et al. 1995; Nita et al. 1997; Segward et al. 1993), Phytophthora infestans (al. 2001), and Leptosphaeria maculiani (et al. 1998). These also reveal the evolution of resistance genes (et al. 2002; Chaudhan et al. 2002; Daniel and 2001). Comparative maps were developed in plants by using molecular markers to reconstruct genetic systems (et al. 1993; Chen et al. 1997; Duncan et al. 2004; Gale and Deves 1995; Saghai et al. 1996; Tansky et al. 1992). The mapping of structural genes, both native and foreign, allowed precise information on the chromosomal location of genes important to plant defense (et al. 2000; Martin et al. 1998; Orchard et al. 2003; Segward et al. 1995). The cloning of pleiotropic resistance genes from many species has shown that they belong to a structural family of resistance genes (et al. 2002; Chaudhan and Lee 2002).
