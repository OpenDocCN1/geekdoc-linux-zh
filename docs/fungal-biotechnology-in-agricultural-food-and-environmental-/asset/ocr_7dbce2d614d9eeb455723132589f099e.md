# Biodegradation of Azo Dyes by Fungi

John A. Bumpus University of Northern Iowa, Cedar Falls, Iowa, USA

The manufacture and use of dyes and pigments is a multibillion-dollar industry. The use of these substances is an integral part of all manufacturing processes. The widespread use of synthetic compounds and the modern industry dates only to 1856 with the synthesis of mauveine by W. H. Perkin (Kirk-Ohmer Encyclopedia of Chemical Technology 1992). Of all the different types of dyes, azo dyes are the most useful and widely used colors. In 1885, J. P. Gross synthesized a yellow dye that was commercialized briefly (Kirk-Ohmer Encyclopedia of Chemical Technology 1875). Azo dyes that saw early commercialization included Chrosidine (Red in 1884), Bismark Brown. Following these early numerous azo dyes have been made and it is estimated that 2000 are currently in use. Wastewater are produced during the synthesis and use of dyes. Such wastwaters must be treated. The most important criterion for treatment of wasters is toxicity which is reduced to acceptable levels. Odors must be reduced by decreasing the amount of dye present. Decolorization is also a primary goal of color-dyeing manufactures.

There are several reasons for the popularity and use of azo dyes. They are available in almost every color across the spectrum, they are group colorfast and can be modified to bind to a variety of natural and synthetic fibers (Kirk-Ohmer Encyclopedia of Chemical Technology 1992). Unfortunately, azo dyes are resistant to biodegradation. This is likely due to the fact that azo linkages have not been exposed in nature. This review focuses on the ability of fungi to degrade azo dyes. Before, 1990, there were few reports of the degradation of azo dyes by aerobic micro-organisms. Cripps (1990) showed that azo dyes were degraded by white rot fungus chrysosporium under aerobic conditions. Since then, many manuscripts have been published documenting degradation of azo by P. chrysosporium and other fungi. Most of the fungi that can degrade azo dyes are white rot fungi. Interestingly, we have found that fungi which are not white rot fungi have been reported to have some ability to degrade azo dyes. The ability of white rot fungi to degrade azo was reported by this author years ago (Bumpus 1995). A number of reviews, at least in present, the degradation and/or decoloration of azo dyes in white rot fungi have appeared recently (Zol 2000 and Virajkumaraghar 2001).
