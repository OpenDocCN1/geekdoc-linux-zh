# 2.9 Firewalls and the Corporate Moat

# 2.9.2 Tunneling Through Firewalls

DANGER LEVEL

The inspiration for this section is the quantum mechanical concept where an electron sometimes can get through a barrier that conventional physics theory cannot explain. You have carefully configured your firewall to allow only requests from the Internet to a few ports and machines as SSH, DNS requests to your “outside” DNS server, and your mail and Web server. You are safe from others probing your network? Probably not. The problem is that most policies are designed for people who most likely play by the rules, in this case the specifications for the underlying protocols.

These include TCP, UDP, ICMP, and ARP. ARP worries about routing and such. Most firewalls are configured to keep evil packets from coming into allowing company people to send any reasonable packets out. Crackers are some of the most knowledgeable probes regarding protocols. They figured out that although most firewalls assume that reply packets are, in fact, to legitimate requests originating from systems inside their networks. Then, the ACKs simply generate packets that look like they are replies to requests and they lead right through the firewall. Can they do this? Yes. No.

It is easy. One simply requests a socket with the “raw” IP protocol rather than the TCP or UDP protocol. Because the crackers know what packets look like, they simply “build” the packets, by byte by byte, and send them out. You might have studied this in a working class. This is where the lecturer droned on so many bytes for the source and destination addresses, so many bytes for the checksum, etc.

One of the techniques is for a cracker to send ICMP echoes. These are the packets that are generated in response to a ping command, to test the connectivity and communications quality between two systems. Most firewalls will assume that this is in response to a ping request, it will be passed on to the system as the destination address in the packet. This is how a cracker communicates with a TFN2000 Trojan horse. TFN2000 is discussed in more detail in “Stealth Trojan Horses” on page 400. The same technique may be used with different types of reply packets for successful probing. For some types of packets, this response will vary, depending on whether there is a process listing on that port. Thus, even with a firewall cracker can map most networks! To watch for these and other packets on your network, scan “tcpdump” on page 370.

Here are the types of TCP packets used for these probes. The different names in all capital letters are the names of the various status bits that may be set in a TCP packet. The use of “|” character, used in C to indicate both OR, will be used here the same way. Thus, “RST|ACK” means a packet with both RST (reset) and ACK (acknowledge) bits set.
