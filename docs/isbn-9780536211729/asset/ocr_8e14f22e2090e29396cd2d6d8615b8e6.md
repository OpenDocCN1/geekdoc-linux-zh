# 2.1.3 Moving to Rings of Security

| DANGER LEVEL ☠ ☠ ☠ ☠ ☠ |

Many SysAdmins and programmers operate on the single Ring of Security idea that UNIX originally used (prior to 30 years of evolution). This idea is that it is necessary to have only one barrier (“Ring of Security”) between the cracker and your system’s data. Security depends on this single barrier being perfect. Worse, a perimeter firewall will not stop the 50–80 percent of intrusions that come from inside an organization. This is not just a problem for individual systems. Many companies have a single carefully configured firewall. Some even call it a limit. Behind that firewall are lots of unsecured systems depending on that firewall being perfect. What is life in perfect?

Instead of single ring of protection, there is the concept of multiple “Rings of Security.” If this is done correctly, even if a cracker gets past one ring there will be another ring to that and possibly even one third. To have multiple “Rings of Security,” it is necessary to improve security so that a cracker will have to follow a sequence of at least two “hard-to-follow” path segments to get to any goal that cause substantial harm. Following a path means the same as penetrating one of the “Rings of Security.”

"Rings of Security” have been added to Linux in several places. It is the reason root should not be allowed to use telnet. This restriction requires a cracker to break two passwords (root’s and some ordinary user’s) before he can log as root remotely via telnet. This creates two “Rings of Security.” See “Limit Which May Log In From” on page 63 for details.

Similarly root is not an acceptable FTP access. “Ring of Security” is also why the FTP daemon has the chooser feature, which can (and should) be used to limit any user (and crackers using anonymous FTP) to a small and carefully constructed portion of the file system. The chooser feature is discussed in “FTP” on page 138 and in “Limiting Consequences of a Named Compromise” on page 150.

The best way to make use of “Ring of Security” is to use attack paths to analyze your system, as discussed in “Attack Paths” on page 17. The only way that a cracker can get from an “easy-to-get” starting point to a point where she can cause harm is by having at least two “hard” paths.

It sometimes takes creativity to figure out how to do this. One place where “Rings of Security” could be very valuable is the path from your CGI programs to your data. Many companies CGI programs or scripts have privileged access to the database. This means if a CGI program has a security bug in it, a cracker owns your database. In many systems this is as severe as becoming root even though the database might be owned by an ordinary user.

CGI programs and scripts commonly written by people who do not have extensive knowledge of Linux security and many of these programs and scripts have severe bugs.
