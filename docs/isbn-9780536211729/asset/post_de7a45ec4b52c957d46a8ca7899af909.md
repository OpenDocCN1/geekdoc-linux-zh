
## 2.2 The Seven Deadly Sins

### 2.2.1 Insecure Default Passwords (#1)

Avoid default Cisco passwords as if your job depended on it.

I know what Cisco uses as the default password for many of its routers because a client's unhardened Linux system was cracked the night it was put on the Web. The password was used to determine which random IPs on the Internet accepted Cisco's default password. A system at the same location, where I had been allowed to "lightly" harden months earlier, remained uncompromised. The next day, the list had grown to hundreds of IPs before the system was taken offline. A better design would have been for Cisco to force the administrator to pick a decent password before the device started accepting traffic. For years now, most Linux distributions have been forcing an initial nondefault password before routing network traffic.

So for Cisco's advertising claim that their routers are more secure than Linux or UNIX systems because they are "security designed in and they use proprietary" rather than using open source. It took me one minute with Google to find a list of the default passwords for hundreds of popular switches.

### 2.2.2 Open Network Ports (#2)

Just as every account on your system is a potential path for a cracker, every network service is a road to it. Most Linux distributions install "tons" of software and services by default. They deliberately prefer "easy" over "secure." Many of these are not necessary or wanted. Take the time to remove software and services you do not need. Better still—do not install them to begin with.

To find out what services are running, use the `netstat -atuv` command or the `ports` program discussed in "Turn Off Needed Services" on page 80. Either will list all open ports on your system. Even a home system can have dozens or hundreds of ports open. A large web server could have many.

If there are services that do not want to be provided by this box, disable them. Many distributions offer a Control panel to do this easily, including Red Hat and Mandrake. You might want to remove the binaries from the disk or chmod to 0, especially any that are set-UID or set-GID.

NFS, the shell, exec, and login r*(ssh, rexec, login), FTP, telnet, sendmail, DNS, and linuxconf are some of the more popular services that get installed by default on many distributions; at least some of these should not be enabled for systems. Most of these are controlled by the inet daemon, inetd; they can be disabled by editing the `/etc/inetd.conf` file.

You do not need the FTP or local daemons to use the respective clients to connect into other systems. You do not need the sendmail daemon listening on port 25 to send mail or to send to local users or download mail via POP or IMAP. (You need to invoke sendmail periodically to de-spool delayed outgoing mail. You only need DNS (named, the name daemon) if other systems will be very happy to read `/etc/resolv.conf` and query your ISP's DNS servers.)

- NFS consists of these daemons: rpc.nfsd, mountd, portmap, rpc.lockd, rpc.statd, rpc.quotad, and automounter, scattered across a number of startup scripts. Technically, portmap is not part of NFS. It is a part of the underlying Procedure Call (RPC) layer on many systems is used only for NFS. When an RPC server, such as NFS, is started it will tell portmap both its service port number and its TCP or UDP port number. A client will then ask portmap, "What TCP or UDP port should I use for service X?" A cracker can lie to portmap and masquerade as a legitimate server. NFS has had plenty of security gaps in the past and its design prevents being in secure configurations.

On Slackware and similar systems, simply comment out the lines that start in `/etc/rc.d/*.initd`. The `grep` program may be used to find these. Be sure to terminate any of these services that are currently running on your system after altering the configuration files.

If you do not want to bother with kill, a simple reboot will do this and verify that the configuration files were correctly altered. (A set of rescue disks before this reboot would be a fine idea.)

> The careful SysAdmins will reboot their systems several times after making changes to startup scripts to ensure correct and reliable startup and operation.

To remove these services from your system, you can use your distribution's package manager to delete them. Red Hat-based installations (Red Hat, Mandrake, Caldera, Yellow Dog, TurboLinux) use RPM. Debian-based distributions (Debian and Core) use dpkg. SuSE uses YAST and Slackware uses pkgtool.

Linux is like the Swiss Army knife of networking—it has one or two tools of destruction that you get all the time, others less often, and some are never used. Unlike the Swiss Army knife, you can slim down Linux to just the services you need, and discard those you do not. I never use the awl or corkscrew on my knife just like I never use rsh or finger. Decide which ports you have to open (as much as and ftp) and close the rest. Closing unnecessary ports makes your system more secure and better.

### 2.2.3 Old Software Versions (#3)

Linux is not perfect. There are vulnerabilities being found monthly.[^1] Do not despair, though. The speed with which problems are found and fixed in Linux is the fastest on the planet. Your challenge as an administrator is to keep up with the changes.

Each distribution has a mailing list that you can (and should) subscribe to, where security bulletins are issued, and an FTP or Web site at which the fix will be available. Also, there are the independent security mailing lists, such as Bugtraq and X-Force's Alert, that are excellent. Get yourself tapped into those mailing lists.

[^1]: Most recent Linux vulnerabilities are not exploitable remotely on systems. This means that most Linux systems are not at risk for remote attack via the Internet. Many of the vulnerabilities may be taken advantage of by someone with a regular account on the particular Linux system. Other vulnerabilities are in programs that most people do not use. In my opinion, this is different from Windows vulnerabilities where almost every client system or Web server using that major version of Windows is vulnerable to remote attack via the Internet and thus to complete control by crackers.

Subscribe to Bugtraq by sending e-mail to LISTSERV@LISTS.NETSPACE.ORG with contents of:

```text
Subscribe BUGTRAQ
```

Subscribe to X-Force's Alert by sending e-mail to majordomo@iss.net with contents of:

```text
subscribe alert you@somewhere.com
```

Other good sources of Linux security information are:

- www.lwn.net/
- http://linuxxtoday.com/

They are distribution-neutral and carry all the major distributions' security advisories. There is much information on Web resources in Appendix C.

One of the beauties of Linux is that when a fix is issued, it is very quick to install, and unless it is in the kernel, your downtime for that service is on the order of seconds or minutes. Rarely, if ever, a reboot is necessary.

### 2.2.4 Insecure and Badly Configured Programs (#4)

The number of security bugs and their severity in commonly used Linux programs have been reduced so dramatically that I have dropped the subset of poor quality security from the most deadly sins. In its place is the use of insecure programs (such as rsh, NFS, portmap, and FTP) in other-than-carefully-controlled situations and the failure to properly configure programs. These "other programs" are capable of good security only if properly configured. Therefore, the Seven Most Deadly Sins have been updated for the second edition to reflect this.

Deadly Sin Five, has been merged with this one. This is because locally written or dated insecure CGI programs continue to be a problem, a substantial percentage of problems are due to SysAdmins making known use of insecure features of programs or failing to take advantage of security features.

Most SysAdmins know that POP and IMAP (unless wrapped in SSL), telnet, and FTP[^2] send passwords in the clear. They know that NFS and portmap have a history of security problems as well as design defects in their authentication. Many use them anyway and are surprised when they get broken into. Do not do that! Use stop, simap, SSH, and SFTP instead.

[^2]: If you are doing anonymous FTP, your password is normally your e-mail address. Unless you are a government-researcher at Groom Lake (Area 51) and you do not want to acknowledge the existence of such a facility, you generally have nothing to worry about.
