The next step is to calculate the power consumption of the hardware that will be placed on the UPS. A UPS is usually rated in watts, as the power consumption on server hardware (where power consumption is measured in BTUs, remember that 3.412 BTU/hr = 1 watt). To determine the power consumption your UPS must support, add up the total power consumption for all of your hardware; get a UPS that provides at least that capacity. A general rule of thumb is to make sure the total power consumption of the devices is at least 15% less than the rating on the UPS. That “cushion” helps prevent damage from power surge that can when follow a massive power failure.

# Post Power-Failure Consumption Surges

To understand the surge in power consumption that occurs after a massive power failure, consider this example. If a long power outage has drained your UPS of all battery power, all of the servers connected to the UPS will go down. When wall-outlet power comes back online, the UPS starts feeding power to the servers, turning them all at the same time. The demand from the servers causes a temporary spike in power demand, sometimes as much as 15% of normal load.

The design of your data center will have a significant impact on what kind of UPS you will purchase. Some colocation facilities provide uninterruptible power for you, you don’t need a UPS at all. For those that don’t have luxury, though, should you get a large “core” UPS or smaller units? The answer depends upon your rack configuration and UPS needs.

Some smaller UPS are rack mountable, you can have one or more UPS per rack, depending on your needs. Multiple small UPS make sense in a ranked environment, where it may not be convenient to have a central power supply. This system may not provide all of the power or battery backup that you need. In this case, it may make sense to purchase a larger core UPS. These are generally larger units with a main power distribution unit containing cords, outlets, and separate batteries. The stackable batteries are nice because you can easily add more capacity to your UPS as you add more hardware. Large UPS systems take up a lot of space, and a single, central location for power may not make much logical sense for your particular data center.
