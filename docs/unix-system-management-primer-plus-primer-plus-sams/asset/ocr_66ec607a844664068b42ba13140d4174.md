# Chapter 9 • PREPARING FOR DISASTER RECOVERY

As a Unix system administrator, you can conduct your own form of IT "fire drill" to test your disaster recovery plan in a less structured situation. Although usually relegated to the fire department, your fire drill can test your company's response to any emergency situation. To do so, you can declare a previously unannounced disaster to the department; declaration then sets the plan in motion to bring up the hot site, relocate office personnel, deploy generators, and anything else in the part of the plan being tested.

Company-wide fire drills are worthwhile exercises, but they are time-consuming and intrusive to daily operations. Individual departments should have the freedom to conduct their own drills, especially the IT department, which are usually responsible for the most complex parts of the recovery plan. Fire drills don't interfere with the company as a whole, and each department can hone or change their procedures on their own.

# Summary

Disaster can strike at any time, and as your company's Unix system administrator, you can be prepared for it or not. Choosing the latter can spell doom for your company and your job if the disaster is catastrophic. Preparing for the worst is a difficult and time-consuming task that involves research, planning, and a lot of testing, but it can save you hours or even weeks of downtime.

Take some time to investigate your company's existing disaster recovery plan. Does it list all of your resources and functionality needs to be available during a disaster? Are human resources and contact information included? What about detailed procedural plans? Does the plan reflect your current infrastructure or is it five years old? If you don't have a recovery plan or if senior management won't support the project, a business impact analysis can prove how devastating a significant outage can be to your business. You may not enjoy all the time you put into recovery planning, but you won't regret it if disaster strikes and your business is up and running while the company next door is scrambling to find extra power cords.
