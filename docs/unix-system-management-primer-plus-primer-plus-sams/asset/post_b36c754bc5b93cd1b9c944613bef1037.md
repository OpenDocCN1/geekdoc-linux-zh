An inappropriate use of resources includes the following general categories of activities:

- Any use of your system that violates your acceptable usage policies
- Any use that is illegal
- Any use that compromises the stability or security of your system

One common example of this problem occurs when a user stores illegal copies of commercial software (these illegal copies are called *warez*) in an organization's disk storage space. These users typically give their passwords so other people can download the software, or they upload the software to another distribution point, usually an FTP server.

A sudden spike in disk usage on one of your file systems may indicate the presence of warez, as nowadays tends to require enormous amounts of disk space. If you notice a spike in disk usage, run a find command to find files that have been modified since the last time you checked disk usage and look for large files that are titled like a game or application. The owner of the files will indicate the user who is involved (unless it is root, in case your server has most likely been hacked). The following command will find all files in the /usr file system that were modified less than 2 days ago:

```
find /usr -type f -mime -2 -print
```

*Overuse* is another common inappropriate use of resources. Overuse typically occurs when people use resources for purposes for which they aren't intended or reserved. A company might maintain a powerful server for rendering video images, for example, but users run long compilations on that server "to speed things up." While running the compilation isn't inappropriate, it on this server certainly is. In effect, these users are stealing resources from video rendering processes and the company who invested in these processes.

Users will try to take advantage of the resources available to them, but they may need to know which systems are appropriate for their tasks and which are reserved for others.

This problem can be addressed in policy. Create a document describing the functionality and authorized users of each system. This information can be put into the functionality document that we discussed in the section titled "Host Functionality," in Chapter 14, "Internal Communication." This document can in turn be included in your acceptable usage policy.

You can also apply the concept of least privilege, which you learned about Chapter 13. Give users access only to systems that they absolutely require to do their work. This control can eliminate many inappropriate usage problems before they happen.

## Harassment of Another User

One of the major functions of networking is to allow users to contact anyone else on the network at any time. Unfortunately, some people use this privilege to harass other people online. Online harassment can take many forms, including the following:

- Threatening or abusive email
- Constant attempts to initiate communication with another user
- Breaking into another user's account to view personal data
- Use of another user's account with the intention of impersonating that user

No matter form harassment takes, as a system administrator, you must take all incidents seriously and respond to them quickly and effectively.

One of the first steps to ward off potential harassment problems is to develop well-publicized procedures for dealing with harassment, which should include the following:

- Reporting guidelines for users
- Contact information for local authorities
- Guidelines for escalating cases to local authorities
- Response procedures for dealing with the offending user
- Follow-up procedures for tracking the progress of cases escalated to authorities

Involve your legal department (or a hire lawyer) and your human resources department when formulating these procedures. Approving your policy through these individuals will ensure that your procedures adhere to both local regulations and company policy.

The way in which, as a system administrator, deal with harassment depends on where the harassment is coming from. You can disable the account of a user on your own systems, but if the harassment is originating from a user on a remote system, you can't stop the harassment on your own. Contact the administrator of the remote site and send as evidence as possible documenting the harassment (you learn how to contact remote administrators in "Dealing with Remote User Problems," later in this chapter). In any case, if any user on your system receives a threatening or otherwise disturbing message, if the user should contact the police or your company's human resources department to handle the issue.

## Launching Attacks on Your System

Most hackers try very hard not be noticed when they are breaking into a system, but often leave shred of evidence that is picked up by system administrators. You learn how to spot and analyze this evidence in Chapter 13. Hacking into a system is illegal and is almost always in violation of usage policies.

As a system administrator, your response to hacker attacks must be swift and severe. When you detect a hacker, immediately cut off access to the accounts the hacker is using to break into your system, kill any existing sessions that may be running, and contact the administrators of any remote sites used to connect to your system. You may also want to deny all connections from a remote network in case the hacker has another account to use on your system. If a hacker attacks from within your organization, contact your manager and the manager of the employee you caught hacking the system.

From the moment you suspect a hacker has attacked your system, beginning collecting evidence to support your suspicions. Gather logs, process lists, and other records that might expose the hacker and the hacking incident. Chapter 13 discusses the techniques for evidence gathering in detail.

## Using Your System to Attack Other Systems

When a user on one of your systems connects to another system with the intent of hacking into it or launching a denial-of-service attack, the illegal action implicates both the user who launched the attack and you, the system administrator. The user is liable for his her activities, but you are responsible for the integrity of the systems that used.

If your system is involved in a hacking incident, the remote administrator of the hacked system might deny all connections from your network for a period of time to prevent further attacks. To reverse this denial, you must remedy the weaknesses in your system that the hacker exploited, and then you must convince the remote administrator your systems are no longer compromised. You should deal with employees hacking from your system just as you deal with those hacking into your system. If you believe one of your users has successfully broken into a remote system, contact the administrators of that system immediately. You can identify the remote administrators using the techniques described in "Dealing with Remote User Problems" in this chapter.

## Preventing User Problems

Previous sections have discussed some of the problems users can generate within the Unix network. As system administrator, you save your time and money, and avert damaging losses of data, by doing all that you can prevent user problems from occurring. Nothing you can do as an administrator will eliminate 100% of the problems caused by users, but you can take some preventive measures to reduce their likelihood.

### Real-Example: The User Advocate

With a user-to-administrator ratio of thousands to one, the University of Michigan's Information Technology Division instituted the position of User Advocate. The User Advocate is responsible for investigating and resolving nontechnical user issues, including policy violations, legal issues, harassment complaints, and even complaints about the system administrators. When the university system administrators caught a hacker in the system or received a complaint from a user regarding policies, administrators would hand those problems off to the User Advocate and move on with their technical duties.
