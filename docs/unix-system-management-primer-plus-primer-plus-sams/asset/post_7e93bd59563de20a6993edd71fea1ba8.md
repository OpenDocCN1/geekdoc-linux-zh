
## Types of System and Product Tests

Testing in its simplest form verifies that an action produces its intended result. When you turn on a server's power switch, for example, you expect the server to power on, boot up, and start its applications. Examining individual functions in this simple form of testing is called unit testing. Unit testing is important in the initial stages of system testing, when missing or failing components can prevent entire systems from functioning (you learn more about unit testing in later sections of this chapter).

When unit testing has determined that individual portions of a system are working properly, you can test higher-level aspects of the system, such as its compatibility with other systems and applications and its performance under stress (referred to as load testing). System administrators, developers, and quality assurance (QA) staff typically perform performance tests and load tests, as discussed in this chapter. Quality assurance staff also conduct regression tests on systems that have undergone changes or upgrades, in order to verify that all system functions perform as they did prior to the changes. Regression testing reveals problems introduced by system changes and should be part of an IT department's overall management plan.

When these tests come, you may choose to allow the actual users to test the service you are offering. Predeployment user tests of systems and products often occur in two rounds: alpha and beta tests. Users are the most qualified people to test a product's usability—how it can be used by an actual user. A service might be functionally sound and perform well, but if the service is hard to use, it won't be received by users. You learn more about conducting and interpreting end user testing in the chapter.

## Quality Assurance Teams

Many software companies have a separate quality assurance team whose primary role is to test the functionality, performance, and usability of the company's software. Quality assurance testing is especially useful in larger projects where developers may not have time to adequately test their code.

## Scheduling Tests

Schedules are usually tight in IT environments, where a new service might need to be designed and rolled out in a matter of days. No matter how rushed the schedule might be, it must include time for testing. Often, testing is overlooked until the last minute, making it difficult or impossible to complete; as a system administrator, you are responsible for ensuring that the testing is built into the schedule from the project's beginning.

Testing timelines should parallel the project as much as possible to prevent a backlog at the end. You can conduct small-scale tests, such as unit tests, as you implement individual functionalities into the system. Large-scale load and usability tests must test the service as the whole or large portions of the service; conduct these tests at milestones. This testing plan ensures that the bits and pieces of a system are working properly, and then tests how they work together.

Scheduling the tests to occur throughout the project is important, but it's equally important to recruit help in conducting tests. You are likely to require the assistance of other IT staff and end users, so you must share the testing schedule with them, clearly communicate to them their responsibilities for the testing process, and make sure that they understand what they are responsible for doing and when are to do it. Without a firm testing schedule and full cooperation of all involved, system testing is likely to be neglected as projects near the end of tight deadlines.

## Test Along the Way

Plan tests along the way as you deploy a system, in order to help catch mistakes that might otherwise cause bigger problems later in the deployment process.

## Create a Testing Checklist

It is helpful to create a written testing schedule. The schedule should include a checklist of items to test, a list of individuals or groups responsible for conducting each individual test, and the deadlines associated with each test.

## Recording and Interpreting Test Results

Scheduling a test is just the beginning—system administrators, developers, management, and any other people involved in a project must dedicate their time to accurately recording the results of the testing process. It is not enough to simply say "Yeah, it works," in order to avoid spending too much time on testing—you must prove that something works and document it.

Some tests cannot be summed up with a simple checkmark or a pass or fail answer; they may have metrics, such as performance measurements, associated with these results. In these cases, testers must indicate not only that an item passed or failed a test, but how well it passed or failed. As the system administrator, you are responsible for interpreting these results to determine what they have to say about the functionality and usability of your system process.

For example, if one of your goals was to deploy a Web site that served a minimum of 1 million requests per day, you should verify that it can serve that many requests. And since that goal was minimum, your testing should determine the maximum capacity of the server. If it can serve 2 million requests per day, it provides much more breathing room above the minimum than if it can serve a maximum of 1.1 million—even though results would pass the test.

When you are designing your testing process, determine what kinds of testing results must be gathered from each test. Then, be certain to have some predetermined guidelines for evaluating those results.

## Real-World Example: Missing Memory

To improve an organization's system, administrators upgraded several servers from 512MB to 1GB by inserting additional memory. The servers booted normally after the upgrade. Shortly afterwards, users noticed that the servers were excruciating slowly. Administrators eventually found the memory shortage occurred because the new RAM was incompatible with the motherboard and therefore prevented the system from recognizing some of the existing modules. If administrators verified the available RAM immediately after installing the new modules, they would have noticed the problem.

## Conducting Unit Tests

With many system components and functions to test, it is often difficult to figure out where to begin testing. The most efficient way to go about unit testing is to test the results of each task along the way as you install, configure, and deploy your server. Certain tasks might have dependencies on others and discovering problems early on will minimize the amount of backtracking you have to do. A system administrator's job is mostly task oriented, so there's no rulebook for unit testing. In general, however, the process involves the following four steps:

1. Document the goals and consequences of a test.
2. Define testing parameters and procedures.
3. Perform the test.
4. Verify the results of the task and remedy any side effects.

In Step 1, you should document the ultimate goal of a test and any side effects that may occur in the process. Testing cannot always be done in closed environments—it is possible that a test can adversely affect other processes or data, and you should note those potential problems here and how to fix them after the test is performed.

Step 2 defines how you will perform the test, including any commands you will use and what the expected results are.
