## Professionally Install Patch Panel Cabling

Let your data center facility manager or a professional cabling contractor install and wire your patch panels for you. This guarantees that each port will be wired properly and with the proper cables. Additionally, if you are in a shared data center, the facility manager can arrange for your wiring to be run under the floor, above the ceiling, or in secure cable trays to avoid logistical or physical security concerns.

Patch panels are very useful, but they can be overkill for smaller installations. In general, patch panels are wise investments if two or more of the following situations apply to your data center:

- Your equipment is located in separate rows of racks.
- Your switch is in a different room or on a different floor from your servers.
- You have a high density of servers in each rack.
- The location of your core switch(es) might change in the future.
- You are running cables between racks.

## Securing Access to Your Data Center

Your data center environment can be neither stable nor secured if unauthorized people are allowed to enter it. Physical security for data centers is critically important, especially if large amounts of private information are stored in one central location. To adequately secure a data center, you must control access to the data center room, the racks, and the components stored on those racks. You can choose from a variety of security options to provide each of those access controls.

## Perimeter Security

Perimeter security—controlling access to the room or rooms that house your data center equipment—is your first line of defense against unauthorized personnel, so it makes sense that it should be your strongest. The location of your room within the building plays a role in determining how easily you can secure access to the room. Locate your data center as close to the center of a building as possible. The data center room should have no windows; windows allow relatively easy access to your equipment, they are subject to weather damage, and—if they break or crack—they can seriously destabilize the data center environment.

Walls should extend the ceiling tiles and all the way up to the next floor, to prevent access to your data center through the ceiling. Secure the doors with an access-control device, whether you use a simple key or a biometric system that performs retinal scans. The most common access-control devices require users to enter a special code into a keypad and pass a magnetic card key over a scanner to gain entry.

## Rack Security

If you lease space in a large hosting facility, your servers may be located in the same room with those from other companies. Where many different people from many different companies have access to the data center, the security of your racks should be your number-one concern. Fully enclosed cabinets are well suited to this environment, and most come with an option for locking doors. You can usually choose between combination and key locks, and some even offer keypad locks.

> Secure Everything Behind Closed Doors

> Open racks leave your hardware and data vulnerable to theft, and leave your network open for access by anyone who can get in the door and access to your switches, routers, and cables. Make sure all of your hardware, including cables, are packed away inside closed doors.

Combination locks are the most convenient form of rack security because they are cheap and do not require you to carry anything, but they can also be a liability. If a former disgruntled employee gains access to your data center, which is out of the question if it is a shared facility, he or she could gain access to your equipment. Keys are much more secure in this case, but even better are programmable keypads in which you can change the combination yourself.

## Component Security

Controlling access to individual hardware components is your last line of security. This "component security" is most important if you are in a shared facility and have equipment that cannot be locked away in a cabinet. Many servers and disk arrays today ship with key locks to prevent uncontrolled access to a machine's chassis, power-on and power-off controls, and disks inside a disk array. Although not the most robust security, component key locks prevent the casual passerby from turning off a server or grabbing one of your disks. It can also prevent curious executives with access to the data center from causing too much damage.

## Keep a Shared Keychain

As the number of servers under your care grows, so the number of keys you will have to manage them on a separate keychain that you can share with other system administrators. Store this keychain in a locked cabinet in your office or data center; make copies of the keys to your data center and the cabinet and give them out to authorized staff. This will minimize the number of keys floating around, in addition to helping you control access to privileged keys. You should also keep a duplicate keychain in a separate location, in case of a disaster.

## Power Management

Of course, none of your data center equipment is of much use without power, which makes the lack of attention paid to managing the quality of the power flowing to business-critical machines quite surprising. Managing the power to your data center involves controlling the power supply, securing redundant power supplies for power failure backup, managing the power cables, and preventing uninterrupted power supplies in place where they are needed.

## Managing Redundant Power Supplies

The power supply is the life force of electronic equipment. This device converts electrical power from a wall outlet into a form that the machine can use inside its delicate circuitry. The problem with power supplies is that when they fail (and they do fail), the machines they power shut down completely, causing downtime for all of the services on the machine. Following are a few methods for surviving power supply failures.

Redundant power supplies provide your machine with at least one more power supply than is needed for operation of the machine (called n + 1 redundancy). Because different components need different numbers of power supplies, redundancy doesn't mean any specific number of supplies. A disk array, for example, might need two power supplies to power all of the disks in the array. To arrange a redundant power supply for the disk array, you would need to make a third power supply available. If hardware that you purchased comes with more than one power supply, check its documentation to see if both are required to be active or if they are redundant.

The obvious advantage of having redundant power supplies is that when one fails, the "extra" will take over the load of the failed supply to keep the hardware up and running. You can replace the failed power supply at your leisure, preventing unscheduled outages.

## How to Tell Whether a Power Supply Has Failed

It is easy to tell when a power supply fails on a system with only one power supply—the system shuts down. However, on systems with redundant power supplies, the symptoms are not as drastic. Most power supplies are equipped with LEDs that indicate whether they are functioning correctly. Other power supplies are integrated into the system, enabling the operating system to query the power supply's status. Check your system's documentation to determine the best method for detecting a failure.

Redundant power supplies also ease the process of migrating a machine to a different strip, eliminating downtime due to power interruptions. If a server with a single power supply is plugged into a strip on one, you'd have to power down the server to move the power cable. If the server had redundant power supplies, you could migrate one power cable at a time to the new strip, and the server would never be without power.

True redundancy requires more than just redundant power supplies To make redundant power supplies truly useful, the supplies cannot be connected to the same circuit. Otherwise, if one circuit goes down, all of the machine's redundant power supplies go down, so your production equipment should be plugged into two or more circuits if possible. If one circuit goes down, only one power supply will be without power, and your hardware will keep.

Another way to ensure a minimum of downtime due to power is is use hot swappable power supplies. Hot swappable power supplies can be removed and replaced while the system is still running on a redundant power supply. If a power supply fails, there is never any downtime involved with its maintenance.

Not all hardware supports hot swappable power supplies, though; if not, a redundant power supply will keep the system running during a power failure, but the system will have to be taken down in order to replace the power supply.

## Using and Managing Power Cables

Of all the cables you work with as a system administrator, none will present you with as much variety as power cables. There are some, thin, some have round plugs, some have square ones, and plugs can have two, three, or four prongs. This wide variety of sizes and configurations ensures that the cables are sized adequately for the voltage they must carry, are grounded properly, and are compatible only with the appropriate power source. This last issue is important; unlike Ethernet or serial cables, power cables carry some serious electrical voltage, which if used improperly could cause injury, fires, or irreparable damage to your hardware. Special plug and outlet configurations prevent you from plugging the cable into the wrong power supply. Both power receptacles (outlets) and their corresponding plugs are identified by NEMA (National Electrical Manufacturers Association) codes.
