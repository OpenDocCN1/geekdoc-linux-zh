# Chapter 4 • TESTING YOUR SYSTEMS

Scheduling the tests to occur throughout the project is important, but it's equally important to recruit help in conducting tests. You are likely to require the assistance of other IT staff and end users, so you must share the testing schedule with them, clearly communicate to them their responsibilities for the testing process, and make sure that they understand what they are responsible for doing and when are to do it. Without a firm testing schedule and full cooperation of all involved, system testing is likely to be neglected as projects near the end of tight deadlines.

# Test Along the Way

Plan tests along the way as you deploy a system, in order to help catch mistakes that might otherwise cause bigger problems later in the deployment process.

# Create a Testing Checklist

It is helpful to create a written testing schedule. The schedule should include a checklist of items to test, a list of individuals or groups responsible for conducting each individual test, and the deadlines associated with each test.

# Recording and Interpreting Test Results

Scheduling a test is just the beginning—system administrators, developers, management, and any other people involved in a project must dedicate their time to accurately recording the results of the testing process. It is not enough to simply say "Yeah, it works," in order to avoid spending too much time on testing—you must prove that something works and document it.

Some tests cannot be summed up with a simple checkmark or a pass or fail answer; they may have metrics, such as performance measurements, associated with these results. In these cases, testers must indicate not only that an item passed or failed a test, but how well it passed or failed. As the system administrator, you are responsible for interpreting these results to determine what they have to say about the functionality and usability of your system process.

For example, if one of your goals was to deploy a Web site that served a minimum of 1 million requests per day, you should verify that it can serve that many requests. And since that goal was minimum, your testing should determine the maximum capacity of the server. If it can serve 2 million requests per day, it provides much more breathing room above the minimum than if it can serve a maximum of 1.1 million—even though results would pass the test.

When you are designing your testing process, determine what kinds of testing results must be gathered from each test. Then, be certain to have some predetermined guidelines for evaluating those results.
