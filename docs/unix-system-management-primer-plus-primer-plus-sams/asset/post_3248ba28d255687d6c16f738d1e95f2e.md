## Analyzing Hardware Requirements

When starting from scratch, chances are that you will spend the majority of your budget on hardware: servers, routers, disks, printers, and even cables. Consider this a luxury; you will only get to do it once every couple of years, depending upon your company's budget, so this is the time to choose wisely.

## Choose Hardware Wisely

Choosing the right hardware is critical, for putting together a working system and maintaining your credibility. If you buy hardware that doesn't work as it should or that fails to scale well with your growing business, your choices reflect poorly on your decision-making skills.

Here are some considerations to make when choosing hardware for your system:

- **What kind of machines do you need?** Do you need database servers? Do you need application servers? Now is the time to put your knowledge of system classifications to use (see “Classifying Your Systems,” earlier in this chapter). Write down the types of machines you need.

- **What hardware will meet the needs of the software?** You've chosen software and operating systems, now what hardware do you need to support those choices? If you choose Solaris as your operating system, you'll need to buy Sun hardware (the x86 version is not widely used, and will not be available beyond Solaris 8). If your application requires 256MB of RAM and 20GB of disk space, make sure your servers meet or exceed requirements.

- **What hardware is required to support the technologies your system incorporates?** Hardware that looks cool may impair management in a walkthrough, but it will impress them when it comes to perform? Make sure the hardware does what you need it to do. Do your servers access to your storage area network (SAN)? Make sure you order appropriate fiber cables for them, and that you have slots to put them in. Do you need mirrored boot disks? Do you better make sure that there are two bays in your server and that your boot disks are identical? Think about all of these little things, and make sure you have all your bases covered.

- **Does the hardware vendor offer good support?** Hardware is useless if it doesn't work. And when hardware fails, it gives an administrator warm fuzzy feeling (after the panic fades) knowing that an engineer and replacement parts are just a phone call away.

## Hardware Support

Hardware support contracts come in many forms, but they must cover one universal concept: replacement parts. If a part fails, your hardware support contract must guarantee a replacement part to be delivered within a specified time. Individual contracts vary in the details of how parts are to be delivered and who is to install them—conditions accompanied by their own variable costs.

The pinnacle of hardware support is represented by a staff of full-time onsite support engineers armed with a staff of spare parts. This arrangement would be a dream come true for any administrator, it's also the most expensive option, and it's impractical in many environments because it requires maintaining staff and parts you may never use.

## Colocation Facility On-Site Support

Some colocation facilities or disaster recovery sites maintain their own onsite staff. SunGuard Recovery Services, the largest disaster recovery facility in the country, has a large support staff specializing in Sun, EMC, and Cisco technologies. SunGuard directly supports its massive infrastructure and it sells the same support services to its colocation customers.

Most companies opt for 24/7 full support arrangement, in which support is available 24 hours a day, a week to help diagnose problems and to deliver any parts you may need. Support contracts for these services include service-level agreements for parts delivery that detail the delivery time you should expect (next-day, two-day, and even four-hour delivery by local couriers are common). Choosing a service agreement, decide what is practical for your company. You can hold out for a day if a critical piece of hardware fails, or do you need fixed right?

## Hardware Support Needs Vary

Some hardware requires higher levels of support than others. For example, you may want 4-hour onsite support for your production database server, but you may be able to get away with 24-hour response time for a development web server.

In addition to parts delivery, many vendors offer onsite support engineers. After diagnosing your problem online or over the phone, the vendor dispatches an engineer to your site who will confirm the problem and replace the failing hardware. The upside is that you don't have to worry about fiddling around inside your expensive equipment. The downside is that you have to wait for the engineer to show up, regardless of whether you have the parts in hand; most agreements dont guarantee an engineer's arrival time. If you are comfortable working with the hardware you may not need support engineer to come out and help you replace the part; this saves a lot of time and reduces the downtime for your systems while you're waiting for the engineer to arrive.
