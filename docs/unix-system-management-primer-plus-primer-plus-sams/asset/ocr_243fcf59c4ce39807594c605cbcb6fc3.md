# Employee Safety First

Throughout the formulation and execution of any disaster recovery plan, it is important to remember that ensuring the safety of employees, not hardware or data centers, should be your organization's number-one priority. Never make an employee decide between family and work during a disaster, and never put an employee in physical danger. The administrators of the plan should verify that all of the plan participants are safe and able to work beginning the recovery process.

# Forming a Disaster Recovery Planning Team

Before beginning the planning process, an IT department should designate a disaster recovery plan administrator. This person is responsible for overseeing the creation of the entire plan, testing, ongoing maintenance, and the actual execution of the plan during a disaster. The plan administrator should form a team of system administrators, developers, backup operators, and other IT staff that can represent their areas of responsibility when putting together recovery procedures.

Each team member should be responsible for developing his or her part of the recovery plan, including identifying human and technology resources and creating the actual per-system recovery procedures. These people will be the points of contact during a real disaster, so it is important that they have a thorough understanding of their areas of responsibility and how they relate to other departments in your organization.

# Conducting a Business Impact Analysis

When you have a good idea of what types of disasters could strike your IT infrastructure, you are ready to estimate the effects of those disasters on your business. System administrators may think in technical terms, such as "our Web site will be down for two days," upper management needs to think in terms of dollars, or as "how much money will our business lose the Web site is down?" Assessing the impact of a disaster on your business requires that you conduct a business impact analysis, or BIA.

Ultimately, conducting a business impact analysis falls under the domain of the business and management side of your organization. While your participation as a system administrator is essential to a successful BIA, let business manage it, since they are qualified to assess the impact of a disaster to individual business functions and the business as a whole. For example, instead of asking how your systems will be down, management will ask how much it would cost if your systems were shut down for a month? Would it lose clients? How much data loss could your business sustain and conduct business as usual?

In addition, when your business is not operational, it loses both current and potential new business. An online bookstore that is down for three days does not only lose sales to loyal customers, but it also loses the potential of new customers that might happen upon visiting the site. Even loyal customers may switch to competitors' sites, and they could switch to using those sites permanently. The effect of downtime can have a huge impact on revenues, business growth, reputation, and customer confidence.
