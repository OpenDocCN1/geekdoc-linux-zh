## Use Remote Mirroring to Minimize Data Loss

Several products, including EMC's Symmetrix line and Network Appliance filters, provide remote mirroring capabilities in which data is mirrored in real time (or near real time) to a secondary site. This option is very expensive; it requires duplicate hardware, adequate network bandwidth, and additional software. But if your business cannot sustain loss of data greater than a few hours, this is the most logical route to take.

## Stagger the Rotation of Offsite Tapes

Never send a batch of tapes offsite on the same day as you are receiving the previous batch of tapes from your offsite storage vendor. While very unlikely, if your data center blows up as you are trading tapes with your vendor's courier, you have lost copies of your backup backups as well as the most recent offsite copies. You should instead schedule the return of media two pickups (rather than one) later. That way there is always a copy of your data offsite.

## Formulating a Disaster Recovery Plan

Educating yourself about disasters is an essential first step in understanding the nature of the threats facing your IT department—and your business. To formulate a working disaster recovery plan, you must also understand the impact of potential disasters on your business, so you can create a plan that is cost-effective in light of the losses your business might suffer.

Planning is a labor-intensive process that involves these elements:

- Identifying critical functions
- Allocating resources
- Identifying critical tasks
- Generating plan procedures
- Testing the plan

A disaster recovery plan is usually part of a larger plan, called a business continuity plan (BCP). It encompasses all aspects of your business—including technology—and how each business unit will continue to function in a disaster scenario. Most aspects of business continuity are beyond the scope of this book, but as you read the information in this section, keep in mind that a disaster recovery plan should always contribute to recovering the business of your organization.

## Employee Safety First

Throughout the formulation and execution of any disaster recovery plan, it is important to remember that ensuring the safety of employees, not hardware or data centers, should be your organization's number-one priority. Never make an employee decide between family and work during a disaster, and never put an employee in physical danger. The administrators of the plan should verify that all of the plan participants are safe and able to work beginning the recovery process.

## Forming a Disaster Recovery Planning Team

Before beginning the planning process, an IT department should designate a disaster recovery plan administrator. This person is responsible for overseeing the creation of the entire plan, testing, ongoing maintenance, and the actual execution of the plan during a disaster. The plan administrator should form a team of system administrators, developers, backup operators, and other IT staff that can represent their areas of responsibility when putting together recovery procedures.

Each team member should be responsible for developing his or her part of the recovery plan, including identifying human and technology resources and creating the actual per-system recovery procedures. These people will be the points of contact during a real disaster, so it is important that they have a thorough understanding of their areas of responsibility and how they relate to other departments in your organization.

## Conducting a Business Impact Analysis

When you have a good idea of what types of disasters could strike your IT infrastructure, you are ready to estimate the effects of those disasters on your business. System administrators may think in technical terms, such as "our Web site will be down for two days," upper management needs to think in terms of dollars, or as "how much money will our business lose the Web site is down?" Assessing the impact of a disaster on your business requires that you conduct a business impact analysis, or BIA.

Ultimately, conducting a business impact analysis falls under the domain of the business and management side of your organization. While your participation as a system administrator is essential to a successful BIA, let business manage it, since they are qualified to assess the impact of a disaster to individual business functions and the business as a whole. For example, instead of asking how your systems will be down, management will ask how much it would cost if your systems were shut down for a month? Would it lose clients? How much data loss could your business sustain and conduct business as usual?

In addition, when your business is not operational, it loses both current and potential new business. An online bookstore that is down for three days does not only lose sales to loyal customers, but it also loses the potential of new customers that might happen upon visiting the site. Even loyal customers may switch to competitors' sites, and they could switch to using those sites permanently. The effect of downtime can have a huge impact on revenues, business growth, reputation, and customer confidence.

Another part of a BIA determines what each type of disaster has on your business. A major two-day outage might have little effect because of an investment in generators. A major fire, on the other hand, could put you out of business for weeks, if you don't have a secondary data center set up and ready to go.

Many companies offer consulting services that can prepare a BIA for your business. In addition, software products such as Strohl Systems’ BIA product (http://www.strohl.com/Software/BIA/default.asp) can collect the appropriate data and generate BIA reports for you. These options are costly and might be overkill for small businesses with few sources of revenue. Larger companies with complex business plans might find the task of conducting a BIA too complex and tackle on their own; in these cases, the investment in outsourcing the analysis might be worthwhile.

## Allocating Resources

An important part of an effective disaster recovery plan is the allocation of vital resources that will replace those made unavailable or inoperable during an emergency. These resources include the:

- Replacement data center hardware
- A secondary data center site
- Operational staff

This part of the disaster recovery process is both complex and critical. Because the alternative data center, equipment, and staff must be capable of supporting your business's IT infrastructure and its operations until your primary data center is back up and running, you must carefully plan and budget to ensure its success. You can choose to plan and procure resources on your own, or you can hire a recovery services company to help you plan and implement your emergency operations.

## Planning for Emergency Hardware

Many companies choose to provide a backup DR (disaster recovery) system for every system they use in their regular production environment. This arrangement is ideal, but it could double your hardware budget for the next year. Most businesses do not have that kind of money, and so scale down a bit.
