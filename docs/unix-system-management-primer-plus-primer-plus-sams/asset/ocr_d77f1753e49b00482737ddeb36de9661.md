# Is That User a Little Too Quiet?

Not all quiet users are well-behaved citizens. One of the goals of hackers is to remain undetected, so do not assume that just because a user does not ask questions or cause any visible problems that they are well-behaved. If you suspect that a system has been compromised in some way, do not rule out the quiet users; every user logged into your system should be a suspect—no matter how quiet they are—until you get evidence that points to the actual culprit.

# The Malicious User

Some people use your systems with the intent to cause damage, either to your system or another system on the Internet. These malicious users are thorns in the feet of system administrators. Malicious users range from "script kiddies" who run prewritten scripts to break into systems for fun, to people who are to destroy systems or use them to obtain sensitive data such as credit card and Social Security numbers.

There is no single definition for malicious activity; it depends on the policies and intended uses of each system you administer. In general though, malicious activity is any set of actions that attempts to harm or disable your system or its users, or any actions that attempt to use your system to harm or disable other systems.

# Look Beyond the Account Owners

When an account on your system exhibits malicious behavior, remember that the owner of the account might not be the culprit. Accounts can be easily compromised; a malicious user may have guessed an account's password or the account owner may have mistakenly given it out. You may use some of the techniques described in Chapter 13, "Implementing System Security," to track down your remote users.

Malicious activity is difficult to detect, but you can look for the following indicators on each of your systems:

+   - A sudden increase in load
- A sudden increase in network traffic
- A sudden change in disk usage
- Users logging in outside of their normal working hours
- Unfamiliar processes running on your machine

Malicious users should be dealt with swiftly and severely, to make it known to them that this kind of behavior is not tolerated. In Chapter 16, "Computing Policies and Agreements," you learned how to create and distribute documents that define the acceptable uses of your services and the consequences of actions that are not acceptable; these documents are invaluable when deciding how to deal with a malicious user.
