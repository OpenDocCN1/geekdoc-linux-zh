# INTRODUCTION

Unix system administration has long been considered a black art by most people, best left to the “wizards” who spend most of their time learning the intricacies of Unix, including the arcane commands, procedures, and configuration files that come with the territory. Numerous books, Web sites, classes, and conferences have attempted to bridge this gap by introducing the various commands and applications used to accomplish certain tasks. As a result of the wealth of Unix training and documentation, many people know how to accomplish certain tasks on a Unix system. But attending a few classes will not make a person a good system administrator.

A true system administrator must be capable of doing much more than performing system maintenance tasks, he or she must have well-developed planning and communication skills and be able to think logically and quickly at the same time. A good system administrator is constantly researching new technologies and assessing their viability, and is ready to adapt to new situations based on prior experience. And don't forget that any successful system administrator also must be dedicated to drive to the data center to fix a computer in the middle of the night. These attributes cannot be taught; they are learned through a combination of education and experience.

So do you learn to be a system administrator? Some universities now offer administration tracks within their computer science programs, but these programs are few and far between, and have little time to mature. Moreover, classroom programs do not give you the real-world experience necessary to run a system in an actual company. In more ways than not, system administration is like a trade of the middle ages, in which a person began a career in a craft craft by becoming an apprentice to a master craftsman. The apprentice watched the master work and assisted in various ways, while the learning more and more about the intricacies of the trade. An apprentice shoemaker, for example, didn't learn how to make shoes; or he or she learned how to be a shoemaker.

Toward the same goal, this book teaches you how to become an effective system administrator. Instead of focusing on the usage of commands to perform tasks within your system, this book introduces valuable techniques and concepts that help you become an efficient, competent, and professional system administrator, with topics that range from choosing operating systems to electronic communication methods. You learn how to design, deploy, maintain, and update a Unix system, and you learn how to avoid issues that result in management and user frustration, as well as unnecessary costs and downtime that contribute to them.

Unix System Management Primer Isn’t a reference or a “cookbook,” though many of the important tools and methods it discusses in each chapter are accompanied by examples and demonstrations to give you a feel for how they work. This book is meant to supplement Unix references and cookbooks by giving you an insider’s view of how system administrators function in the real world.
