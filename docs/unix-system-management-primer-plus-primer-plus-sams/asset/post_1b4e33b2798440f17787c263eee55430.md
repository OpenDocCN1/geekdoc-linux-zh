Users are the system administrator's customers and clients. This relationship holds true whether you administer a single development system for three programmers or an e-commerce site serving one million people. Understanding the different types of users on the systems and knowing how to manage, control, and communicate with them are skills as essential to good system administration as an understanding of system hardware and software. This chapter explores the interaction between system administrators and their users and some effective methods administrators can use to keep people happy, productive, and well-behaved when using the network.

# Types of Users

A Unix administrator, a user is any person who utilizes the resources available on a system. With this definition in mind, you can expect to find several types of users on your systems, including users with little technical expertise who require constant attention, and those who push the performance of your systems to the limit. Yet another type of user has the sole intention of disabling or hacking into your system.

The techniques you use to deal with these user types can help you identify the strengths and weaknesses of your systems and support infrastructure, avoid and resolve user-related problems, and keep your systems and their users operating efficiently and productively. The sections that follow define and describe some common types of users you'll encounter as a Unix system administrator. Later sections in the chapter offer practical advice for dealing effectively with all of the users who rely on your system.

## The Newbie

A "newbie" is a user who is new to your systems and Unix in general. As a result of their lack of experience, newbies often need the most help when using the system. Unfortunately, newbies often rub people the wrong way because they need to ask the most experienced people the most basic questions. Sometimes, they have trouble formulating a coherent question about the technology they are attempting to use. A newbie might ask for, "Your system is not very good. I typed in my username and password the computer printed bash$. What do I do?

To avoid frustrating and confusing the newbie (and irritating higher-level technical support), your help system direct newbies to first-level support whenever possible. If you are responsible for communications with all of your users, be patient with them and remember that you were always a newbie at one point!

You should always provide newbies with much "hand holding" as possible, which includes documentation for your services, FAQs, and even online first-time user tutorials for more complicated services. Universities are great role for this kind of support; in addition to phone and electronic support, they employ onsite support staff to help with user problems at computing sites. These people have a general knowledge of all of the university's computing services and can always escalate problems to other support levels in case they can't solve a particular problem.

## Real-World Example: Newbie Email

Newbies tend to be very vague when describing the problems. One university administrator received an incredibly vague email request for technical support from a student who wrote, "The server is down. Please fix this as soon as possible. My password is "password". Thanks in advance." There were hundreds of servers at this university, and the student neglected to say which server (and service) he thought was down. He also provided his password in email—like many newbies—did know about the technology he was using to how to ask for technical support, and this should be expected. The "clash" of communication between the technically savvy and the technically untrained often results in lost time and frustration for both parties. One of the solutions to this problem is to require users to fill out forms that request specific information about their problem; these forms are discussed in more detail in Chapter 5, "Support Administration."

## The Power User

The power user is at the opposite end of the technical expertise spectrum from the newbie. A power user knows all the details of your systems and has used them for quite some time. These individuals use your system extensively and, over time, become accustomed to its characteristics. Power users always want more from the system; they might make frequent requests for new software, more processors, or whatever they feel need at the time.

Because power users are so dependent on the system in their daily activities, however, they can become highly disturbed by any change you make to the system's current services. Installing a new version of Perl might send them into a frenzy, for example, because they're worried that something might go wrong as a result of the upgrade and they would lose access to the system until as power users can be, they are often their most valuable users—they use your systems and consider critical parts of their jobs, education, or for whatever reason they use it. ## The Know-It-All

As a Unix administrator, you can expect to have some users on your network who have a strong knowledge of Unix and its applications and would like nothing than to tell you how to run your systems. Many-know-it-all users are former system administrators who are now in a development or management role. They tend to voice their opinions, especially when they disagree with decisions you have made regarding your systems. It is important to keep them at bay and continue doing your job as normal, but don't shut them out completely—many of their suggestions turn out to be valuable.

## Use Know-It-Alls to Your Advantage

As a system administrator, you may find know-it-alls to be quite annoying, but their comments are often justified and backed up by real-world experience. Use this to your advantage by treating their comments as input from a knowledgeable colleague and drawing some benefit from their participation in your services—these users effectively (and unknowingly) act as unpaid quality assurance staff!

## The Quiet User

Quiet users use your systems expeditiously and go on their way. They never complain, rarely ask questions, and never cause problems—the perfect user! Though new systems tend to have the smallest percentage of users, as times pass and more users become comfortable with your system, their numbers tend to increase.

Some novice users are too timid to ask questions, especially if they have had bad experiences. To encourage users to communicate, you should use friendly language in your documentation and provide easily accessible lines of. You can read more about the different methods in Chapter 5.

# Is That User a Little Too Quiet?

Not all quiet users are well-behaved citizens. One of the goals of hackers is to remain undetected, so do not assume that because a user does not ask questions they are well-behaved. If you suspect that a system has been compromised in some way, do not rule out the quiet users; every user logged into your system should be a suspect—no matter how quiet they—until you get evidence that points to the actual culprit.

## The Malicious User

Some people use your systems with the intent to cause damage, either to your system or another system. These malicious users are thorns in the feet of system administrators. Malicious users range from "script kiddies" who run prewritten scripts to break into systems for fun, to people who are destroy systems or use them to obtain sensitive data such as credit card and Social Security numbers.

There is no single definition for malicious activity; it depends on the policies and intended uses each system. In general though, malicious activity is any set of actions that attempts to harm or disable your system or its users, or any actions that attempt to use your system to harm or disable other systems.

# Look Beyond the Account Owners

When an account on your system exhibits malicious behavior, remember that the owner of the account might not be the culprit. Accounts can be easily compromised; a malicious user may have guessed an account's password or the account owner may have mistakenly given it out. You may use some of the techniques described in Chapter 13, "Implementing System Security," to track down your remote users.

Malicious activity is difficult to detect, but you can look for the following indicators on each of your systems:

- A sudden increase in load
- A sudden increase in network traffic
- A sudden change in disk usage
- Users logging in outside of their normal working hours
- Unfamiliar processes running on your machine

Malicious users should be dealt with swiftly and severely, to make it known to them that this kind of behavior is not tolerated. In Chapter 16, "Computing Policies and Agreements," you learned how to create and distribute documents that define the acceptable uses of your services and the consequences of actions that are not acceptable; these documents are invaluable when deciding how to deal with a malicious user.
