KVM switch in a densely populated rack requires a significant amount of cabling; usually three cables connecting each machine to the KVM (and the video cables used to connect the monitor aren't exactly low profile). In addition, you must be physically present at the KVM console in order to use it, so a KVM switch does not provide remote access to the server. Another, often overlooked, disadvantage is that each connected to the KVM needs a video card, which can take up valuable expansion slots in a low-profile server and add to the total cost of your deployment.

Remote consoles provide the functionality of a KVM switch, combined with remote access—which is achieved through a network. Remote consoles are much less common than KVMs, but one manifestation of a remote console is the Compaq Insight Lights-Out Edition (http://www.compaq.com/manage/remote-lights.html). This remote console is an expansion card for Compaq servers that serves the console's keyboard, video, and mouse over the network—even over a Web browser.

Only if you use physically separate hardware for network access it can be considered truly out-of-band. Programs like Windows Terminal Services and VNC provide in-band console functionality that runs on top of the operating system and the network.

As previously discussed, a serial console is a textual interface served over a serial port. Most modern Unix operating systems support serial consoles to varying degrees. Both Sun hardware and the Solaris operating system offer native support for serial consoles. You can access everything from the POST (power-on self test) diagnostics to the boot prompt to the login shell on a serial port, just as if you were on a real console with a keyboard and monitor.

Popular free operating systems like Linux and OpenBSD rely on commodity hardware, and only support serial consoles after they start the boot process. Therefore, on Intel-compatible motherboards, you will not be able to access the BIOS diagnostics or setup menus from a serial console. This limitation can reduce the utility of a serial console, especially during hardware failures. Still, the potential for remote console access is usually worth the trade-off.

The advantages of using serial consoles are as follows:

- They require only a single cable, rather than the three cables required for a typical monitor/keyboard/mouse configuration.
- No video card is required, saving both money and expansion slots.
- Their text-based interface allows for easy out-of-band access from anywhere.

The disadvantages of using serial consoles are as follows:

- Every task must be performed from the command line.
- Serial lines are slow, usually between 9,600 and 115,200 baud. Any significant output will take some time to scroll off the screen.

An endless number of vendors sell serial console solutions; this chapter discusses two representative solutions. Both use an RJ45-to-serial connection using standard category 5 cabling, requiring a 9-pin (DB-9) or 25-pin (DB-25) adapter at the server end of the connection. This configuration allows for very densely packed ports on the console hardware and uses standard cabling.

The Digi Terminal Server (www.digi.com) line lies at the low end of the serial console spectrum. These are low-footprint devices that provide up to 32 RJ-45 ports for serial connections. Different models provide different numbers of ports and different access methods. The Digi servers require a server referred to (hereafter as the console server) for remote connectivity, port configuration, and serial communications software, and they're relatively dumb devices.

Two methods are offered: Ethernet and Ethernet. The Ethernet terminal is physically attached to the Ethernet bus on the server (and can be daisy-chained), and the Ethernet model (called EtherLite) can be physically located anywhere, as long as it is accessible through the network from the console server. The Digi hardware is relatively cheap and very effective, but it requires a separate console server to provide the communications software.

At the high-end, enterprise level of the console spectrum is the Lightwave Communications Console Server (http://lightwave.com). Like the Digi console solutions, the Lightwave provides RJ-45 ports for serial connectivity, but that is where the similarity ends. Most obvious difference between the two solutions is that the Lightwave is an all-in-one console; it provides the ports, configuration, network access, and communications software all in one package. It's also modular and capable of taking 4-port cards for additional network, serial, or modem ports. On the software side, the Lightwave server provides more advanced features, such as offline scroll buffers that enable you to see what happened on the console before you actually connected to the port. All of these features come with a price tag, which can add up to ten times more the Digi solution.

# Other Hardware for Out-of-Band Management and Remote Access

Out-of-band management applies to more than just serial consoles. A perfect example of this is remote power control. Have you ever wanted to power on a server from your desk, without having to go to your data center? Perhaps a server is hung completely, and only a power cycle will reset it; in that scenario, a networked or serial power controller is your answer. These power controllers allow you to establish a networked or serial connection to the server and provide an interface for controlling the power on each outlet. It's much better to Telnet and use commands to power cycle your server than it is to drive down to the data center to flip a switch.

Some servers provide their own power management. Sun's Netra line of servers contain a "lights out management" (LOM) module running on a separate circuit from the mother-board. # Emergency Remote Access

If your network fails, you're 30 miles from the data center, and you need to log in to your router to find out what's wrong? Is there any way to do this without driving to the data center? Some companies have redundant network feeds from different providers that may even terminate in different cities, so if one link goes down, the other is still available for use. That option is viable, but expensive. Another remote access option is to have two links to your data center, and make one of them a private point-to-point connection to your home office. Using that option, you don't have to sacrifice administrative access from your office if you lose your Internet connectivity.

If your network is completely inaccessible? Maybe your router is on fire or an earthquake-knock out your network provider—very grim, yet possible, situations. You need out-of-band access that doesn't use your connectivity at all. A modem does the job nicely in this situation. When it's 3:00 a.m. and the last thing you want to do is drive down to your data center, an emergency dial-in modem is a life saver.

Remember, however, that a dial-in modem opens a hole into your network. Because that modem will most likely allow entry behind your firewall, you should, at the very least, take the precautions when setting it up.

- Never give the dial-in number to unauthorized staff.
- Disable dial-out capabilities on the line.
- Require authentication before access is granted to a dial-in user.
- The modem should be connected to a server that is doing nothing else. Use it as a jump-off point should be for access to more critical servers.
