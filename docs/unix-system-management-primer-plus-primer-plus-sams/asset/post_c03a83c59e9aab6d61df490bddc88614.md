
# Summary

Managing outages can be a challenging part of a system administrator's job; often, these outages can be scary and overwhelming. However, you can't let these outages control your daily life or take over your IT department. As you resolve problems over the years, you learn how to better analyze new problems and fix them using previous experience. Your calm will eventually overtake your anxiety, and you will be handling situations with composure you didn't know you had.

This chapter introduced the most common types of outages that can occur and how to manage them. Some outages are created on purpose to service hardware and software; these scheduled outages should be performed within a designated maintenance window. When outages occur, it is important to accurately measure how long they last to monitor your compliance with service level agreements (SLAs). Your actions during an outage are important as well; setting production values such as responding to problem reports in a timely manner and documenting outage procedures will help you and other administrators deal with problems more effectively. Finally, when an outage is resolved, you should perform a root cause analysis to determine the true cause of the problem and fix it; this goes a long way toward eradicating those outages from your infrastructure for good, and fewer outages make for a happier system administrator!

# What Is an IT Disaster?

People define disasters in many different ways, but in the information technology world, the disaster is any event or condition that disrupts operations for an extended period of time. The definition of "extended period of time" really depends on your business. An outage of a bank's master account database might be considered a disaster if it lasts for several hours. A small business might be able to operate for up to a week without an email server before the situation reaches its disaster stage.

Every area and every business is subject to its own unique natural and man-made disasters. Several types of disasters, however, are common to a wide number of businesses. Include the following:

- Power outages
- Fires
- Floods and other natural disasters
- Data loss

The following sections examine these types of disasters—how and why they occur, what can happen as a result, and what you can do as a Unix system administrator to minimize your data center's losses in their wake.

# Power Outages

Most people have experienced power outages, both at home and at work. At home, power outages can cause real inconveniences, loss of property, and—where the home's electrical supply powers critical medical equipment—even threaten the health and well-being of residents. Typically, however, homeowners prepare for and minimize the damage of power outages by following some simple procedures.

- Have candles, flashlights, and batteries on hand.
- When an outage occurs, notify the electrical company of the problem.
- Keep the refrigerator and freezer doors closed to keep food and other perishables as possible.
- Use kerosene heaters or fireplaces as auxiliary heating supplies.
- If essential services depend upon electricity, maintain gas generators to supply power until the electrical service is back up and running.

## Be Prepared—Even at Home

As the system administrator for your organization, you probably have some form of home access to your business network. If, your disaster preparedness plan needs to incorporate provisions for power outages at your home as well as your data center. Uninterruptible power supplies (UPS) and even small generators might be part of that plan. Make sure all essential equipment located in your home is equipped with adequate surge protection, to avoid surge damage from lightning or power restoration.

Residential power outages might seem insignificant when compared to large data centers with UPSs, stacks of batteries, and huge generators. By minimizing the inconvenience and loss of any type of power outage involves the same basic approach: having emergency equipment and supplies available and using a planned procedure for minimizing damages from the power outage. The same type of planning and execution can help minimize power-outage damage to your production infrastructure, as, but it requires a great deal more thoroughness and documentation.

Power outages can occur from a variety of man-made and natural disasters, including storms, floods, and poorly planned digging with backhoes. Power company maintenance can interrupt service, as can temporary system overloads. Some outages are even planned and scheduled, as was the case with California's rolling blackouts in the summer of 2001. Although you can't predict the causes of power loss, you can plan for steps to minimize its effect on your systems.

## Potential Damage from Power Outages

A power outage can cause damage to your infrastructure on many fronts. The most obvious and immediate damage is that your systems no longer function, but this damage is remedied after power is restored. However, there are more severe consequences that a power outage can have for your systems, as follows:

- Sensitive electronic components can be damaged due to the surge after power is restored, rendering individual components or systems useless.
- If power is lost before a system has a chance to flush its pending writes to disk, the system loses data. If the data is critical operating system data, your system may not boot when power is restored.
- Magnetic tapes that are currently being read or written by a tape drive may get stuck in the drive when it abruptly stops. This often requires vendor maintenance and/or replacement of the drive.
