
# Matching Technical Support Infrastructure Business Size and Customer Needs

The size and organizational structure of your technical support infrastructure is determined by the size and structure of your particular organization. You can't offer your customers reliable, effective technical support unless you develop a technical support infrastructure that matches the types of customers you serve and how those customers communicate with your business. The size of your technical support staff and its response capabilities also play important roles in shaping your technical support infrastructure.

For most small companies of 25 to 100 people, system administrators are the support staff—there is little justification for creating a separate support infrastructure; it's just too much overhead for such a small organization. But if, as system administrator, you are the only person providing the technical support for an organization of over 100 people, you probably should consider adding staff.

You might start with two or three administrators who rotate support duties. As your organization continues to grow, you can build a separate support group. Of course, the support staff size and organization also depends on your company's technology infrastructure. A company with an office network and a single Unix server for development may not need any dedicated support staff at all. However, a company with five development servers, four Web servers, two back-end database servers, twelve developers, and a customer base of 50,000 Web surfers at home needs some support staff.

Use the following guidelines to help you determine the right staffing structure for your organization:

- Most organizations should have one system administrator for every thirty Unix servers in the organization.
- If you or your staff have trouble coordinating and prioritizing the numerous problems assigned to you, you should evaluate the use of trouble ticket management software.
- If you provide connectivity services, as an ISP would, for example, always provide alternative communication methods, such as a phone support line. This option requires support staff to answer the phones; system administrators can also rotate phone duties.
- Unless hired exclusively for user support, a system administrator's daily non-support workload would typically occupy 80% of his or her day, while the other 20% should be left to handle problems. If more than 20% of your time is spent handling support, consider hiring additional administrators to spread the load.

While small sites and internal customers can get away with a help desk made up of a couple of administrators answering, larger support infrastructures usually follow a layered model. The low-level staff communicate with the customers directly, high-level staff diagnose and repair serious or difficult problems, and mid-level staff handle everything in between. A common real-life manifestation of this is the three-tiered support model, consisting of level 1 support, level 2 support, and level 3 support, which you learn about later in this chapter.

# Identifying Your Customers

In order to determine what technical support infrastructure is right for your company, you first decide what customers your support organization serves—in other words, who are your customers? The answer may vary wildly depending on your company, and even the organization that you belong within company. As a system administrator, your customers are the people who are using the systems for which you are responsible. If you administer Web servers, your customers are the people using the sites hosted by those servers. If you administer development services used by your company's programmers, those programmers are your customers, even though they are internal to the company.

Companies may have several support organizations, some for internal customer issues and some for external issues. Identifying your customers is the first step in developing your support procedures and will help you decide what infrastructure needs to exist between you and the customers in order to ensure an efficient support process.

Splitting the duties of internal and external support into separate groups is a decision that should be based on the volume of problem reports you face each day. If most problems are submitted by external users, with just one or two internal problems per day, dedicating staff to internal problems is a waste of resources. If the sources of problem reports are more balanced, however, you can split the support staff into separate groups.

## Trouble Tickets

In the support world, the basic unit of work is the trouble ticket. A trouble ticket is a "container" for each individual problem, recording details such as a description of the problem, the problem reporter's contact information, who is assigned to work on the problem, a log of the work that has been done for this particular ticket, related trouble tickets, and so on.

# Offering Help Support

The most basic technical support organization is the "help desk." Help desk staff answer the phones when customers call, and they read emails coming from your Web site's customer page. They are responsible for creating trouble tickets from these communications and categorizing and delegating trouble objects to other groups. Help desk staff should be given training and scripts to follow when working with customers on the phone or over email.

Depending on the volume of traffic that comes to the help desk, the job of help desk staff can be easy and boring one day and challenging the next. Help desk employees may be the target of customer abuse, especially during an outage situation when hundreds of people are calling into the support line, outraged that something is wrong. Help desk abuse isn't an everyday occurrence, though; more significant help may be the lack of appreciation many receive. Many problems are beyond the help desk's capabilities, training, or capacity, and they must be passed on to the next level of support. In those situations, help desk might need to be relegated to simply relaying information from customers to other support personnel. Due to this unpredictable and often stressful nature of the job, help desk departments can experience high staff turnover.

Although turnover is inevitable, it's important to make each member of your help desk staff excited about his or her job. To keep your help desk employees involved in and contributing to the technical support process, provide them with ongoing training in the use of your products and services, as well as general technical training to help them better understand the inner workings of what they are supporting. The more your help desk knows, the better they are able to solve problems on your; that means they send fewer problems off to be lost in the caverns of multiple support levels.

Of course, with training comes the desire for advancement, and it is a certainty that you will lose well-trained help desk staff to higher levels of support within or outside your company. Although a promotion might cost you, it is not necessarily a bad thing; someone who has moved up the ranks through the years from help desk to system administrator to management has a unique—and valuable—insight into different aspects of your organization. This kind of promotion also helps with staff retention, because it shows that there is opportunity for advancement.

A common practice is to have periodic meetings between help desk staff, their management, and higher-level support such as system administrators, and even developers if they are involved in the support process. These meetings can be used to review reports and to raise any concerns that each party has. Ongoing communication between the multiple lines of support can improve the support process.

You should also provide your help desk staff with tools that empower them to investigate problems on their own, even if you have to write the tools yourself. Providing these tools not only gives the help desk an opportunity to actually solve user problems, but it reduces the number of issues that escalate to your group.

# Outsourcing Help Desk Functions

Due to the high turnover and headaches involved with help desk support, many companies choose to outsource it. Several companies provide help desk services, and outsourcing companies typically have a large pool of available resources to redicate to your company if the needs arises. Outsourcing your help desk functions may be more expensive than hiring part-time employees to handle the service, but it can eliminate the time and energy required to combat high employee turnover problems. If employee retention is becoming a problem for your organization, outsourcing might be your solution.

One major caveat with outsourcing is to make sure the staff you are providing are qualified to provide support for your product or service. Are they temps do they have actual technical experience or certifications to justify their hiring? You should also ask for the agency's references for organizations similar to your own. Any outsourcing agency should be able to provide you with this information, and if you are not satisfied with what you see, you should go with another agency.
