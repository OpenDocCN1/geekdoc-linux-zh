## Software Support

Software support has limitations of its own. Like hardware support, most vendors offer software support as a 24/7 or 8/5 (normal business working hours) phone support service, depending upon how much you want to spend. Software engineers can hold your hand while diagnosing problems from disastrous crashes to simple upgrades.

![](img/894492738e879267320b8d6f9a044180_44_0.png)

### Ask for the Help You Paid For
Don't be afraid to ask a software support engineer for help; you shouldn't hesitate to ask questions such as, "I'm uncomfortable upgrading to this new version without help; I don't want to lose my data." You're paying for help with these kinds of situations.

Software support can also include the right to receive patches and upgrades on CD-ROM or downloadable files from the Internet. Often, paying support customers receive these patches and upgrades before you're waiting on a new feature or bug fix. Vendors can also make development code available for their customers, either for beta testing or for emergency bug fixes that haven't been formally released yet. This benefit is invaluable, especially if you're the one who submitted the bug report in the first place.

## Vendor Liability

Pick up any support contract from your files, and you'll likely find a liability clause lurking in the details. No vendor will assume responsibility for damages caused by your use of its products or the vendor's support for them, at least not in a boilerplate contract. Your company's lawyers may have a problem with this, but most companies find it futile to battle with large vendors like Sun or Microsoft. Unfortunately, this means the responsibility of supporting your infrastructure ultimately rests on yours.

If management stresses that liability is a problem, stress to them that you may need expertise of staff to cover any lapses by the vendor. Remember, an experienced administrator may be able to solve a critical problem faster than it takes a support engineer to drive out to your site and make the proper diagnosis. This kind of support is discussed in Chapter 5.

## Summary

The first activity a system administrator should engage in is the planning process. You can't build a house without blueprints, and you can't build a reliable technology infrastructure without out detailed plans. This process begins by learning about your project's business goals and translating them into technical goals that you can use to drive your technology decisions. From there, you must make important choices about the specific components of your network, hardware, software, and operating systems, keeping in mind that each should be able to support your business goals and scale with your business's growth. The entire planning process, while long and arduous, will pay off the life of the project by forcing you to prepare ahead of time for many of the problems and issues that inevitably accompany any major project.

Think about your home and what makes you comfortable in it. You have a roof over your head, and possibly air conditioning, running water, and food in the refrigerator. Servers and network equipment have basic needs, too, but their "home" is a data center. The data center is essential to the proper deployment and functioning of your systems; it provides secure housing, power, network and phone communications, air conditioning, and storage. The specifications of a data center are so important that some organizations choose to build their own. Those who do not build their own data centers choose from a wide variety of hosting facilities where equipment can be colocated. This chapter introduces the most important concepts concerning data centers, in order to help you decide whether to build or locate.

## Should You Build Your Own Data Center?

When you're preparing to construct your data center, your first choice is critical: Do you lease space in a colocation facility (also called a hosting facility) or do you build your own data center? As you'll see in this chapter, building your own data center requires a large capital investment and a great deal of time spent planning, building, and installing the infrastructure. It's a lot like buying a house! If you can afford that storm of planning, payments, and waiting, building a data center may make sense for your company.

Some of the benefits of building your own data center include the following:

- It can mean your exact specifications.
- You do not have to share it with other organizations.
- There are no space or bandwidth limitations.
- You, and nobody else, control the access to the facility.

## Recurring Costs Add Up

Don't forget that you have to pay for all the utilities in your data center, including power and air conditioning. These are recurring costs that can add up very quickly!

On the other hand, leasing space from a colocation facility is a lot renting an apartment— and it offers some of the advantages. Leasing space doesn't require a huge investment, and most of the infrastructure—including racks, power and air conditioning—are provided for you. When you lease, you have to abide by the facility's policies on important matters such as security and inventory control—and these policies might be too strict or too lenient to suit your company. Even little things like ample parking space can make a difference when you're renting to concentrate on the business of running your data center. That said, leasing space from a colocation facility has the following benefits:

- There is a much lower cost upfront.
- Power and air conditioning are the responsibility of the hosting facility.
- You pay only for as much space as you need.
- Colocation facilities have high speed (usually 45Mbps or greater) communication lines to multiple tier 1 network providers, and can provide you with more bandwidth than you could possibly afford upon your own.

So begin your decision-making process by listing all of the features you want to have in a data center; know which of those are optional and which are essential. If you find a colocation facility that meets your requirements, you might decide leasing is the best arrangement for your company. But if you can't meet the minimum specifications you've set for your data center, a "build-your-own" solution might be best for you.

## Real-World Example: Parking Woes

A national ISP has a colocation facility in a large downtown facility. Being in a city, parking was limited, and even though a private garage was available, spaces were available to all customers, so no spaces were guaranteed. During one outage, system administrators drove down to the facility in the middle of a business afternoon, only to be denied parking when they had to search for parking for 15 minutes, eventually parking several blocks down the street in a public lot. In the end, this cost the ISP an extra 25 minutes of downtime, because just was unavailable in the middle of the afternoon, and this was very upsetting to its clients! Parking may seem like a minor detail, but it's a convenience that you can afford to be without.
