# Redundancy

Redundancy is the simplest technique for ensuring high availability in your systems. By duplicating components of a system, redundancy eliminates single points of failure, any of which could cause a service interruption. Many parts of a system can be made redundant, from entire servers to network interfaces. Some components that are commonly redundant include the following:

+   * Servers
* Disk drives
* Network interfaces
* Communication lines (T1, T3, and so forth)
* Routers and switches
* Power supplies
* Fans
* Buses

Redundancy can be an expensive option. At the very least, when you purchase a redundant component, you are purchasing twice as much hardware as your service needs to function. Beyond that, you probably need other hardware, software, licenses, or configurations to make the redundant parts work together, which adds even more to the total price of your system. In spite of its costs, redundancy remains one of the most reliable and straightforward methods of achieving high availability, and it is an essential part of many of the high availability mechanisms discussed in this chapter.

# Failover

Failover is a process in which a system transfers functions from a failed component—either hardware or software—to an identical working component. All failover configurations by definition rely on redundant components; at least one of the redundant components must be active, while the others are standby for use should the active component fail.

The best example of a system that utilizes failover components is a RAID (redundant array of inexpensive disks). RAIDs typically contain pools of spare disks to be used in place of a failed disk that was previously in use. These disks can be considered the standby units in a failover configuration, while the disks actually participate in the RAID array are active. The standby disks don't actually do anything until another disk fails. You learn more about RAID later in this chapter.
