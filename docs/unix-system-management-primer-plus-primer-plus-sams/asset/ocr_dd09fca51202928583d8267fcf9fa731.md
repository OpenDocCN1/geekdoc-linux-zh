Another part of a BIA determines what each type of disaster has on your business. A major two-day outage might have little effect because of an investment in generators. A major fire, on the other hand, could put you out of business for weeks, if you don't have a secondary data center set up and ready to go.

Many companies offer consulting services that can prepare a BIA for your business. In addition, software products such as Strohl Systems’ BIA product (http://www.strohl.com/Software/BIA/default.asp) can collect the appropriate data and generate BIA reports for you. These options are costly and might be overkill for small businesses with few sources of revenue. Larger companies with complex business plans might find the task of conducting a BIA too complex and tackle on their own; in these cases, the investment in outsourcing the analysis might be worthwhile. IBM’s Global Sites contains an excellent example of an effective BIA methodology at http://www-1.ibm.com/services/continuity/recovery.nsf/services/Continuum48.

# Identifying Critical Functions

You know a disaster will have a huge financial impact on your business, and you know you have to minimize that impact, where do you start? Begin the process of creating a disaster recovery plan by identifying all of your critical resources—those resources that must be available, even after a disaster strikes. Instead of thinking of all resources in terms of individual pieces of hardware that you need to have running, identify resources in terms of the business functions that your company cannot live without.

Table 9.1 lists a fictitious company's functional resources and which of them the disaster planning team deemed critical in a disaster. Your business may have different needs than this company; for example, if many of your employees work from home, remote access would be critical to your business in a disaster.
