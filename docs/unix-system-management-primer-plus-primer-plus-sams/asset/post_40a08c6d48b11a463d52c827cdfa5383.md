
# Portable Generators

Portable generators are the cheaper solution, but you must use them carefully to avoid risk to both data center personnel and equipment. Your plan for installing and using a portable generator should include these considerations:

- How will you provide adequate ventilation for the generator's fuses and still protect the generator from rain, snow, and other elements?
- How will you connect your equipment to the generator's electrical supply?
- How will you keep equipment functioning during the switchover to generated power?
- How will you protect your equipment from the "dirty electricity" created by portable generators?

If you plan to use a portable generator, you first need to determine where you'll place your generator during an outage. Most importantly, your equipment's power cables must be able to connect to the generator's power supply. You can use extension cables, but remember that you don't want multiple 50-foot power cables strewn across your data center—they are a danger to you and equipment near, especially if you trip over them.

Ventilation is the other determining factor in a generator's location. Gas-powered generators emit exhaust fumes, just like those produced by an automobile engine. And, just as you shouldn't run a car inside a closed garage for an extended period of time, you shouldn't run a generator without proper ventilation, either. If you locate the generator inside your data center or any enclosed building—you must provide adequate ventilation to safely expel the exhaust fumes.

Although locating the generator outdoors might seem the obvious solution, this arrangement doesn't always work. First, you might not be able to find an outdoor location that places the generator close to your building's PDU or your data center. In that case, you would be forced to use long cable runs to connect your equipment to the generator.

Second, you must protect the generator from heavy rain, snowfall, or flood waters. Heavy rain falling on a 5,000-watt generator and power cables creates an unsafe condition that can result in electric shock, power surges, and even fire. If you locate the generator outside the building, you still must provide adequate shelter for both the generator and the cables. Consider purchasing a small waterproof tent or using a well-ventilated trailer to protect your portable generators.

# Fuel Storage

Always store fuel for your generators in an approved fuel container. Seal the containers when they are not in use and place them in a well-ventilated area to prevent fume buildup, which could lead to an explosion.

Finally, you must take precautions to protect your equipment from problems associated with the power supplied by portable generators. The power created by portable generators is often "dirty"—full of voltage and frequency irregularities. Dirty electricity can cause electronic equipment, turning a simple power outage into a much larger and more expensive disaster. To avoid such problems, never plug systems directly into a portable generator; put a UPS between them to smooth out the irregularities in the generator power. Be aware, however, that many UPS units won't accept dirty power from a generator, as it can damage the UPS, so you should look for a UPS that allows you to adjust its sensitivity to these irregularities.

# Forcing the UPS to Accept Dirty Power

Some UPS units, such as those manufactured by APC (http://www.apcc.com), have an adjustable sensitivity width that can force the UPS to use dirty power. Use this with care, reducing the sensitivity too much can allow power anomalies like surges and irregularities to pass through your systems. Set the sensitivity at the highest level that still allows your equipment to function.

# Turbine Generators

Turbine generators are large, nonportable units that are installed (usually outdoors) by professional technicians and hooked up to your building’s power distribution unit (PDU), which feeds power to your entire building or parts of it. When you fed the generator through your building’s PDU, you can arrange a cable-free switchover from utility power to generator power by connecting the generator to a transfer switch. The transfer will seamlessly hand over power to a secondary source while managing all of the spikes and fluctuations that occur during the change, thus avoiding an outage during the transfer.

![](img/894492738e879267320b8d6f9a044180_247_1.png)

# Let a Professional Do the Work

Hire a professional electrician or ask your building’s facilities staff (who you should notify in any case) to hook up a generator to your power distribution unit. This is dangerous work; you could end damaging equipment or even fires if the work is done improperly.

Turbine generators are powerful; they can provide power to your data center or to your entire building during a power failure. Remember to take into account the capacity of your generator, though; you don’t want a tiny 2,000-watt generator powering your 20,000-watt data center, or even worse, powering your entire building.

# Hooking Up a Portable Generator to a PDU

You can hook up a portable generator to a PDU with the appropriate transfer switch, but this is less common in data center environments, where large amounts of power are needed to run the entire data center, which includes the HVAC system.

These generators are much easier to maintain and operate than portable generators, but cost much more to purchase and install—on the order of tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars. Large companies may invest millions of dollars in turbine generators. After you have made a substantial investment in the turbine generator's purchase and installation, you also must provide fuel to power the generator. Frankly, many generators run off of natural gas. Natural gas turbine generators offer several advantages. They can use the same gas and supply—that heats your building. This means you don't have to worry about refueling or even checking fuel levels. Using natural gas can be less costly than powering a generator with gasoline. The cost of operating a natural gas-powered generator usually is 40% to 50% less per hour than the cost of fuel for gasoline-powered units.

After the generator is installed and running, it's generally maintenance-free (at least, from your perspective). After inspections and periodic tests, you should have few responsibilities concerning the operation of a turbine-powered generator. When the generator kicks on an outage, the transfer switch should automatically switch power sources from your utility's power feed to your generator's feed, and you'll be basking in the warm glow of light-emitting diodes while others are running hoping their UPSs don't run out of battery power.

# Testing Your Generator

A generator is not of much use if it doesn't work, so it is important to test your generators periodically. For turbine generators, you should have a professional inspect them every few months; the vendor who installed the generator should be able to perform this test for you or recommend someone who can.

You need to inspect portable generators yourself. You should test them once a month; this is especially important in the winter, when parts of the engine might freeze. The test should involve the generator, checking oil levels, starting it, and verifying that power is supplied to test equipment (even a simple fan or a lamp will do). Let the generator run for 5 or 10 minutes to verify that it doesn't fail, then turn it off.

# Protect Your Generator During Winter Months

If you use a portable generator, take the time during the late fall and winter months to add fuel stabilizer and cold-weather oil. This will prevent freezing, reduce engine wear, and eliminate the need to drain the fuel tank after long periods of storage.

These generator tests are simple and effective. A more comprehensive test would involve a complete disaster drill in which you switch power to your servers over to generator power. A comprehensive test involves a bit of risk, though; if the test fails, you will incur downtime, which may not be acceptable to your organization. Get approval from your management before running this kind of test.
