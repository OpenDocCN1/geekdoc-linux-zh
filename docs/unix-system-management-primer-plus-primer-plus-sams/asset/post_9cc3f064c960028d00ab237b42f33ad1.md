## Acceptable Usage Policies

Many agreements prohibit the inappropriate use of resources, but the word “inappropriate” can have vastly different meanings in different organizations and even on different systems within the same organization. An acceptable usage policy (AUP) defines what activities are appropriate—and, in some cases, inappropriate—for a particular service. An acceptable usage policy (also known as an appropriate usage policy or conditions of use) also details actions the organization can take against users who violate the policy.

Organizations with network services, including large national bands and local ISPs, need acceptable usage policies. This need exists whether the organization’s network services are publicly available or limited to internal use. However, acceptable usage policies are most critical for services that require accounts, such as login servers or online banking sites, in users log in to gain access to privileged resources that could misuse in some way.

Acceptable usage policies (AUPs) can be long and drawn out, especially if you have a legal department that is concerned with the use of your organization’s information technology. Organizations can distribute such lengthy policies in various ways, depending upon how they can reach their user base. Policies can be posted on a central Web site, and mailed to users, handed out to internal users, perhaps at an orientation meeting designed to explain and discuss the policies. Policy content and distribution techniques vary among organizations, all system administrators should cover the following three main points when drafting an AUP:

- Intended use of the service
- Prohibited activities
- Consequences for policy violations

The following sections discuss each of these content items in more detail, in order to help you better understand how to create acceptable usage policies that adequately protect your organization and your system’s users.

## Defining the Intended Uses of Your Service

Every service has a defined purpose, no matter how generic that purpose may seem to be. By clearly defining the intended purpose of a service in your appropriate usage policy, you provide users an unambiguous profile of the types of activities your organization deems appropriate for that particular service. The scope of the service itself may be quite broad, but the intended use is clearly defined.

For example, an AUP’s definition of the intended use for a login server that hosts users’ account might state that the server is “intended for interactive use only.” The policy might go on to say that users can use the server to check email, compile programs, read news, edit documents, and so on. By listing examples of appropriate usage in addition to the definition of intended use, the policy gives uninitiated users further information about the policy defines as acceptable. Even if a user doesn’t fully understand “interactive” means, for example, after seeing that it refers to reading and compiling programs, he or she will probably have a much clearer idea of the proper uses of this system.

## Always Use Examples

Try to list the three or four of the most common uses for a system in AUP. Examples reinforce the definition of intended use provided in your policy, to help ensure that the majority of your users are comfortable with their system's intended use.

## Listing the Prohibited Activities

Due to the almost infinite number of tasks that can be performed on a computer, the list of prohibited activities typically is longer than the list of acceptable activities. You cannot possibly list (or even think of) every prohibited activity, much less include all of them in a policy, you can specify some of the more common malicious activities. The purpose of this list is to catch the eye of users who may one day pursue one or more of these activities; if users see that there are consequences for the exact actions they are contemplating, they are more likely not to commit those actions.

Here are some examples of the types of malicious activities you may want to include in your AUP:

- Using an account to send spam
- Using the accounts of other users
- Harassing other users online
- Launching attacks on this system or using this system to launch attacks on remote systems (including denial-of-service attacks) as well as attempts to gain root access

The list of prohibited activities in your AUP should be open-ended—you don't want to give users the idea that the items you list are the only things they are allowed to do or with your system. You can't possibly cover all malicious activities, and you don't want users to engage in some inappropriate activity, and then say, "The usage policy didn't say that was prohibited." One way to communicate that the list is open-ended would be to state, "Users engaging in the actions on this list or any other actions that are deemed illegal or harmful to this system or users will have their computing privileges revoked."

## Defining the Consequences for Policy Violations

You can't properly enforce any of your policies if users suffer no consequences for policy violations. The consequences for violations of your AUP should be clearly stated within the policy itself. The severity of consequences varies from organization to organization; a government agency may fire an employee and sue for damages over something that might evoke no more a slap on the wrist in a university setting.

Your policy can go into detail with regard to consequences for individual activities, but too much detail can drastically increase the length of your AUP and make it like a law book. You want users to be able to read and easily understand your policies, not skip over them because they are too long and complicated. Your goal is to keep your AUP at a usable length, while still clearly conveying to its readers the penalties for policy violations.

## Real-World Example: The Three-Strikes Policy

One university with thousands of users decided to implement a "three-strikes" policy for violators. For severe violations, such as hacking into university systems, the university immediately revoked the offender's computing privileges. However, for less severe violations, such as sending spam or running resource-hungry processes on servers for interactive processes, users were given three. The first incident resulted in a warning. The user-advocate was responsible for handling incidents (in a corporate environment, the human resources department manage this). This action aimed at showing the user that the situation would be escalated, and to warn the user that more severe action might be warranted. After the third, system administrators disabled the user's account and let the user-advocate deal with the situation from that point on, which would involve permanently revoking the user's computing privileges by exacting less severe penalties for minor violations.

## User Rights and Responsibilities

An acceptable usage policy lists what users can do using your services, but users also have rights that limit you as an administrator (and other staff) can do with regard to their account. You state these rights in a written policy order to protect your users against system administrators or other privileged IT staff who choose to abuse their authority. These rights are provided given that users honor responsibilities placed upon them. The policy documents these items is often called a "User Rights and Responsibilities" document. This document can be available electronically on a Web site or as a hard copy; what is important is that users are notified of its existence before using any of your services.
