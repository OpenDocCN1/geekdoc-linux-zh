From the moment you suspect a hacker has attacked your system, beginning collecting evidence to support your suspicions. Gather logs, process lists, and other records that might expose the hacker and the hacking incident. Chapter 13 discusses the techniques for evidence gathering in detail.

# # Using Your System to Attack Other Systems

When a user on one of your systems connects to another system with the intent of hacking into it or launching a denial-of-service attack, the illegal action implicates both the user who launched the attack and you, the system administrator. The user is liable for his or her activities, but you are responsible for the integrity of the systems that person used in the attack.

If your system is involved in a hacking incident, the remote administrator of the hacked system might deny all connections from your network for a period of time to prevent any further attacks. To reverse this denial, you must remedy the weaknesses in your system that the hacker exploited for his or her attack, and then you must convince the administrator that you've resolved the problem and your systems are no longer compromised. As a system administrator responsible for protecting your own system from hackers, you know how convincing your antics must be to ensure the remote administrator your system is "safe".

You should deal with employees hacking from your system just as you deal with those hacking into your system. If you believe one of your users has successfully broken into a remote system, contact the administrators of that system immediately. You can identify the remote administrators using the techniques described in "Dealing with Remote User Problems" in this chapter.

# # Preventing User Problems

Previous sections have discussed some of the problems users can generate within the Unix network. As system administrator, you save your time and money, and avert damaging losses of data, by doing all that you can to prevent user problems from occurring. Nothing you can do as an administrator will eliminate 100% of the problems caused by users, but you can take some preventive measures to reduce their likelihood.

> ### Real-Example: The User Advocate

With a user-to-administrator ratio of thousands to one, the University of Michigan's Information Technology Division instituted the position of User Advocate. The User Advocate is responsible for investigating and resolving nontechnical user issues, including policy violations, legal issues, harassment complaints, and even complaints about the system administrators. When the university system administrators caught a hacker in the system or received a complaint from a user regarding policies, administrators would hand those problems off to the User Advocate and move on with their technical duties. This organization realized burdened technical staff could become with such a large base and took positive action to make sure users didn't suffer because system administrators lacked time to respond to user issues.
