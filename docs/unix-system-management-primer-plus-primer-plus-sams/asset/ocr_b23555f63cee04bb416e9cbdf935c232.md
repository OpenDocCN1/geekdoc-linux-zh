# # Identifying Your Customers

In order to determine what technical support infrastructure is right for your company, you first decide what customers your support organization serves—in other words, who are your customers? The answer may vary wildly depending on your company, and even the organization that you belong within company. As a system administrator, your customers are the people who are using the systems for which you are responsible. If you administer Web servers, your customers are the people using the sites hosted by those servers. If you administer development services used by your company's programmers, those programmers are your customers, even though they are internal to the company.

Companies may have several support organizations, some for internal customer issues and some for external issues. Identifying your customers is the first step in developing your support procedures and will help you decide what infrastructure needs to exist between you and the customers in order to ensure an efficient support process.

Splitting the duties of internal and external support into separate groups is a decision that should be based on the volume of problem reports you face each day. If most problems are submitted by external users, with just one or two internal problems per day, dedicating staff to internal problems is a waste of resources. If the sources of problem reports are more balanced, however, you can split the support staff into separate groups.

# ## Trouble Tickets

In the support world, the basic unit of work is the trouble ticket. A trouble ticket is a "container" for each individual problem, recording details such as a description of the problem, the problem reporter's contact information, who is assigned to work on the problem, a log of the work that has been done for this particular ticket, related trouble tickets, and so on.

# # Offering Help Support

The most basic technical support organization is the "help desk." Help desk staff answer the phones when customers call, and they read emails coming from your Web site's customer page. They are responsible for creating trouble tickets from these communications and categorizing and delegating trouble objects to other groups. Help desk staff should be given training and scripts to follow when working with customers on the phone or over email.

Depending on the volume of traffic that comes to the help desk, the job of help desk staff can be easy and boring one day and challenging the next. Help desk employees may be the target of customer abuse, especially during an outage situation when hundreds of people are calling into the support line, outraged that something is wrong. Help desk abuse isn't an everyday occurrence, though; more significant help may be the lack of appreciation many receive. Many problems are beyond the help desk's capabilities, training, or capacity, and they must be passed on to the next level of support. In those situations, help desk might need to be relegated to simply relaying information from customers to other support personnel. Due to this unpredictable and often stressful nature of the job, help desk departments can experience high staff turnover.
