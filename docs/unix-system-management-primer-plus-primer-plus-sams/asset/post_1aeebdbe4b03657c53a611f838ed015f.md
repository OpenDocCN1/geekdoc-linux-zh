# Don't Spam Your Owners

Spam refers to any unsolicited email, usually of a commercial nature, that is of extremely low value to the recipient. Most users do not appreciate being sent spam that fills their mailboxes, may just delete messages from people they don't know. When sending bulk email to users, state the purpose clearly in the subject. Drawing from the example used in this chapter, heading such as "Test the next generation of Nearest Integer Bank Online" would be appropriate. Also careful not to send too many messages from you on a daily basis, they are more likely to ignore your messages as time goes on.

You must also provide some sort of incentive to entice your users to participate in your testing. Sometimes the pressure of testing is enough. Wording your announcement to stress this importance, as in, "You can participate in the development of tomorrow's online banking technology," gives users a sense of worthiness. Sometimes incentives need to be more tangible. Companies pay users to help test, while others give discounts on future services or provide the product free of charge to testers after it is released to the general public.

# Pay Attention to User Demographics

Choosing the right users for testing is just as important as the testing itself. You may find that most users willing to participate in an alpha or beta test are technically savvy, because everybody is generally satisfied with the status quo. Try to recruit a representative sample of your users so you don't ignore any potential use of your system. You can do this by choosing a large number of users at random and offering them the opportunity to test a new service in exchange for demographic information, including their experience with computers and how often they use your services. You can use this information to determine whether any particular demographic group is underrepresented, at which time you can ramp up the recruitment process.

# Alpha Testing

Alpha tests enable customers and users to test new systems before they are functionally complete. Users can comment on bugs, features that they would like to see added, and the value of the system in general. The advantage of alpha testing is that you can get input from end users in the early stages of the development process.

Alpha testing comes with certain disadvantages, too. Most alpha testers are technical types who like to be on the bleeding edge. The average everyday user is not likely to participate in an alpha test, as they would be testing an incomplete product, possibly full of bugs, that provides little or no current value to them. As a result, expect your results to be somewhat skewed toward the high-end uses of the system, rather than representative of the average, day-to-day use case.

Alpha testing should be used to gain input from users using the basic functionality of a service, so you should offer an alpha test to them before you finalize all of the features of the service. Make clear to the testers that this is not a complete service, and that some functionality may be buggy or even missing.

# Beta Testing

Beta tests release a functionally complete product to the public (or your customer base) before it is officially released. Beta tests differ from alpha tests, in that beta tests evaluate complete products, whereas alpha tests evaluate incomplete products whose specifications may change at any time.

Products in beta testing usually include known bugs that might make them unsuitable for everyday use. As user input flows through the communication channels, developers and administrators can fix the known bugs as well as new bugs and improve performance of the system, simply based on third-party user input. Subsequent beta versions can be released as more bugs are found, and until the reports drop off to an acceptable level.

# Keep Communication Lines Open

Always provide a convenient forum, such as a mailing list or a newsgroup, for your testers to report bugs, problems, or comments. Stay involved in the forum so you know what users are saying about your service.

No product is bug free; some problem inevitably rears its ugly head just when you think you've fixed every little glitch. You, developers, your quality assurance team, and upper management need to determine the acceptable level of bugs in the final product or system.

Many times, a company will pull a product out of beta and into production when all major bugs have been fixed, leaving minor bugs to future maintenance releases. In this case, major bugs might be defined as bugs that affect the functionality of the system or cause a failure of the system. Minor bugs would be annoyances like display glitches, or bugs that appear rarely, perhaps 1 out of every 200 users.

Beta tests can also be used to broaden the testing base far beyond the capabilities of your QA department. You cannot possibly predict how a user will use your system, but offering it to users ahead of time will give you a good idea and indicate any problems that you or your QA department would not have noticed.

# Testing Release Candidates

Release candidate testing goes one step beyond beta testing. Release candidate products contain code that the company believes is ready for release, but that might still contain some minor, yet undetected, bugs. In a beta test, the release candidate system is made available to end users for comment, and any last-minute bugs that they find are fixed. Also as beta tests, companies might issue multiple release candidates for a product before it reaches a point when it becomes suitable for public consumption.

One reason to issue a release candidate is so developers who use your systems have a chance to update their software on new systems. This is especially important for users who run a lot of their own scripts and programs on your system—they may need time to rewrite their code to work with your new system. Using an example from the operating system business, Sun will release early access versions of Solaris to developers so there are products available when it is eventually released to the public.

# Summary

Testing is an essential part of any system administrator's or developer's normal routine. The deployment process is complex and the final product must please a wide variety of people, including the system's users and the company's upper management. To make the deployment a success, everything must work as documented.

Unit testing is a good start, making sure every functional piece of a system works as it should. To guarantee that no compatibility issues remain in the system, conduct compatibility testing. Load testing stresses the system as a whole; load tests pinpoint the system's breaking points and help ensure that the system performs to specifications. User testing is the final phase of the process. Alpha tests, beta tests, and release candidate tests offer your users early access to products and systems while helping you gather valuable input on bugs and function issues in your system. No matter what your business is, testing is crucial to the success of your products. Remember: there is no such thing as too much testing.

Every computing service requires some kind of infrastructure to support its users.
Most users at one time or another experience a problem that they need to communicate to their service provider, and they expect to see that problem resolved within a reasonable amount of time. As a system administrator, you need to provide and participate in a support infrastructure that allows users to effectively escalate problems with systems to the appropriate staff. These staff, in turn, must also be able to communicate the status of each problem back to the user who first reported it and keep detailed records of the problem for future reference. To function effectively, this process requires the proper coordination of staffing, procedures, training, communications, and record keeping. This chapter introduces various methodologies and software that Unix system administrators can use to create and utilize such a support infrastructure in their organizations.
