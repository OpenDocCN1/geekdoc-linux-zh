This tells logsurfer to look for the pattern 'su root failed and pipe the log to the logmail program.

The most significant feature that logsurfer offers is the addition of dynamic rules. Dynamic rules are created on the fly to handle certain situations as they happen. For example, in what, if the /var file system filled up a server, your email, pager, and every other mechanism you set up for notification will be full of thousands of messages like this:

Nov 26 18:43:03 server1 ufs: NOTICE: /var: file system full

You'd receive thousands of these messages because they would be generated by the kernel for every unsuccessful write to the fully full file. Swatch would act on each of them independently; if you paged for file system full errors, your battery would soon need replaced.

Logsurfer can handle this much more gracefully. You could create a dynamic rule that ignores file system errors for a particular server after the first message had been received, as follows:

+ "file system full" . . 0 continue
pipe `/usr/local/bin/logmail`
"file system full" . . 0
rule top "file system full" . . 900 ignore

The first rule pipes the first message to logmail. The second rule creates a new rule that ignores the file system full pattern for 900 seconds (15 minutes). It does this by inserting the rule on top of all other rules (in the top in rule). Instead of getting thousands of notifications that the file system was full, you'd get one every 15 minutes, a more subtle reminder that your server needs you.

# Notification

All the monitoring in the world won't do any good if you're not notified about outages. There are various notification methods to choose from, and picking the right one depends on your needs and capabilities. The most important factor to consider when making this choice is reliability, emergencies can happen at any time, and your notification system needs to be able to get in touch with you any time. Systems use a variety of notification systems, including pagers, cell phones, email, and instant messaging. The following sections describe using these options individually or in combination to provide emergency emergency for your system.

# Pagers

Pagers are the quintessential system administrator accessory. Nearly every system administrator carries at least one pager. Pagers come in two types: numeric and pager. Numeric pagers are designed to communicate phone numbers and, therefore, display only a limited series of numbers. Although codes as "911" for emergencies and "215" for running late have made numeric pager communications more tolerable, numeric pagers usually aren't the right solution for a notification system.

Text pagers can receive text messages of several kilobytes and have larger displays that can display several lines of each message at a time. Messages can be sent to pagers by an operator at a dispatch center, through a modem, or through email. Many modern text pagers are two-way and can both receive and send messages. Text pagers are clearly the notification tool of choice for any system administrator.

Email and modem are the two most popular methods of sending pages. Although email is highly convenient and integrates into all monitoring systems, or your email infrastructure must be operational in order for you to send email messages. If your email servers are down, you can't receive a notification of the problem via email.

Out-of-band communication solves this problem. Using infrastructure that is outside your production network guarantees that pages will be sent out even if your system has lost connectivity to the Internet. Modem falls into this out-of-band category, and most pager services today provide a modem gateway to your pager. These services usually provide a public phone number through which anybody can send pages TAP (Telocator Alphanumeric Protocol, also known as the IXO protocol). Using a modem attached to your monitoring server to send pages completely separates your infrastructure from your notification mechanisms; you'll never be in the dark when something goes down.

# Monitor Your Monitoring Server

It is important to keep watch over your monitoring server so it is always available to notify you in case of failures. A simple ping test from a separate server can alert you when your monitoring server crashes (this is covered in the "Network Monitoring" section of this chapter).

Several paging software packages are available on Unix, but by far the most versatile and cost-effective (free) Qpage (http://www.qpage.org). Although it's been three years since the last release, Qpage enjoys widespread use.

- Support for TAP/IXO
- Client-server architecture; paging groups facilitate multiple users
- Message queues provide periodic transmissions

# Cell Phones

Cell phones are becoming increasingly popular notification mechanism, as prices for phones decrease. Today, cell phones can also support text messaging and even Web browsing, making them even versatile than pagers.

# Email

Some events are important enough for you to be notified, but you don't necessarily want to hear about them at three o'clock in the morning. While dead circuits and power outages warrant immediate notification, a connection denied by a firewall doesn't fit into the category. If an event doesn't warrant emergency notification, you still need to know about it. Email is a convenient middle ground for notification; it lets you know what's going on without intruding into your life. You can check on your schedule.

# Instant Messaging

The Internet's "killer app" was email. The second was the Web. The latest killer app is instant messaging (IM). Services like AOL Messenger and ICQ enable two or more peers on the Internet to converse in real time, exchange files, and play games in real time. Instant messaging is also a great tool for system problem notifications, because it doesn't require that a person be on either end of the "instant" communication.

Various command-line units and APIs exist that can be incorporated into notification scripts, providing real-time notification of problems. The disadvantage to this arrangement is that it relies on an outside party to provide the IM service; this limitation may not be acceptable from standards of reliability and security. Hosting your instant messaging service alerts these problems. Jabber is the most mature of these services (http://www.jabber.org).

As an example, the following fragment of a Perl script uses the Net::AOLIM module to log into the AOL Instant Messenger and send an instant message to an administrator who is also logged into the service:

```
#!/usr/bin/perl
use Net::AOLIM;
$conn = Net::AOLIM->new(
  'username' => $user,
  'password' => $password);
$aim->signon();
$aim->toc_sec_im($admin_user, "The server is down!");
```

# Combination Solutions

Sometimes more of a good thing is better. Many companies utilize two or more notification mechanisms to make sure that staff receive the messages they mean to see. A common notification service provides paging and email. When a log is deemed emergency through the system, both a pager and an email are sent out. If the page doesn't make it (for example, the pager is out of range or the provider gives a busy signal), administrators still receive the email notification of the problem.
