
- *Sprinklers*: A sprinkler system is the most common fire-suppression mechanism, but it can be a very dangerous choice for a data center. Dousing explosive equipment with water can damage it almost as severely as a fire can. Many install sprinkler systems because they work reliably, they're cheap, and they're easy to install and maintain. But this up-front cost-saving can cost a lot down the road: If fire strikes, you run the risk of damaging all of your hardware with the water used to put out the fire.

## Limiting Noise

Excess noise won't damage equipment or cause it to work less effectively, but it can really bother the people who work in the data center. The number-one culprit in data center noise pollution is the HVAC system; simply put, large air conditioning units are noisy. You can reduce HVAC noise by locating the HVAC unit outside of the machine room and running the air through ducts or under a raised floor. Open racks also contribute to noise levels by leaving fans and spinning drives exposed. If this is a problem, consider using enclosed cabinets for your hardware.

![](img/894492738e879267320b8d6f9a044180_52_0.png)

> Controlling noise isn't just a "nicety" for the people working in the data center. A noisy data center can make phone conversations difficult. When data center workers are discussing a problem on the phone with support staff, you want to make sure that they can hear each other clearly to avoid any misunderstandings or mistakes.

## Choosing Raised or Fixed Flooring

The floor underneath your system affects all other pieces of the network infrastructure. The floor physically supports the system hardware, just as a foundation supports a house. You can choose from two types of floors: fixed and raised. The flooring decision you make directly influences the choices you make down the road, so consider your choice carefully.

## Fixed Floors

Fixed floors are the floors you are used to walking on every day—solid platforms that rest on building structures and don't move. The advantages of fixed floors are as follows:

- *Stability; fixed floors don't move.
- Can bear very heavy loads.
- Low maintenance.
- Smooth surface makes it easy to move equipment.

The disadvantages of fixed floors are as follows:

- Cables run at floor level are exposed to damage from third parties or accidents; even taped-down cables can cause trip-and-fall accidents and act as obstacles as you roll along the floor.
- Ceiling cable trays are difficult to reach.
- Use ambient air to ventilate hardware (air conditioning is provided through wall ceiling vents or a large central unit).

## Raised Floors

Raised floors usually consist of a grid of individual 2- by 2-foot tiles resting on a sturdy framework supported underneath by a fixed floor. Raised floors have numerous advantages over fixed floors, but can be more expensive to build and maintain. The advantages of raised floors include the following:

- Flexible cabling options; you can run cabling under the floor into your racks, hiding 100% of your cabling from view (you learn more about racks in the next section).
- You can ventilate through the floor, forcing cold air into your racks.

The disadvantages of raised floors are as follows:

- Long cable runs are extremely difficult, requiring multiple tile pulls to access the cabling.
- Easy to catch one cable on another (including cables that do not belong to you) during pulls.
- More expensive to build, and tiles can be damaged, requiring periodic replacement.
- Requires a ramp or steps from the lower level entrance to the raised floor.
- Seams between tiles can make moving larger equipment across the floor.

## Choosing the Floor That Fits Your Needs

Which floor right for you? This is a decision you will have to make whether you are building your own data center or choosing a hosting facility. Raised floors are more common in older buildings where large mainframes were once located; they covered cooling pipes and power cabling. Unless you have one of these mainframes, a raised floor is probably not worth the money to build; smaller machines and large amounts of sensitive cabling do not lend themselves to the logistics of a raised floor. Raised floors are still available from many hosting facilities, who keep them for customers who need them or prefer them.

Fixed floors are much more affordable and do not require any extra infrastructure to build. In fact, it is quite easy to build a data center out of existing office space while keeping the floor; many organizations choose to do this in their own leased office space. The space can usually be converted back to office space just as easily, making a fixed floor a very flexible choice.

If you're still undecided about choosing a raised or fixed floor, your answers to the following questions may help you decide:

- *Is space at a premium?* Raised floors require ramps or stairs at the entrance to, and those represent unusable space. Even a relatively short 8-by-4-foot ramp can take over 30 square feet—space you'd have available for equipment in a fixed-floor room.

![](img/894492738e879267320b8d6f9a044180_54_0.png)

If you are building your own data center and space is at a premium, consider building the ramp side of your server room. This trades potentially unused space in a hallway for valuable floor space in your data center.

- *Have you already purchased racks?* A raised floor is most useful when cables are under the floor. Your racks need open or removable bottoms to accommodate this cabling. If you're stuck with closed bottoms, you won't get use.

- *Is physical security a concern?* In a shared facility, raised floors can be a security risk as tiles can be removed.

## Raised Floor Hazards

Although raised floors hide cables, they present their own hazards. It is all too easy to trip over misaligned tiles or missing tiles, which can be disastrous. In general, although raised floors have benefits, unless you have a pressing need for one, it makes more sense to use fixed floors which are cheaper, safer, and more flexible.

## Choosing and Using Racks

When purchasing hardware for your data center, you may choose both standalone and rack-mounted equipment. Standalone hardware is too large to fit into a rack and is designed to stand directly on the data center floor. Rack-mounted hardware, on the other hand, is smaller and can be housed in vertical racks—structures designed to hold multiple pieces of equipment atop a single "footprint," saving space in the data center, facilitating equipment connections, and providing easy access for maintenance and repair.

Racks come in many shapes and sizes, but the most common types are four-post and two-post racks (cabinet storage units are discussed in a later section). Each type of rack requires different cable management methods and hardware mounts. The racks you choose and the way you fill them can determine how efficiently you run your data center.

## Two-Post Racks

Two-post racks are simple and affordable, consisting of two posts supported by a base that typically is bolted to the floor for stability. Each post has prepunched screw holes running up the front and rear that are used to mount equipment directly onto the posts; you can install front-, rear-, or center-mount hardware.
