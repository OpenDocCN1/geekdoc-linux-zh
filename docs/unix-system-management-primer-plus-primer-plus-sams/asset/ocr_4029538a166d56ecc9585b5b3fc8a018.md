# Air Flow in Cabinets

Without nearby ventilation, it can be difficult to circulate the air around hot machines in an open rack. Enclosing this hardware in cabinets with roof fans can often solve this problem by forcing air out and of the cabinet and promoting air flow along the vents of the hardware. Data centers with air conditioning running through raised floors can take advantage of this with little effort.

Cabinets have many advantages, but they can be costly. Investigating all the options for a cabinet can feel like shopping for a car, and when you add up the numbers, the price tag makes it feel like you're buying a car! A high-grade cabinet with options like fans, locking doors, power strips, and cable management can cost more than $2,500.

# Choose Rack Width Wisely

When choosing a cabinet, make sure the rack width is suitable for your equipment (rack width is the distance between the mounting posts, not the width of the entire cabinet). Most common widths are 19-inch and 21-inch. Most server hardware is designed to fit into 19-inch racks, but you'll find that telecommunications hardware require 21-inch racks. Some cabinets have adjustable rails so you can increase or decrease the rack width to adapt to the dimensions of your hardware.

Because cabinets are enclosed at the front and back, your equipment must fit within the depth of the cabinet. If your hardware is too long, the cabinet's door won't close, resulting in poor air flow, unwanted noise, and an eyesore. Two common depths for cabinets are 30 inches and 36 inches. Although most hardware will fit in 30-inch deep cabinets, administrators invariably acquire one or two servers that need a 36-inch deep cabinet. Plan ahead! # Real-World Example: A Size Compromise Backfires

A company needed cabinets for its new corporate system build-out. The model they decided to purchase was only available in two dimensions: 24x36x84-inch and 24x30-inch. The company was located on the second floor of an office building that had no freight elevator. Unfortunately, the 36-inch deep cabinet was too long to fit in the passenger elevator, so the company's system administrators opted for the smaller cabinet—which was large enough to hold their Sun Netra T105 servers. A couple of months later, they ordered another Enterprise 4200 which was almost 29 inches deep with mounting rails for a 36-inch deep cabinet—too deep to be rocked in the cabinet. Fortunately, the IT department placed the servers on shelves, but the server length still stood out, which prevented the cabinet door from closing. After much deliberation, they had no other recourse but to remove the rear door. The company's servers are running fine, but the IT department now has to live with extra noise and the eyesore of an open cabinet.
