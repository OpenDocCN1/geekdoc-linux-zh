# Gathering Details About Specific Business Goals

Once you've defined your technical goals, it's time to crush goals into real numbers. In order to do that, you need to get more information from management about the specifics of its business goals, its priorities for the project, and its willingness to support the technical requirements for meeting those business goals.

Many details go into the design of technical solutions for meeting business goals. If a Web server must support one million hits per day, for example, what are the criteria for meeting that goal? Page retrieval in less than seven seconds? Low load on the servers? No client-outs during the browsing session? To know the answers to these questions, you must find out exactly what management expects from the technical solution for meeting its business goals.

# Being Prepared with Solution Details

You need to provide management with specific details regarding your technical solutions. For the "one-millioned" Web server, management will want to know what resources you need and why you need them. To provide that information, you must answer many questions. How many servers should you buy, and from what vendor? What operating system should they run? How many processors should they have? How much memory should they have? What software stack should you run? What are the timelines for deployment? How much staffing do you need to meet those timelines?

That's a lot of questions, and any good IT manager or CTO will expect you to answer all of them before moving ahead with your project. Performance management and capacity planning are an integral part of the design of any system architecture, and you learn more about those processes in Chapter 11, "Performance Tuning and Capacity Planning."

To gather necessary details about management's business goals for the specific project, you can begin by asking a very simple question:

What problem are you trying to solve?

You can make your job easier by helping management to articulate its view on the problem you're tasked with solving. Encourage management to stick with describing the problems in its own terms, rather than describing the solutions it wants to provide. You can help your managers through this process by educating them about how technology affects their business goals.

# Defining Basic Project Parameters

When you've gathered information about management's specific requirements for the business goal, you then can determine the parameters that are basic to all projects, such as management prioritizes the project in relationship to others; when management expects the project to be completed; and how much money management is willing to spend to meet its goals.

Beginning gathering this information by asking the following questions:

> What is this project's priority in relationship to other projects in our query?

Too many "business-critical" projects are canceled or find their way to the bottom of the pile because they weren't adequately prioritized. A major project with a short timeline but very low priority is doomed to failure, as other projects are likely to supersede and cause delays. You should alert your management as soon as possible if you find that other "important" projects are pushed ahead of those with short timelines. Otherwise, you will be responsible for missing deadlines.

Next, ask this question:

> What are the expected timelines?

As a system administrator, you're doing at least ten things at once. In addition to prioritizing the project, you need to know how to fit it into your busy schedule. Loose timelines can cause projects to stall and drift to the bottom of piles, and this can be a problem when your manager asks how the project is coming along. (You learn more about managing conflicting priorities in Chapter 14, "Internal Communication.")

Finally, ask this question:

> Do we have money in the budget to fully support the project plans?

You might be able to design a Web architecture that supports 10 million hits per day and can be implemented in one week, but your precious project will come crashing with a loud thud if its costs do not fit into the budget. If you think the project will be expensive from the start, express this to your management. Ask if management is prepared to back up the critical project with the money it needs to succeed.

# Real-World Example: Justification for Breaking the Budget

An IT department at a small startup company had its budget slashed for the next year. The project hurt most by the costs was a backup initiative costing $100,000, whose allocation was reduced to $75,000. However, the CEO was becoming very concerned with disaster recovery, to the time the staff decided to use that for their advantage. They created a proposal for the backup solution including hardware, software, and support specifications, split it into two sections: minimal and ideal. The minimal configuration was functional, but did not provide enough drives for tape duplication, which is required for safety. The ideal configuration contained more drives and was much more expensive, over the order of $120,000. The proposal stated that the minimal configuration did not meet the requirements for offsite tape storage, and the CEO approved the purchase. The IT staff spent the time to work out the details of project and justified it in terms that management could understand, and it paid off nicely.

# Assessing Compatibility Needs

Not every project starts with a clean slate. One of the most important things to consider when a project involves obtaining new hardware or software is compatibility with your existing systems. You've probably made a large investment—of time, money, and energy—in your current infrastructure, so it's in your best interest to ensure that any newcomers play well with it.

# Don't Forget Deployment Costs

When assessing the value of your current infrastructure, don't forget to consider all the details of its deployment. Think about how many scripts you wrote compiled from source, and how you installed servers to automatically load the operating systems and software onto several machines at once. Understanding the total investment in your current infrastructure can help determine how much of it is worth saving for your new system architecture.

To avoid the cost and frustration of "reinventing the wheel" in your new project design, work toward incorporating compatibility into your new system. You start software on one machine to be able to run on another. Files on your file servers should be accessible by all of your machines. And you certainly don't want your piles of documentation to double or triple in size to accommodate new incompatible systems.
