Proprietary planning software applications have export functions that can, for example, save a copy of your plan to a PDF file, which you can print and view without a copy of the software itself. In fact, you should expect that you would not have access to your office or the planning software. This is important when you do not have access to anything but the hard copy during a disaster; make sure all of the information you require is in the hard copy, including procedures for your own systems that you developed yourself.

# Planning Software Does Not Replace Your Own Procedures

Disaster recovery planning software will simplify the overall processes, resources, and contact information required for recovery. While this can be an enormous amount of information, it still does not substitute for your own system-level recovery procedures. For example, the software will not know how to reload and configure a Solaris database server; you will have to create and attach this to the plan. All planning software will allow you to incorporate your own documents into a plan, but you must still write them yourself.

# Distributing and Storing Copies of Your Plan

However you choose to generate your plan, you should always keep up-to-date copies in multiple secure locations. If your building is on fire, you won't have access to the copy of the recovery plan that you're storing on your company intranet or sitting in your file cabinet. It's a good idea to keep a copy of the disaster recovery plan in a waterproof safe at your office, in a safe deposit box at a bank, and in a safe location in each plan participant's (and backup participant's) home or car. Redundancy will ensure that you have access to a copy of the plan in any situation.

# Testing Recovery Procedures

Testing is the final component in the recovery planning process. Just because you spend months creating a plan using advanced software and consultants doesn't mean the plan will work. There are always unforeseen pitfalls and problems that will hinder you on your path to recovery, and it is best to discover them much rather than later.

After completing your planning, schedule a test with all involved staff and facilities. You'll probably find several things that need improvement, as poor communication or missing steps in procedures, that could add hours to an actual recovery process if you haven't tested them.

Testing requires the use of your secondary site's facilities, including your spare hardware. That means you'll need the secondary site's cooperation during the test. The facility you choose should allow for several recovery tests during the year, allowing your plan to change with your technology and staff.
