# When All Else Fails, Email root

If you are unable to get in touch with or get a response from any of the contacts for a domain, your last resort is to send mail to root at that domain. This might seem like the logical first step, but there are two problems with emailing root.

First of all, not all administrators forward root to a real user's mailbox where it will be read. If that's the case, any email you send to root might not have been seen for months, if ever. The other problem with emailing root is that if a system has been compromised, mail to root may end up being seen by the hackers on the system or they may have disabled root email altogether. And if the remote system is owned by that user in the first place, sending mail to root is pointless. In these cases you will have to contact their network provider, which you can identify using whois.

# Summary

One of the primary jobs of a system administrator is to support a system's users, from beginner "newbies" to other "know-it-all" system administrators. Effective communication is the key to this process; this chapter introduces several methods of communicating with users, such as the write command, newsgroups, and the message of the day, and the appropriate situations in which to use each of them. Such situations include incidents in which a user misbehaves and is in violation of your usage policies or even the law. Remote users can also present a problem, especially when they exhibit malicious behavior toward your system—knowing how to discover and contact remote site administrators solve many of these problems before they get out of hand. The who, whose, last, and topdump commands are very useful for tracking down the location of these remote users. However, always remember that users are your customers and clients; without them, your systems (and your job) would not exist in the first place. Rule your systems with a firm hand, but always listen to users' concerns and treat them with respect.
