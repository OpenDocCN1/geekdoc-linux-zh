
# Restricting Services

Most systems connected to a network offer some services to remote hosts. These services can be broken down into the following four categories:

- Business-critical services are required for your organization to function normally. Email, DNS, the corporate Web site, and database servers most likely fall into this category for most organizations. These services must always be allowed to run.
- Required services are required for a system to function normally. Services like SSH, serial consoles, and syslog fall into this category for most sites. These services support the infrastructure of a site and should always be enabled.
- Useful system services are not required for the proper functioning of a site, but they are handy for users of a system. You may use the finger service to find out which of your developers is logged into your Web servers, for example; the finger service is just a handy tool, you could just easily as SSH to the Web servers and use the who command. You can choose to allow useful services to exist on your system, or you can be more paranoid and run only critical services—it is a tradeoff between usability and security that you will have to decide. 
- Unused services are enabled on your servers but are not used by any person or machine. If your organization is using SSH to access remote servers, services such as Telnet and remote shell should probably be unused. In this day of exploits (discussed in Chapter 13), there is no reason to keep an unused service enabled for all access. Such services offering nothing but a source of worry for system administrators.

Choosing which services to allow and which to restrict draws upon the concept of least privilege, which was introduced in Chapter 13. In this application of privilege, you are not asking whether a particular user needs access to a service; you are asking whether the system needs the service to be functional.

Your security policy should define your organization's business-critical and system services and specify how to handle the other noncritical services. In general, you should disable noncritical services until they are needed, in order to avoid any possible security breach through services. Your policy should also document the procedures for enabling a service. The policies should call for a written justification and an approval process for enabling new services.

# Be Cautious When Enabling Services

Just because a user finds a service useful, you shouldn't feel compelled to deploy the service immediately on your system. After all, every service running on a system is just another potential doorway into that machine. Enable only those services that you deem necessary or truly beneficial to your organization—and compatible with your organization's security goals.

# Establishing Policies for Physical Security

The physical security of an organization protects its various sites and physical property from unauthorized access or damage. Some items you might see documented in a physical security policy include:

- Entry/exit access mechanisms (such as keys and card readers)
- Logging requirements for office visitors and vendors
- Logging requirements for hardware brought into a data center
- Visual monitoring requirements (such as cameras)
- Methods of disposal for sensitive documents (such as shredding)
- Removable media restrictions (for example, employees cannot copy confidential files onto a floppy disk and remove it from the office)
- Storage requirements for sensitive paper documents (for example, put them in a fire safe)

Many system administrators are so preoccupied with the system and network side of security that they neglect the physical aspects of security. A server that has been hardened at the operating system level and sits behind a well-configured firewall may be relatively safe from remote hackers. But if that server is not properly locked in your data center, anyone with a serial cable or a CD-ROM could potentially access that system from within your own borders. Make sure your security policies include ample guidelines for ensuring the physical security of your system.

# Require Passwords for Single-User Mode

With access to the physical console of a Unix system can reboot it into single-user mode. Although most Unix operating systems require the root password to enter single-user mode by default, Red Linux does not. You can require the password by adding the following line to /etc/inittab:

```
ss:S:once:/bin/sologin
```

However, entering single-user mode is not the only way to gain root access without a password. You can tell LILO (LILO Loader, the default boot loader for Red Hat and other Linux vendors) to boot directly into LILO altogether. Type linux init=/bin/bash at the LILO prompt to see this in action.

Fortunately, you can configure LILO to require a password before booting using the restricted and password parameters in each boot image section. The restricted parameter tells LILO that access to the image is restricted, and the password parameter sets the password in clear text. Add the following lines to /etc/lilo.conf, changing "mypass" to another password and run the lilo command to activate your changes:

```
restricted
password=mypass
```

You should also use chmod to only allow root to read /etc/lilo.conf, as the password is stored in clear text.

Security incidents take many forms, some of the more serious incidents include:
- Compromised account
- Denial-of-service attack
- Breach of physical security

Each of these incidents requires different responses, but in general follow:
1. Identify and stop all activities in breach of your policy. This process—known as containment—minimizes damage.
2. Disable any local accounts.
3. Report all activities to your legal department.
4. Secure the services.
5. Enable the services.
6. Monitor for any suspicious activity.

As you can see by reviewing the steps, incident response entails more than simply stopping attacks in their tracks; it involves investigating the causes, repairing the damage, and stopping breaches that occur. These actions prevent history from repeating itself and causing you to suffer through similar incidents.

# Keep a Hard Copy of Security Incident Procedures

As with disaster recovery plans, you should distribute and store hard copies of incident response in case a security breach renders your systems unusable.

# Involving Other People in Your Security Policy

Security is not just an information technology initiative; it requires the cooperation of the entire organization. RFC 2196 suggests that the following people be involved in the creation of:

* Site security administrator
* IT staff
* Management from each group within the organization
* System administrators from each group within the organization
* Incident response team
* Executive management
* Legal counsel

One reason to include all of these in your policy decisions is to gain support from all departments in your organization. By involving multiple parts of your organization in the creation of its security policies, you quickly learn each department utilizes the services available to them, and you are better able to identify potentially insecure practices. Legal counsel will verify the legality of your policies and provide the actual legal ramifications for policy violations.

# Enforcing Your Security Policy

A security policy must be properly enforced in order to effectively harden your organization's security. From applications to password selection, everything needs adhere to the policy, and it is the job of the site security administrator to ensure adherence.

The most important way to ensure your authority to enforce security policies is to gain the support of your organization's management and—if applicable—legal department. Because the security administrator is likely a member of a technology department, having the support of upper management can help to quickly resolve interpersonal disputes.
