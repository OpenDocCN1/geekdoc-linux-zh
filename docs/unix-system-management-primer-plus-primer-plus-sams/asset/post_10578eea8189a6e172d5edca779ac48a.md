
# Hardware Mounting Types

Front- and rear-mounted hardware attach to rack posts at either end of their chassis. Two-post racks often require center-mounted hardware—attached at the center of a chassis—to properly support the weight of the hardware.

Two-post racks are most appropriate for light pieces of equipment. Because the racks support the equipment from a single mounting point—at either the front, back, or center of the equipment chassis—the unsupported weight of the machine can cause the entire rack to lean and become unstable. Center-mounted equipment is somewhat more stable than front or rear-mounted equipment, but can be hard to find.

If you have to mount a heavy server to a two-post rack, you can use a high-load shelf that has bolted to either side of the rack, as shown in Figure 2.2. This solution incurs an additional expense and can take up valuable rack space, but with a two-post rack, you really have no choice.

# Space Two-Post Racks Properly

When installing two-post racks in parallel rows, you need to space them far enough apart to allow room for you to walk between the racks when they're both filled with equipment. A common rule of thumb is to leave one and a half machine lengths (the length of the largest machine you might install in the rack) between the racks. This leaves at least half a machine length between the racks, which should give you enough space to navigate between the filled racks to access the mounted equipment.

# Four-Post Racks

Four-post racks are one step up in stability and convenience from two-post racks. These racks support your equipment with four steel posts—two in front and two in back—usually 30 or 36 inches apart. Each post is either prepunched with screw holes or with square holes meant for cage nuts (see the "Mounting Hardware" section later in this chapter for more information on cage nuts). You can mount any piece of computer hardware to a four-post rack, and these racks simplify both hardware maintenance and space management.

Four-post racks are appropriate for mounting heavy hardware such as disk arrays and larger (over 50 lbs.) servers that need support on all four corners to stay securely mounted. These racks can house the same hardware as a two-post rack supports, as long as the hardware does not require center-mounting. The advantage of using a four-post rack in this case is that you do not have to use shelves to support the heavy rack-mountable hardware, reducing cost, complexity, and logistical problems during maintenance.

Most server hardware comes with rack-mounting kits designed for a four-post rack, which adds to the appeal of these types of racks. These usually consist of adjustable rails and bolts. Some come with sliding rails that allow you to slide a machine almost completely out of the rack for maintenance rather than having to remove the equipment completely from the rack to another work space. Sliding rails are especially useful for working with heavier machines, as you can work on them without removing them from the rack; you can read more about sliding rails in Chapter 3.

Four-post racks create fewer space-management problems because their outer dimensions are fixed—unlike the open two-post system. No hardware (unless very oddly shaped) can protrude beyond the bounds of the posts, so you do not have to worry about machines from one rack encroaching on the space of another nearby rack.

# Rack Mounting Kits

You will find that there are as many ways to mount hardware as there are types of hardware. Vendors usually provide rack mounting kits with enough rails, brackets, bolts, nuts, and complicated instructions to make your head spin, and none of them seem to work the same way. Take your time, follow directions, and recruit others to help you—every little piece of the kit helps the hardware in place and should be installed properly. Skipping a step or forcing something into place may compromise the stability of the rack and cause hardware to fail.

# Cabinets

Cabinets are enclosures that hold your hardware in a sealed and secure compartment. The most basic cabinets are less than enclosed four-post racks. Other cabinets have six posts (three pairs in parallel) so you can mount hardware of different sizes in the same cabinet; the posts in the center are usually adjustable. # Four-Post Racks Versus Cabinets

You will often see the terms "cabinet" and "four-post rack" used synonym, as cabinets are the most common incarnation of the four-post rack. However, do not assume this is always the case. If a vendor or hosting facility is offering you a four-post rack, verify whether it is an open rack or an enclosed cabinet.

Cabinets come in such a wide variety of styles, with so many available options, that they deserve a separate discussion. Cabinets offer several advantages in addition to those of the four-post rack, as follows:

- **Security**: Cabinets are enclosed to protect hardware.
- **Adjustable posts**: Many cabinets have adjustable posts to accommodate different hardware.
- **Cable management**: Cabinets provide space for organized cabling.
- **Better air flow**: Enclosed designs can help manage thermal environments.

# Choose Rack Width Wisely

When choosing a cabinet, make sure the rack width is suitable for your equipment (rack width is the distance between the mounting posts, not the width of the entire cabinet). Most common widths are 19-inch and 21-inch. Most server hardware is designed to fit into 19-inch racks. Some cabinets have adjustable rails so you can increase or decrease the width. Because cabinets are enclosed at the front and back, your equipment must fit within the depth of the cabinet. If your hardware is too long, the cabinet door won't close, resulting in poor air flow, unwanted noise, and an eyesore. Two common depths for cabinets are 30 inches and 36 inches. Although most hardware will fit in 30-inch deep cabinets, administrators invariably acquire one or two servers that need 36-inch deep cabinet.

# Real-World Example: A Size Compromise Backfires

A company needed cabinets for its new system build-out. The model they decided to purchase was only available in two dimensions: 24x36x84-inch and 24x30-inch. The company was located on the second floor of an office building that had no freight elevator. Unfortunately, the 36-inch deep cabinet was too long to fit in the passenger elevator, so the company's system administrators opted for the smaller cabinet—which was large enough to hold their Sun Netra T105 servers. A couple of months later, they ordered another Enterprise 4200 which was almost 29 inches deep with mounting rails for a 36-inch deep cabinet—too deep to be in the cabinet. Fortunately, the IT department placed the servers on shelves, but the server length still stood out, which prevented the cabinet door from closing. After much deliberation, they had no other recourse but to remove the rear door. The company's servers are running fine, but the IT department now has to live with extra noise and the eyesore of an open cabinet.
