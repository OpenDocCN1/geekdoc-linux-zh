As an organization grows, new groups are formed, and new products and services are deployed, the group assignments in your trouble ticket system can become out of date. When that happens, tickets can go to the wrong group and the problem resolution is delayed. You should review ticket assignments periodically, at least twice a year, to keep in sync with organizational changes. You should also monitor how tickets are currently assigned, and the rate at which they are reassigned to different groups. If the rate is more than 5%–10%, you may want to re-evaluate the current assignments sooner than later.

## Status Updates

No matter how hard you are working on a problem, your users will only sit idly for so long before wondering about its progress. Eventually they will barrage you and your support staff with demands to know when the problem is going to be fixed, and rightfully so.

You can alleviate this problem by providing periodic status updates to keep your users informed and show that your organization is sensitive to their needs. Some effective forums for providing these updates include the following:

- Support Web site
- Mass emails
- Recorded phone message

In order for status update to reach your users, you need to choose a forum that has not been affected, directly or indirectly, the problem you are working to fix. If your users' mail server goes down, it won't do any good to send out emails with the status of the problem. If you are an ISP and your modems go down, it isn't useful to post any kind of electronic message as your users cannot connect to the Internet in the first place. A recorded message would be more appropriate in this situation.

## Use Recorded Messages to Reduce Help Overload

Most users who call into your help desk during an outage are simply wondering about the status of the problem. If your user base is large you might experience a huge influx of calls that will overwhelm your help desk with repeated questions like "What's going on?" "How long until service is restored?" During outages you should consider recording a message for your phone system that is played when users call your help desk. This message should be restored. This will most likely satisfy 99% of the users who call into your help desk, allowing your support staff to deal with other problems that arise.

Your organization might or not provide these status updates to users, but at the very minimum your help desk needs to know when problems are fixed for its own records, it can respond to any subsequent calls inquiring about the status of the problem—especially, if the call comes from the CEO of your company wondering what all the fuss is about.

## Keep in Touch with Your Help Desk

Provide your help desk with constant updates during long outages. If you provide updates only at the beginning and the end of a problem, your help desk staff will not be able to communicate any progress you make in the meantime to your end users.

If a problem has been escalated through several levels of support, staff at each level need to provide updates to the level escalated to them, and so on. Eventually, the information will make its way back to the help desk, without missing anyone along the way.

this eliminates confusion and uncertainty during long outages where many groups are involved and everyone is curious about what's going on. If only a few people are involved, phone calls can be a good way to provide updates, but in a massive outage where many people at each level are involved, be sure to update logs in your trouble tickets so that each update does not require a huge number of phone calls.

## Providing Information About Escalated Problems

If you are on the second or third level of a problem's escalation, you must be able to get adequate information to understand, diagnose, and formulate a solution for a problem reported by a user. Without proper information recording procedures, you might end asking whoever reports the problem to you, and in turn you may have to go back to the help desk, who may have to contact the customer again. This process could take hours, even days, so it's everyone's best interest to get it right first time.

The best way to ensure that adequate information is asked and recorded when a problem is initially reported is to provide front-line support personnel with forms that ask for the information you need. This form could be a simple list of documentation required for escalation, or it could be an electronic form in an application or on the Web. Most of the support tools that you learn later in this chapter provide some facility for forms, and some customizable forms for your use.

## Use Forms Instead of Email

Users should not be expected to know what to provide. Well-designed forms can prevent delays. Following is a list of questions for the help desk. It should be worth the effort.

- What account is experiencing the problem?
- What is the contact information for the account (include phone)?
- What is the user's description of the problem?
- With what service(s) is the user experiencing?
- Is this an outage situation?

## Contingency Planning

What happens if there is an emergency, and the on-call person doesn't respond, and doesn't answer the cell phone? Does the problem go unresolved? Not in a well-run support organization! Plan for emergencies by having an escalation procedure for on-call staff, and provide this procedure to the help desk.

Your contingency plan might be quite simple. Maybe you'd like the help desk to try contacting other staff if the on-call person doesn't respond. One even had escalation procedures that required the director of information technology to be notified in case no other technical staff could be reached. This contingency was probably to keep the IT staff honest—no one would want the director of IT to be contacted and tell the staff was unavailable to do its job.

A contingency plan escalation procedure could look like this:

- Page on-call person. Wait 15 minutes.
- Call-call person on cell phone. Leave message and wait 15 minutes.
- Choose another IT staff member who is in the on-call rotation and repeat steps 1 and 2 for that.
- Repeat step 3 until all staff are exhausted.
- Page or call the IT Director and notify that no on-call staff are responding.

## Another Use for Call Forwarding

Step 2 in the preceding plan could be improved by using the call-forwarding mechanism discussed earlier to forward through all of the available phone numbers for members of the on-call staff.
