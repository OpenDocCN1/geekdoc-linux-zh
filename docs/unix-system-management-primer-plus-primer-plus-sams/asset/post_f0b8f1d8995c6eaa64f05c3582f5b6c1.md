# Mounting Hardware

The mounting hardware connects your equipment to the rack or cabinet usually consists of screws, nuts, and bolts. The mounting screws screw into holes in the rack posts or sliding rails; because they come in a variety of widths, they need to match up with the holes in the rails. Some hardware comes with preinstalled screws, and other hardware has holes where you can insert freestanding screws. Two of the most common screws are 10/32 and 12/24. Racks with threaded rails make mounting a straightforward task, but the holes in the rack posts are threaded to match the threading of a particular screw. When you purchase this kind of rack, make sure your posts match your equipment screws ahead of time; otherwise, you will not be able to use the rack. If you have hardware with different screw requirements, you would have to replace the rails to mount it to this type of rack.

You might also encounter another, less common, configuration: a rack with unthreaded holes. If the holes are unthreaded, nuts are needed to secure the screws to the rack, which can be a real nuisance because you need to reach behind the rails after the hardware has been racked to screw in the nut. Stay away from this configuration if you have the choice.

# Get Vendor Racking Requirements

If you have any doubts about racking your hardware, verify the requirements with your vendors—we deal with these problems every day.

Racks with EIA (Electronic Industry Alliance) M6 rails contain square holes that accept M6 cage nuts. Cage nuts are square pieces of metal with a threaded hole in the middle; flaps of metal on the cage nuts allow them to be snapped into the rail's square holes. You can buy cage nuts to accommodate a variety of screw sizes, so they offer a useful option for mounting equipment with varying screw sizes. But inserting cage nuts into rails is not always easy, and they can break easily.

![](img/894492738e879267320b8d6f9a044180_61_1.png)

# Insert Cage Nuts Correctly

It's important that you insert cage nuts correctly, by snapping them into the back of the rails, the opposite side from the front. That, the screws—not the cage nut—supporting the weight of the hardware. The cage nut is simply replacing a standard nut, so it belongs on the back.

# Cable Management

You may have 500 servers in your data center, all densely packed into well-ventilated cabinets and labeled nicely, making those servers easy to find. Five hundred servers may seem like a lot to manage, but the cables are an even bigger problem. For each of 500 servers, you might have a network cable and two power cables for redundant power supplies. In addition to those, your data center also might have SCSI cables, secondary network cables, console cables, and fiber optic cables. In this example, you easily could have over 1,500 cables to manage.

Cable management requires good organization and labeling, organizing your cables is your first priority. Poor cable organization can result in knots of cables in the back of your racks, often referred to as "spaghetti net". When cables are tangled and knotted, they're nearly impossible to move or trace back to their origins.

Fortunately, there are many solutions to the spaghetti net problem. Cable ties offer the simplest solution. A cable tie is a strap fastener that you use to bind a bundle of cables to keep them together and untangled. You can evenly space ties along the lengths of the cables, to keep the group tightly bundled along their entire length. Most cable ties are self-locking plastic straps that fasten tightly against the cables and lock into place when pulled. The problem with this type of cable tie is that you need to add or remove a cable, you have to cut the cable tie and fasten on a new one after your work is done. Velcro ties are reusable, however, and avoid the "cut and discard" problem.

Cable ties are great for cables that span long distances and don't move much, but they're cumbersome for cables that connect to equipment in the same rank and simply hang loose in the back of the rack. Additionally, large bundles of cables can get in the way when you're doing work in a cabinet. To keep cables well organized and out of the way, you can route cables through raceways, loops, or closed cable channels—typically referred to as cable management. Cable management is much like the conduit used to enclose electrical lines that pass through the walls of buildings. Your cable management solution can run horizontally along the top or center of the rack to channel cables from side to side. Vertical cable management coming from the top or bottom of the rack, usually attached to the posts, and organizes cables coming from the top or bottom of the rack, as illustrated in Figure 2.5.

# Patch Panels

The servers in your racks must be connected to a network switch (or hub) to send and receive network traffic. If you have multiple rows of racks, it can be difficult to route all of the cables to a single high-capacity “core switch”, due to distance and physical barriers. Such logistical problems also add to the cost of installing a new server, as you must run a new cable to the switch for each new server.

One solution to this problem is to install smaller switches in each rack or row of racks, but this can get expensive and adds to the complexity of your network. A more elegant and effective solution is to use patch panels—rack-mountable panels of network jacks that are directly wired to one or more switches. You install the patch panels in each rack and connect them to your switches once, before any servers are installed. The network cables from your servers plug into the patch panels in their respective racks of the switch itself. Every cable connected to your servers remains within your rack, alleviating the cable management headaches that occur when cables cross over into other racks.
