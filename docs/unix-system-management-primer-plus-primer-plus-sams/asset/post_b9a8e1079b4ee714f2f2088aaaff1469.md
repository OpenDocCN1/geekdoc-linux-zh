## Use a Headset to Let You Keep Working

Purchase a phone headset to use emergencies at the data center, so you can work and talk at the same time. The headset is especially helpful when talking to technical support, which usually requires you to type commands.

Of course, if the problem is resolved or you've come to a crossroads (maybe you need to order parts or wait for vendor support), contact the help desk immediately and update the status of the problem. This can also be done with a double ticket system like Remedy or req, which streamlines the whole process for you.

## Maintain Activity Logs

You may remember and understand everything about what the instant you finish working on it, but it's a good bet that you'll forget 50% of what you did the next day. Keeping a detailed activity log will help you document the entire problem solving process, including command output, vendor contacts, and timelines.

All good ticket management systems provide some sort of logging functionality (Remedy likes to get personal and calls it a diary). These logs are invaluable tools for both future reference and analysis of a problem that just occurred. A very simple but typical log entry might look like this:

## Reference the Activity Logs During Outages
You should consult old activity logs when new outages occur. There may be tips and tricks in those logs that can save you time and when dealing with identical or similar problems.

## Remain Calm

It is difficult to remain calm in highly outage situations, but you can't debug a problem and execute highly technical processes while you're running around like a chicken with its head cut off. As an old coworker once said, "You get ice water in your veins," meaning that as you experience outages and problems over the years, you become more and more calm even in the most dire of situations—your blood no longer boils at the mention of the word "outage."

The more panicked you are during an outage, the more likely you are to make a mistake, possibly worsening the situation. What's worse is panic is contagious—if you are running around your office or data center screaming, other members of your staff are likely to start doing the same.

Lead by example and stay calm analyze the problems you need to solve, and take things one step at a time. If other administrators are panicking, ask them politely to leave, as they only add to the problem at hand. Nontechnical coworkers are likely to stop and ask what's going on; it is very easy to get angry at them for bothering you during an outage, so simply ask them to go back to their desks and let you do your job. Even your managers need to be told this as well; you cannot possibly remedy a major outage with a manager looking over your shoulder, for example, reminding you how much money the outage is costing the company. Just ask everyone to leave you alone so you can fix the problem.

## Root Cause Analysis

All problems, no matter how complex, have a root cause. A root cause is where a problem originated—the spark that caused the fire. Sometimes the root cause of a problem is easy. In the example of a problem discussed in the activity log text listed in the preceding section, the root cause of the slow response time on goat was a failing gigabit cable. Sometimes it is not so easy; often the problem must be traced further through many steps to find out what.

For example, a user might call your help desk saying that she isn't receiving any. Upon further investigation, you find out the file system housing her mailbox is full. You reclaim some space, and mail begins flowing into the system. What is the root cause of the problem? Was it the full file system? Certainly caused the user's problem, but what caused the system? Maybe you were suddenly sent a massive amount of spam that filled the mailboxes. In that case, the spammers are to blame, and you remedied it by altering your SMTP rule to deny access.

Perhaps the full file system was the result of a general increase in usage over the past weeks. Administrators were not aware of or ignored the trend of increasing usage. The root cause of this problem is the ignorance of the administrators, which could be remedied by implementing new monitoring procedures or configuring a software monitor to report excessive disk usage before it becomes critical. This example demonstrates a situation where proactive monitoring (discussed at length in Chapter 6) is more effective than reactive monitoring.

In general, identifying a root cause requires you to trace the development of a problem from its symptoms back to the event or condition that set the problem into motion. After determining what caused the actual symptoms of the problem, you must determine what caused those problems, and so on, until you reach a problem for which there is no cause that you can remedy—that will be the root cause of the problem. In essence, you are creating a genealogy of the problem, tracing its roots back to the beginning.

## Avoid Band-Aid Solutions

Band-Aid solutions are those which mask the symptoms of a problem, but do not actually eliminate the cause of the problem. For example, in the spam example previously mentioned, adding more disk space would prevent the mailbox file system from filling up, but only temporarily. This is a Band-Aid solution; to truly solve the problem, the cause—in this case, spammers—must be found and remedied.
