+   Install and configure the replacement systems ahead of time and test their functionality
stability.

+   Allow end users to test the new system before the decommission takes place.

+   Provide ample transition time for training and system tweaking before the old system is
turned off for good.

Consider the example of an IT department in a company whose employees traditionally have\usetnet to connect to the corporate network from home. The company’s CTO has decided
that using Telnet is too insecure and limited. The CTO declares that a VPN (virtual private net-
work) solution should be in place within three months, at which time Telnet access is to
disable.

Two months are ample time for system administrators to have a VPN solution in place. But they
don't rush the system into it immediately. Decommissioning Telnet won't give you the
other staff much time to adjust. Instead, the IT department sends out informational notices
cerning the eventual Telnet decommissioning, beginning early in the three-month trans-
period the CTO has declared. Then, after the new VPN system is ready, the IT department
uses the final month of the transition period to slowly phase out Telnet.

During the month-long end-user transition, IT issues VPN clients to staff accompanied by
detailed instructions. It also arranges VPN software for creating desktop support staff, so they
can provide support to the inexperienced users. During this one-month transition, Telnet
access is left open while everyone gets to use the VPN. This "dual service" also leaves a
proven viable remote access solution in place during the transition—if users begin to
experience problems with the VPN, they can fall back to Telnet while administrators fix it.
After the month is up, an announcement can be made that Telnet is finally being
decommissioned.

# Create a FAQ for the Transition

During the transitional phase, you may discover problems and questions that are common to most
users. Create a FAQ that provides answers and solutions to the end users and post it somewhere
easily accessible, such as a company intranet, or distribute it via email.

# Decommission, Don't Destroy

When most people finish reading a good book, they might put it back on a bookshelf or
even pack it away in a box, but rarely do they toss the book in the trash. You can think
de decommissioned services as being similar to those used books: Even though you're no
longer using a service, you don't rush to destroy its data or break its server down for spare
parts, at least not immediately. What happens if something goes wrong with your new deploy-
ment a day, a week, or even month down the road? What if you need data from the old
server that isn't in your current backup library?
