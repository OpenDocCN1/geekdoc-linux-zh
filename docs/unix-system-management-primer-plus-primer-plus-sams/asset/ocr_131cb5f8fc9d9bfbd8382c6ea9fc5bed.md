# Matching Technical Support Infrastructure Business Size and Customer Needs

The size and organizational structure of your technical support infrastructure is determined by the size and structure of your particular organization. You can't offer your customers reliable, effective technical support unless you develop a technical support infrastructure that matches the types of customers you serve and how those customers communicate with your business. The size of your technical support staff and its response capabilities also play important roles in shaping your technical support infrastructure.

For most small companies of 25 to 100 people, system administrators are the support staff—there is little justification for creating a separate support infrastructure; it's just too much overhead for such a small organization. But if, as system administrator, you are the only person providing the technical support for an organization of over 100 people, you probably should consider adding staff.

You might start with two or three administrators who rotate support duties. As your organization continues to grow, you can build a separate support group. Of course, the support staff size and organization also depends on your company's technology infrastructure. A company with an office network and a single Unix server for development may not need any dedicated support staff at all. However, a company with five development servers, four Web servers, two back-end database servers, twelve developers, and a customer base of 50,000 Web surfers at home needs some support staff.

Use the following guidelines to help you determine the right staffing structure for your organization:

+   * Most organizations should have one system administrator for every thirty Unix servers in the organization.

+   * If you or your staff have trouble coordinating and prioritizing the numerous problems assigned to you, you should evaluate the use of trouble ticket management software.

+   * If you provide connectivity services, as an ISP would, for example, always provide alternative communication methods, such as a phone support line. This option requires upupport staff to answer the phones, system administrators can also rotate phone duties.

+   * Unless hired exclusively for user support, a system administrator's daily non-support workload would typically occupy 80% of his or her day, while the other 20% should be left to handle problems. If more than 20% of your time is spent handling support, consider hiring additional administrators to spread the load.

While small sites and internal customers can get away with a help desk made up of a couple of administrators answering, larger support infrastructures usually follow a layered model. The low-level staff communicate with the customers directly, high-level staff diagnose and repair serious or difficult problems, and mid-level staff handle everything in between. A common real-life manifestation of this is the three-tiered support model, consisting of level 1 support, level 2 support, and level 3 support, which you learn about later in this chapter.
