## Types of Rights

You may be wondering what kind of rights a user can have while using your services. Some of the more common types of rights listed in these policies are listed here:

- Privacy of data
- Customer support
- Adequate notice before scheduled outages

Of all of the rights listed, privacy is the most important right you can give to your users. A statement of privacy rights assures that their email, files, and network communications will not be viewed or modified in any way by staff in a position of authority; such as a system administrator. In addition, privacy rights imply that system administrators will do their best to prevent other users and third parties from breaching the security of their systems and possibly compromising user data.

Not all organizations provide the same rights to their users—organizations provide user rights based on the nature of the organization and its security needs. For example, although a small ISP may protect against internal staff reading others' email, a government agency may require that all incoming and outgoing email be screened for inappropriate or suspicious activities.

## Balancing Rights and Responsibilities

It might be difficult to enforce user responsibilities if you are unable to properly monitor your users due to the rights you provided them. For example, preventing employees from sending personal email is quite difficult if your rights specify that IT staff won't read those messages without consent. Verify that user rights don't prevent you from fulfilling your responsibilities as an administrator.

## Types of Responsibilities

The user rights in this chapter do not come freely; users earn rights by fulfilling certain responsibilities. System administrators determine responsibilities by setting guidelines for appropriate user behavior. User responsibilities differ from site to site, but users should be expected to observe the following globally applicable guidelines:

- Respect the privacy of other users.
- Respect the intended use of system resources.
- Respect the integrity of the system and network (that is, do not attempt to hack into a system).
- Respect the right of other users to use shared resources.
- Protect passwords, smartcards, and other authenticators that could be used to impersonate you.
- Honor the legal protection afforded by copyrights and licenses for material you might post or distribute electronically.
- Do not commit illegal acts using system resources.

Such "universal" responsibilities may be obvious to most people, but some responsibilities are specific to particular businesses and might not be obvious to users. For example, systems at a nonprofit organization might require that users not use system resources for profit, as this jeopardize the organization's nonprofit status. Health insurance companies might require compliance with the HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act), in that data must be kept private.

Users who are ignorant of all responsibilities could participate in behavior that leads to severe legal ramifications for an organization. As the system administrator, you must ensure that all users know and understand their responsibilities. Finding the most appropriate and effective avenues for communicating policies and requiring users to verify that they have read understand the policies are essential steps in formulating user agreements for your system.

## Security Policies

Since the event of the Internet, most organizations have placed an increasing priority on security. You learned about implementing security measures in your system in Chapter 13, "Implementing Security System." Security policies establish the goals and guidelines for your organization's security in a formal document.

In addition to describing system and network security, these policies also define proper user behavior, which is as important to overall system security as any system configuration. The information in this section of the chapter provides a brief overview of drafting and implementing security policy, but the topic is much too broad and complex to cover in a single chapter this book. Several books provide excellent information on establishing system security and crafting effective security policies (see Appendix A for a list of security references). You can find more information in RFC 2196, Site Security Handbook.

## What Is an RFC?

RFC stands for "request for comment" and is the primary method for proposing standards in the Internet community. You can find everything from a formal definition of the FTP protocol to security handbooks in an RFC. A comprehensive archive of all RFCs is located at http://www.fqs.org.

## Choosing the Site Security Administrator

Before establishing any response procedures, you should designate a site security administrator. This person is responsible for maintaining your security policy, implementing its requirements, and monitoring compliance within your organization.

The site security administrator must have the knowledge to understand and implement all parts of the security policy, as well as sufficient privileges to monitor and enforce the policy. In many organizations, a system administrator fits nicely into this role; in larger organizations, the site security administrator can be a full-time position.

## Defining Your Security Goals

The majority of a security policy consists of your organization's security goals. One example of a security goal is to encrypt all company communications over public networks. Another might be to require access cards on every entrance and exit to your company's offices and data centers. Security goals generally fall into the following categories:

- Access to resources
- Electronic data security
- Service restrictions
- Physical security

In addition to setting forth security goals, security policies also can specify the means by which your organization implements security. For example, if your policy requires that all company traffic that passes through a public network is encrypted, it can also suggest the use of SSH for interactive login sessions and a VPN for other types of traffic.

## Use Generic References to Hardware and Software

Although security policies can include information specifying the means for implementing system security, it's best to minimize references to specific hardware and software in your policy. For example, you do not want to change text in your security policy just because you switched to a different SSH client.

## Limiting Access to Resources

Every security policy should define the conditions under which people can gain access to electronic resources. The concept of least privilege, which was explained in Chapter 13, dictates that people should be given only those privileges necessary to do their jobs. Your policy should define different roles in your organization and the privileges assigned to each role. Some examples are shown in Table 16.1.

**Table 16.1 One Company's Roles and Their Associated Privileges**

| Role | Type of Access |
| :--- | :--- |
| System Administrator | Full access to all electronic resources, offices, and data centers |
| Chief Executive Officer | Full access (on request) to all resources, offices, and data centers |
| Sales & Marketing | Sales file shares, customer records |

### Specify Titles, Not Names
Always use titles instead of individual names in your security policy. You want to be able to apply this document to the whole of your organization without changing it every time someone is hired or fired.

## Controlling Electronic Data Security

The security and integrity of electronic data is critical to every organization with a computing infrastructure. Your security policy should define how your data is to be protected on the system level. Some things you might find in this part of a security policy include the following:

- Password selection guidelines
- Password expiration schedule
- File permission guidelines
- Group membership guidelines
- File encryption requirements
- Network traffic encryption requirements
- Remote access requirements
- Privacy requirements
- Email content restrictions
- Procedures for granting access to new hires and revoking access from terminated employees
