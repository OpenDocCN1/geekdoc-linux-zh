
Storms, including hurricanes and tornadoes, can damage both the data center facility and the equipment it houses. Power surges and outages, which you read about earlier in the chapter, can present just one type of storm-related damage. Powerful storms can rip through data center facilities, opening holes in the walls and roof. These types of events can result in structural damage to the equipment or secondary damage from rainwater entering the building.

To minimize damage from floods or storms, you should rack your equipment in a cabinet, which will provide some minimal protection against small projectiles, and all machines should be at least 1 foot from the floor (though higher would be better), in order to prevent water damage. To prevent damage to stored media, make sure that any fire safe you purchase is also waterproof—water is just as damaging to media as fire is.

## HVAC Equipment Failures

If your data center's HVAC system fails, the unregulated temperature and humidity around your systems could reach a point at which your systems begin to fail. The most effective (but expensive) option for dealing with this problem is to install a secondary HVAC system that can take over when the primary system fails. Large collocation facilities employ a secondary HVAC system, but that solution is not economically feasible for small organizations with minimal data centers in their offices. Smaller organizations can purchase inexpensive wall units or portable air conditioners that vent through the ceiling, or out a door, if that is your only method of ventilation. If you lease your data center space, your landlord might be able to hook up for this.

Most system administrators do not monitor (or have the capability to monitor) their HVAC systems; this task is usually the responsibility of a building landlord, a collocation facility, or a facilities team. You can take a proactive approach, and use digital thermometers to monitor the temperature in various areas of your data center. Many vendors offer thermometers that attach to a server's serial port, and some servers and disk arrays actually come with built-in temperature sensors that you can query via the command line or remotely via SNMP.

## Loss of Access

When environmental, physical, or circumstantial events prevent you from accessing your data center, your operation can suffer a severe slowdown or even potential damage. Loss of access is often "collateral damage" in that it results from damage caused by another disaster. You need to take loss-of-access threats as seriously as those of other disasters when planning your recovery procedures.

All kinds of events can cause you to lose access to your data center; fires, floods, and earthquakes are some of the more obvious ones, but some subtle culprits could cause problems—namely, if your data center experiences a minor fire, for example, if that is quickly extinguished and causes little damage, you might think that a bit of clean-up will put the operation back in order. But what if you were to stroll into the next day to find that the data center is surrounded by police tape! The police are investigating arson as a possible cause of the fire, and that investigation could take a week or more. Your data center is now a crime scene, so you have no option but to reschedule routine maintenance, and the maintenance for the rest of the week (and hope that the investigation proceeds to schedule).

Even if you think a police investigation is an unlikely event at your data center, you still might be faced with damage resulting from other strange occurrences. For example, if the building housing your data center is not structurally sound, the roof could collapse, causing major damage and requiring days of work to clean everything up enough to allow safe access to the machine room. If a severe storm like a hurricane passes over your area, the resultant flooding might not damage your data center, but it might render the roads leading to it impassable. If something goes wrong at the data center, critical staff won't be able to deal with the problem.

Additionally, the anthrax scares of 2001 taught us all about building contamination. The Hart Senate Office building was closed for months before it was deemed safe for occupancy. The problem doesn't have to be as serious as anthrax; asbestos is in older buildings, and when it begins to seep into the air, it is dangerous enough to warrant evacuation to remove it.

Contemplating these and other unthinkable situations isn't a pointless exercise in "what if." By trying to anticipate all events that could harm or impede your operations, you're better able to prepare your department to avoid or minimize the damage events might hit. When damage is unavoidable, you'll be prepared with a recovery plan that will get your operation up and running again as quickly as possible. Preparing this recovery plan is a major task that is discussed later in this chapter in "Formulating a Disaster Recovery Plan."

## Real-World Example: Flooded Out

In September 1999, Hurricane Flood had a disastrous effect on the east coast of the United States. The storm hit one ISP particularly hard. A day-long power outage required staff to run portable generators to keep the critical systems in the data center up and running. When it came to rotate the staff the next morning, many technical employees couldn't make it in; some roads to the office were flooded or largely impassable, and that's where many employees lived. By calling staff from towns near the office, the system administrator was able to relieve the workers who had been on "generator watch" the night before. In retrospect, the system administrator of this ISP realized that he could have saved his department a great deal of inconvenience and anxiety by spending the extra money necessary to install turbine generators that didn't require watching or refueling. Who would have thought that a flood would be the event to bring this to?

## Managing Data Loss

One of the most critical assets of any company is its data, whether it consists of financials, customer demographics, or email. Logging a few files here and there is commonplace, and system administrators can mitigate damage with prudent backups. Restoring entire file systems and databases can take some time, during which users can't access that data. Data loss can occur for a variety of reasons, some of which are listed here:

- Hardware failure
- Human error (such as gross misuse of rm -rf)
- Malicious behavior (as a hacker or virus)
- Operating system crash
- Inconsistent backups

Many causes of data loss can be prevented. Chapter 10, “Providing High Availability in Your Unix System,” describes many different methods, including RAID and network redundancy, that provide protection for your data on the back end. Chapter 13, “Implementing System Security,” provides some proactive approaches to monitor and defend against security breaches, which a malicious person or virus could unintentionally destroy your data. Unfortunately, human error is more difficult to manage; proper training and minimizing the use of root and other privileged accounts are the most effective methods for counteracting human error.

Depending on the nature of the data lost, a few employees could lose a couple of spreadsheets, or all of your customers lose months of email. It's up to you and your management to decide what constitutes a disaster and develop plans to recover without.

One of the most important things you can do to minimize loss of data during a disaster is to store backup tapes (or other backup media) offsite in a geographically separate location. This prevents your backup media from being destroyed along with all of your hardware and live data. The frequency at which you move this media offsite will determine how much data you can potentially lose in a site-wide disaster.

## How Far Away Is Far Enough?

Determining the appropriate safe distance from your data center to store media offsite is an interest of dilemma. On one hand, you want your data as far away from any disaster involving your data center as possible. On the other hand, you want your data close enough that you can retrieve it in a reasonable amount of time during a disaster. In general, offsite media should be stored at least 50 miles away, but close enough that it can reach you within 4 hours, or the service time vendor has specified in your contract.

Many companies offer offsite storage of media, but the most well-known company in this field is Iron Mountain. Iron Mountain will pick up boxes of your media, store them in a secure facility, and return them to you on designated dates. The company will also return stored media to you within a specified amount of time following a disaster.
