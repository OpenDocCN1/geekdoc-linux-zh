The security administrator cannot enforce the security policy. Members from each department within an organization should be selected to ensure that each department's processes comply with the policy. Questions and concerns can be brought to the attention of the security administrator and worked out without the administrator having to dive into every aspect of the organization's daily operations.

Ensuring compliance is just one part of enforcement. Users who willfully violate a security policy, by attempting to gain root access to a server or by removing documents from company grounds, for example, may be reprimanded that violations will not be tolerated. A reprimand may come in the form of a verbal warning, or it may be as severe as termination; the severity of the reprimand is the choice of your particular organization.

## Security Waivers

The job of a system administrator carries an enormous amount of responsibility; one of the most important responsibilities is maintaining the privacy of users. It is very easy for a system administrator to actually violate his or her security policy by performing routine tasks like sniffing network traffic or checking for easily guessable passwords. To give a system administrator permission to view sensitive data necessary in the course of performing his or her job, organizations use system administrators documents known as security waivers.

Most administrators and their employers understand that this kind of investigation is part of the job, and that administrators are required to behave in an ethical manner. But this "understanding" may not provide ample protection for the administrator in extreme cases where a user feels that his or her privacy has been violated. A security waiver is a document giving a system administrator the express permission of an organization to view sensitive data in the course of daily operations; it is usually signed by the CTO or CEO of a company.

The security waiver does two things for administrators: It gives them the power to violate certain sections of a security policy to do their jobs, and it protects them against remediation for viewing potentially private or confidential data. However, the waiver should only apply to an administrator's daily operations—casually reading other people's email while sitting home at night does not qualify as daily operations and would clearly violate any security policy as the security waiver.

Following is an example of a very simple but effective security waiver:

> Joe Manager, Chief Technology Officer of Example.Com, hereby grants the Senior System Administrator, Mike Admin, the right to view personal, private, and confidential data in the process of daily operations of the Senior System Administrator. This includes sniffing network traffic, viewing email messages, and viewing the contents of files in the course of diagnosing system problems.

## Using Implicit Agreements

Chapter 15, "Interacting with Users," discussed using display banners to provide information to users before they log in. Many system administrators use banners to display user agreements—a perfect application of this technique. An agreement presented in this fashion is called an implicit agreement, because the user implicitly accepts the terms of the agreement by accessing the system.

The problem with user agreements is that users must agree to them. Distributing paper copies of user agreements and gathering signed agreement forms can require a lot of time and effort—and for many system administrators, distribution is the least of problems associated with user agreements. On the Internet, for example, every person on the planet with a telephone line and a computer has the potential to access your public servers. Given that user base, you have no chance of knowing who is connecting to your servers and what their intentions are.

It would be nice if you could freely monitor and log the activities of all users without consequences, but you need to provide a notice detailing these practices to users before they actually use your services. By notifying users of this practice through a display banner—called a login banner—you establish an implicit agreement which users agree when they access the site. The wording of your agreement should specifically state that users accept the terms of the agreement when they access the system; here is a simple example of an implicit agreement:

This is a private example.com server. Use of this system implies your consent to the monitoring and logging of all activities on this server. Please send all questions and comments on this policy to help@example.com or call extension 1234.

## Banners for SSH

Prelogin banners for SSH (secure shell) can be displayed only if they are supported on the client. Otherwise, you must display the banner text in the server's message of day or as a prelogin banner on the client itself.

In addition to notifying legitimate users of your intentions, this agreement also alerts any passersby to your watchful eyes, much like a home security sign posted on your lawn. Amazingly, this type of warning does deter some users from pursuing malicious activities, though it won't stop a determined hacker.

Your implicit agreements should include contact information, in case a user does not agree or has questions about the policy. Always include an email address and one alternative form of communication, to accommodate users who can't contact you through email or their current computer if they choose not to log in based on the policy.

## Implicit Agreements on Computer Servers

Use implicit agreement banners on all of your servers, even if they are not open to the public. Employees of a company are just prone to break the rules as someone on the outside, and you should warn them against this behavior.

As a Unix system administrator, you may need to write an implicit agreement prelogin banner to be displayed on all user desktop computers. Because desktops typically are a user's gateway into your organization's computing resources, the implicit agreement should be all-encompassing, covering the use of all computing resources, not just a particular desktop machine. The following example might work well for a typical company:

Welcome to the example.com information technology environment. Use of this and other systems within this environment, as well as the use of external systems accessed from this environment implies your consent to the monitoring and logging of all activities. All questions and comments on this policy should be sent to help@example.com or you can call extension 1234.

## Summary

Technology is becoming more powerful and far-reaching every day. Policies and agreements are needed to ensure fair, secure, and ethical use of this technology both inside and outside of an organization. This chapter discusses several types of policies that are generally applicable to the proper management and security maintenance of a Unix network system. Acceptable usage policies and user rights and responsibilities ensure that users agree to your conditions of use and are aware of their own rights. Security policies define an organization's security requirements and enforce them across the organization. Security waivers allow a system administrator to perform his duties without worrying about the implications of viewing personal or confidential information. Each of these policies helps influence the behavior of users and system administrators alike so they all respect the technology resources available to them as well as the other people that share them.
