## Get Good Emergency Contact Information

After you've designated emergency staff responsibilities, you need to make sure you and the other participants of the plan can contact the staff when you need them. Here's the contact information you should compile:

- Work phone number
- Home address
- Home phone number
- Pager number and/or address
- Vacation schedules
- Direct manager
- Backup personnel (who else can perform the same task)
- Emergency contacts

Emergency staff assignments and contact information are part of your disaster recovery procedures; keep this information with your plan and update it along with the rest of the plan (you learn more about updates later in this chapter). Participants cannot be expected to carry around a disaster recovery plan with them at all times; however, at the very least you should make the contact list available separately from the overall plan so people can carry it around with them. One company went so far as to laminate a small wallet-sized card with contact information so it would be as convenient to access as a driver license or credit card.

## Identifying Critical Tasks

There is no master switch to flip that will transition your business into disaster mode. You need to carefully plan the sequence of events that will get your operation through disaster. Even the migration of a single server to another location can become a guessing game if the requirements haven't been researched and documented.

To eliminate any confusion or guessing on your part during disaster, document the requirements of each critical task, including:

- Necessary technology
- Necessary human resources
- Time required for each step
- Locations for each activity
- Dependencies within the process

### Planning Software

Remember, you aren't creating a "to do" list; you're creating a full recipe for recovering each service or function. Using server migration as an example, you can create a list of tasks for moving services from primary center to recovery center. Here's an example of such a list:

| Step | Task | Duration | Dependencies |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1. George | Call secondary site | 5 minutes | - |
| 2. Steve | Call storage facility | 5 minutes | - |
| 3. Jeff & Steve | Drive to hot site | 30 minutes | - |
| 4. Steve | Wait for backup tapes to arrive | 2 hours | 3 |
| 5. Jeff | Verify software is installed and configured on DR server | 10 minutes | - |
| 6. Steve | Restore from backup tapes | 1 hour | 4 and 5 |
| 7. Jeff | Start services on DR server | 5 minutes | 6 |
| 8. Chad | Change DNS to reflect new server's address | 5 minutes | 7 |
| 9. Mike | Test services from office | 15 minutes | 8 |
| 10. Steve | Schedule regular backups | 15 minutes | 6 |

This was a fairly simple, ten-step recipe for migrating services from one location to another. Each step notes the person responsible for that step, the exact task (including details as phone numbers and addresses), the time the task is expected to take, and any dependencies.

## Use the List as a Guide

In your critical task list, some steps might be able to be performed simultaneously or out of order, depending on the nature of each step. In the critical task list in this chapter, for example, step 10 can easily be performed immediately after step 6; Steve may not want to reschedule backups until the emergency team has verified that everything is functional.

This procedure is just one set of tasks that would need to be performed in migration of services during disaster. You need a detailed procedural plan for each service that must be recovered following disaster. You must merge all of these into a central plan.

## Generating a Disaster Recovery Plan

It's now time to take all of the information you've collected in the previous steps and create a detailed recovery plan you can use in any disaster, from a leaky roof to a tornado. Creating this plan can be a difficult endeavor; it requires you to take into account every type of disaster that you could possibly encounter and plan procedures that respond to each of them. Procedures for recovering from a fire are drastically different from recovering data loss caused by human error, but you still need to make sure that you know what you are doing in each situation.

In a fire, for example, hardware, buildings and other valuable infrastructure can be lost; your plan must include provisions for procuring new hardware and network connectivity as well as relocating to another data center. Data loss, on the other hand, requires restoration from backups and possibly reloading of operating systems and software; your recovery plan for such a loss needn't include the replacement of any physical space or equipment.

You have several options for creating your plan. Most organizations choose one or more of the following paths:

- Create the plan in-house.
- Hire a consulting firm specializing in disaster recovery to assist with creating the plan.
- Purchase planning software that helps you organize and maintain the plan.

Smaller organizations with straightforward business processes may be able to create the plan in-house by putting their heads together and constructing a document using Microsoft Word, for example. However, larger and more complex organizations will struggle with this approach without an experienced person to lead the project. These organizations should consider hiring consultants to analyze your business and technology and help create the plan.

### Evaluate Disaster Recovery Consultants Carefully

Any consulting firm that you hire to help with your recovery plan should have the knowledge, training, and real-world experience to analyze your business. Ask about prior engagements, look for industry certifications like Certified Business Professional (CBCP). Above all, ask for sample plans that demonstrate the scope of work. Many organizations choose to supplement the do-it-yourself approach with these.

Disaster recovery planning software collects all of the information necessary for compiling the plan (as you've learned in this chapter), including resources, locations, personnel, dependencies, and even mundane information as serial numbers. The software puts together a comprehensive plan for your business to recover from any type of disaster. Two such applications are the:

- Strohl LDRPS (Living Disaster Planning System)
  http://www.strohl.com/Software/LDRPS/default.asp
- Sungard PreRecovery® Business Continuity Software
  http://recovery.sungard.com/recovery.cfm

The products from Strohl and Sungard are two of the most popular disaster planning applications, and can be purchased independently of any disaster recovery engagement with the vendor. Their solutions are pricey, however, each costing $20,000 for multiuser licenses. The cost of such software might become more manageable as part of an overall disaster recovery engagement.
