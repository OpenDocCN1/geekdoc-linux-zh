
# Using Other Technical Support Staff

Support engineers represent the next level of trained technical support personnel. In large organizations that offer multi-level technical support, support engineers work directly with the help desk staff and might work onsite at the help desk facility. Support engineers are often junior-level administrators who have a better understanding of all the technology at a site fits together. This support personnel have the ability to solve some of the more difficult problems faced by the help desk.

Senior administrators are the highest-trained support personnel in any organization. Senior administrators should have the technical knowledge to support the system and ample experience in diagnosing and fixing problems on the platforms they support. In multi-tiered support systems, senior administrators have access to hardware, configurations, and administrative accounts. These individuals also have all vendor contact information, so they can bring in outside technical support assistance to resolve problems they can't fix.

# Offering Three-Tiered Technical Support

As you learned earlier, many companies need more elaborate technical support services than can be provided by a simple help desk. One common solution is to use a three-tiered support structure that offers advancing levels of technical support for resolving more complex issues. In most three-tiered support systems, the levels are as follows:

- Level 1: Help desk staff. The front-line individuals who take the customer calls for tech support, answer those questions that can be dealt with immediately, and record and route remaining support issues to higher levels of technical support.
- Level 2: Support engineers. When help desk staff are unable to handle a problem on their own, they can pass it on to level 2 support, usually support engineers. Support engineers do not have the access, experience, or technical know-how to fix every problem that flashes across their screens; sometimes the ball is passed to level 3 support.
- Level 3: Senior support administrators. Most senior support administrators consider themselves the last line of defense. When all fail, senior administrators solve the problem. If the problems stem from product issues, senior administrators might bring in vendors to fix problems with their own products. In this case, the senior administrator calls the help desk of a vendor, and the support cycle starts all over at the vendor’s site.

The key to success of three-tiered support is communication. Customers see only the front-line level 1 support staff. An issue is passed to level 2 only if level 1 staff cannot solve the problem. Likewise, level 3 handles support problems only if level 2 pass it on. Eventually, as problems are solved, information is passed back down the communications chain to level 1 staff and the customer. # Using Flexible Technical Support Structures

The three-tiered support model is considered the ideal situation for an organization because each level is isolated from the petty problems of the levels both above and below it. In a three-tiered system, for example, system administrators rarely speak to the customers directly; that's the job of the help desk and, on occasion, the support engineers. And, since the help desk in a three-tiered system doesn't dive into the deeper, more problematic issues, first-level support staff can dedicate their resources to managing trouble tickets and tending to the customers' needs.

Not all organizations, however, have the need or the resources to implement a three-tiered system. A large ISP may need a help desk with support engineers and senior engineers working behind them, but a small local ISP may only need a few help desk staff and the system administrators that maintain the shop scenes. In small organizations, all support staff need to wear many different hats. The help desk may need to know a bit more about the underlying infrastructure to effectively communicate problems to senior engineers, so help desk staff need to understand and involve themselves in the issues addressed by the engineers.

At the same time, the senior administrators need to be able to translate technical jargon into plain English for the help desk, and they might even need to talk to customers directly. Senior administrators in small support organizations can't entirely separate themselves from the duties and responsibilities of the help desk staff. Where organizations exist only online, communication with their customers takes place through email or Web Forms. In these organizations (especially in smaller shops), the engineers themselves may be answering the support mail.

As you can see, anyone can be asked to wear hat at any time in smaller, less-structured organizations. Having a flexible technical support structure staffed by individuals who are prepared to assist in a variety of roles can be cost-effective and the best service model for your customers. Managing this kind of technical support structure can be difficult, though, especially during large-scale outlets. If each person is qualified to find and repair the problem, you may have so many people trying to "help" solve the same problem that you run into the law of diminishing returns—too many people working on one task can hinder your progress. It appears that multiple administrators are just getting in the way of each other; send some people home or assign them to other tasks.

# Developing On-Call Procedures

Like a doctor, a system administrator working in a 24/7 operation needs to be available for emergencies at all times—even during non-business hours (off hours). And, like a doctor, providing this availability is known as being "on call." As system administrator, you are responsible for the on-call procedures for your technical support organization. Organizations vary widely among companies, but all must address three major concerns: delegation, regulation, and communication.

## Delegation

Delegation—designating others to share on-call responsibilities—is essential for any system administrator. To understand how important delegation can be, imagine you work for a large ISP with a site hosting 100 servers, 500,000 customers, and a help desk tending an average of 100 calls per day. Now that you are the only person who is on call for that—that you have to deal with every single problem that the help desk can't handle on its own, every hour of day of every week of every month. That's not a life anyone would want to live. Even if only 5% of all support calls make the help desk, you would be dealing with five problems every day, in addition to your usual daily workload. Responsibilities can lead to job dissatisfaction, inefficiency, poor work performance, and burnout.

The obvious answer to this problem is to delegate some of the responsibility for responding to help desks. In your support organization, arranging for your coworkers to share in the call duties makes life easier for everyone in the long run. System administrators in most companies participate in an on-call rotation, where, for example, each person is assigned on-call responsibilities for one week. During that week, individual is responsible for all off-hour production duties. No one else has to worry about off-hour calls except the individual designated for that's on-call day. The next week, the on-call responsibilities move to another individual, and so on, until the duties rotate back to the first individual, and the rotation begins again.

Rotation tend to work well; they help avoid employee burnout and give people flexibility in arranging their schedules; individuals can swap on-call times with other coworkers when necessary.
