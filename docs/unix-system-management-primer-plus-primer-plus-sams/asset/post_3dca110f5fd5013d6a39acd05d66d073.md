## Planning for a Secondary Facility

When planning the resources you'll need to continue data center operations after an emergency, you must include plans for a secondary site of operation. This secondary site is where your technology operations (and potentially your business) will reside during a disaster that incapacitates your primary data center. As you consider the options for procuring your alternative data center, keep in mind that if the online side of your business will be running, as such, you should research and plan it as carefully as you did your primary data center (in Chapter 2).

The three types of secondary sites offer varying levels of disaster readiness:

- * A cold site is a secondary site that provides the data center, power, HVAC, and other necessary infrastructure. It's not populated with properly configured hardware ahead of time, adding to the minimum recovery time. This is the cheapest secondary site option.
- *A warm site provides the data center infrastructure as well as properly configured hardware. The only thing you need to provide for recovery purposes is an up-to-date set of data from backups. A warm site is more expensive than a cold site, but not expensive as a hot site.
- *A hot site is a completely functional replica—though on a smaller scale—of your production site, including recent application data. Data is usually mirrored in real-time or copied periodically to the hot site to keep updated with the most current data. In a disaster, you should be able to quickly transition all services to the hot site with little downtime or loss of data. This is the most expensive secondary site option.

Your organization's budget and your tolerance for downtime are the primary factors that will drive your decision on whether to pursue a cold, warm, or hot site. A thorough impact analysis will take these factors into account and determine which type of site is right for your organization.

The data center facility might not be the only secondary site you'll need following a disaster. Disasters that make office space unusable create problems beyond resulting from a few servers down. If people have no working space and basic office facilities, they can't keep the business running.

When offices are rendered unusable, the people working there must be relocated to another facility. Many companies that provide secondary sites for disaster recovery also have temporary office space available for customers. Temporary space is usually limited (especially in the case of widespread disasters, such as floods and hurricanes); available space usually is filled on a first-come, first-serve basis. A temporary facility must provide for all of the office functionality you specified in your plan, which may or may not include the following:

- * Individual workspaces
- Desktop workstations
- Phones
- Operator/call network
- Private network
- Internet access
- Voicemail

## Plan Emergency Office Operations Wisely

Only small companies will be able to relocate their entire staff to a secondary location. Not only are workspaces a strain on an emergency partner's resources, they are extremely expensive options in your services contract. Your recovery plan should include a list of the minimum staff that must be relocated to keep the business running, as well as their backups in case someone is not available. Others can return to work after the disaster has ended or they can telecomm. In fact, telecommuting is an attractive option when reserved workspaces start to eat a large hole in your budget.

## Choosing a Recovery Partner

One facility is hard to maintain, unless two or three, so many businesses choose to partner with companies experienced in disaster recovery procedures. These service companies provide the recovery sites, infrastructure, and personnel to help you through the transition. Sungard® Recovery Services and IBMs Global Services division are just two big names in a pool of recovery service companies. Such companies can offer data centers, hardware, Internet connectivity, planning software, and professional consulting services to guide you through the emergency operations process.

Your recovery partner can also provide the hardware that you use during recovery. Companies usually offer hardware identical or at least comparable to your specifications, then tack a fee onto your bill to cover the costs of reserving the hardware for you.

As you've learned in this section, you can plan and procure your own emergency equipment and site operations facility, or you can hire a recovery services company to handle that process for you. Both choices have advantages and disadvantages. The advantages to procuring your own hardware include the following:

- There is a fixed short-term capital cost.
- The hardware can meet your exact specifications.
- The hardware is guaranteed to be available during a disaster.
- You can test configurations and procedures in-house.

Here are some of the disadvantages of procuring your own hardware:

- You must pay for support contracts yourself.
- The cost of buying new hardware can double, as you must also purchase recovery hardware (assuming a one-to-one ratio of production to recovery hardware).
- You are responsible for maintaining those systems while they are idle.
- The hardware, if idle, cannot be used for anything else.On the other hand, the advantages to reserving hardware from a partner are:

- There is no need to purchase hardware yourself.
- The partner is responsible for support and maintenance.
- Your contract might allow for upgrades when next generation hardware is available.
- The partner can load and configure operating systems.
- The partner is responsible for transportation of hardware.

The disadvantages of reserving hardware from a partner include:

- If hardware needs to be delivered or reconfigured, you will have to wait for that to become usable.
- Your hardware may be part of a limited pool of shared resources. (This becomes a factor during regional disasters).
- There are recurring operational costs to keep the hardware reserved.

## Allocating Emergency Staff

Recovery procedures cannot take place on their own; you need the contributions of people responsible for each business function to keep an emergency operation functioning after a disaster. Because a disaster can happen at any time, you need to plan ahead by assigning responsibility for each function to one or more individuals and establishing emergency contact information and methods.
