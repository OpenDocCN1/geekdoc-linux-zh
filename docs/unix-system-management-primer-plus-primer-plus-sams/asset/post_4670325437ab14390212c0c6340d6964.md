As more and more companies extend their businesses onto the Internet, clients and customers are coming to expect services to be available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week—in other words, clients expect high availability of online services. This high-availability requirement is especially true for businesses with international interests, where traditional workday doesn't apply. ## Providing High Availability

Ensuring that your Unix system provides high availability requires that you first understand what a highly available service is. Many people confuse high availability with fault tolerance, but the two terms mean very different things. Fault tolerance strives for 100% availability with no interruptions in service; the goal of high availability, on the other hand, is minimal-interruption in service.

Fault-tolerant systems are able to recover gracefully from an unexpected hardware or software failure. A server with a UPS is a simple example of a fault-tolerant power subsystem, as it can withstand a power failure without any ill effects.

Highly available systems, on the other hand, are able to recover gracefully from certain failures, but do not guarantee 100% uptime; they may experience service outage while the systems are recovering from a failure. For example, Cisco PIX firewalls can be configured so that one unit can take over for another when there is a failure. However, it takes time for the failure to be detected—sometimes up to 30 seconds—and some additional time for the other unit to take over. During this transition, all or part of the firewall functionality may be unavailable.

More traditional fault-tolerant systems use redundant components in parallel to eliminate the impact of any single component. This methodology is best characterized by the switch, in which pairs of computers must agree on the result of any particular operation to ensure the accuracy of navigational calculations. If the computers are in agreement, operations are handed off to another pair of computers where the calculations are recomputed and compared.

Complete system redundancy ensures that hardware and software failures do not interrupt the normal operations of all—a guarantee that's essential to keeping the crew safe. Vendors make fault-tolerant equipment for those of not privy to NASA-funded projects. One of Sun's most recent is the Netra ft 1800, a fault-tolerant Unix server. Every subsystem in the quad-CPU ft 1800 is duplicated, using multiple power supplies, CPUs, drives, and buses. Hardened device drivers prevent data failures, and then fail over redundant components; special PCI card allow for hot swapping of cards that are not normally swappable. You'll pay a hefty price for this peace of mind, though, as of this writing, the Netra ft 1800 stands at $160,000!

In a highly available system, system failures are not eliminated, but system administrators find ways to reduce system failures and work around those that occur. To an outside client, the only perceptible difference between a fault-tolerant system and a highly available system is its uptime. A fault-tolerant system should theoretically be 100% uptime with no interruptions in service. A highly available system would be expected to have, for example, 99.999% uptime (called the "five nines"). This 0.001% of downtime makes all the difference in the world, but is much cheaper and easier to implement in practice. This chapter will focus on the techniques, hardware, and software used to implement high availability solutions on Unix systems.

## Techniques for Providing High Availability

Over the years, system administrators have developed a number of techniques for ensuring high availability. You can apply many of these techniques to your own Unix environment—in fact, you may find them familiar with some of them. The three most important techniques that system administrators use to provide highly available systems are redundancy, failover, and load balancing.

## Redundancy

Redundancy is the simplest technique for ensuring high availability in your systems. By duplicating components of a system, redundancy eliminates single points of failure, any of which could cause a service interruption. Many parts of a system can be made redundant, from entire servers to network interfaces. Some components that are commonly redundant include the following:

* Servers
* Disk drives
* Network interfaces
* Communication lines (T1, T3, and so forth)
* Routers and switches
* Power supplies
* Fans
* Buses

Redundancy can be an expensive option. At the very least, when you purchase a redundant component, you are purchasing twice as much hardware as your service needs to function. Beyond that, you probably need other hardware, software, licenses, or configurations to make the redundant parts work together, which adds even more to the total price of your system. In spite of its costs, redundancy remains one of the most reliable and straightforward methods of achieving high availability, and it is an essential part of many of the high availability mechanisms discussed in this chapter.

## Failover

Failover is a process in which a system transfers functions from a failed component—either hardware or software—to an identical working component. All failover configurations by definition rely on redundant components; at least one of the redundant components must be active, while the others are standby for use should the active component fail.

The best example of a system that utilizes failover components is a RAID (redundant array of inexpensive disks). RAIDs typically contain pools of spare disks to be used in place of a failed disk that was previously in use. These disks can be considered the standby units in a failover configuration, while the disks actually participate in the RAID array are active. The standby disks don't actually do anything until another disk fails. You learn more about RAID later in this chapter.

Failover configurations are not perfect high availability solutions. Because the standby units definition do not do anything until they are called into action, system administrators find them very easy to ignore for long periods of time. Components with mechanical parts (for example, disk drives) are prone to failure, whether or not they are in active use. To solve this problem, you must monitor standby components just as you monitor active components. A spare disk isn't of much use if it doesn't work. Most hardware and software capable of failover provide diagnostics that report problems with failover, but you could always monitor services using the techniques discussed in Chapter 6, "Monitoring Services."

## Load Balancing

Insufficient capacity is a major player in system failures. A web server designed to handle 10,000 page hits per day that instead handling 1 million hits per day is bound to fail due to inadequate resources like CPU and memory. If a server is incapable of providing services due to limited capacity, redundancy and failover techniques won't resolve the problem. Load balancing is a technique used to help minimize problems resulting from inadequate capacity.

## A Single Machine Is a Single Point of Failure

Though increasing the server's capacity might seem the logical solution in this situation, a behemoth server with 16 CPUs and 64GB of RAM is a single point of failure. A failed machine means downtime for your service no matter how powerful the machine. Instead of increasing the capacity, load balancing splits the work over several servers. Hardware or software balances incoming requests for services among the individual servers. You can also use load balancing techniques on routers, switches, and other hardware, but this section focuses on load balancing Unix servers (the concepts remain the same in any application).

What does the distribution of "labor" buy the system administrator who uses load-balancing techniques in his or her infrastructure? First, load balancing can increase overall capacity by distributing work over several components. Second, load balancing can increase uptime by providing multiple components for a single function, ensuring that no single component breakdown can stop the service delivery. That distribution eliminates many of resource contention issues that can exist on a single system, such as processor sharing, excessive memory usage, and paging.

## Load-Balancing Systems Detect Server Failures

Most load-balancing hardware and software are able to detect server failures and all subsequent requests to servers that are still operational. End users never know that a server went down.
