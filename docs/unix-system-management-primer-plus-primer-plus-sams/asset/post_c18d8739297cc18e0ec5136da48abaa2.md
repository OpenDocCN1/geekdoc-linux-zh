Without software such as Remedy, a change proposal would have been sent manually to every person who needs to approve it, and the approvals would have to be gathered and accounted for manually as well. Communications could take place via email, phone, or even in casual hallway conversation. It is important, however, to keep records of change approval communications—written or electronic—to formally document that proposals were approved ahead of time.

Determining the granularity of the changes that require approval is also an important part of the approval-gathering process. You certainly need to get approval for adding a user to a Unix cluster of Web servers, as the latter might seem like micromanagement to you, but are not unknown in large, hardened environments like those of banks and insurance companies. How do you determine a change requires approval? Each company has its own guidelines for this, but in general, any change that has an impact on production systems or users—no matter how small the impact might be—require approval.

Change processes work only if they are enforced; these processes are your responsibility as a system administrator. For example, if one person decides to reboot a machine due to poor performance without going through the approval process, the system breaks down. Incidents such as this one breed further such incidents. Ensure that your coworkers are aware of the urgency of any change approval process that you implement, and the severity of not abiding by its procedures.

## Enforcing Change Approval Procedures

Declaring adherence to change approval procedures is a part of each administrator's annual performance review—or other performance measurements—often provides enough motivation to make everyone comply with these procedures. Repeated failures to comply with the procedures should result in some sort of discipline, ranging from a stern lecture to termination. Discuss these situations with your Human Resources department to find the solution that works best for your organization.

## Change Notification

When you have obtained approval for a change, you need to issue change notifications to all people who might be affected by the upcoming change. A common method for communicating about upcoming system changes is through email.

Depending on the individuals and departments included in this group, you may have to limit or fully explain the technical details of your change in your notification. Everyone receiving the notification must be able to understand what you are doing and how it affects them. An ISP that is rebooting its RADIUS servers (their primary method of dialup authentication), for example, might send the following announcement to its nontechnical customers:

# Chapter 14 • INTERNAL COMMUNICATION

From: Your ISP Engineering Team
To: ISP Customers
Subject: Dialup outage

Dear valued customer,

On Saturday April 6, at 5 AM we will be performing routine maintenance on the servers that handle authentication for your dialup connection. This maintenance will cause a brief outage for new dialup connections during that time; we do not expect the outage to last more than 5 minutes. Existing connections will not be affected by this outage.

If you have any questions or comments about this outage, please send e-mail to support@example.com.

Thank you,
ISP Engineering

Note how this email includes very little technical detail, but still makes it perfectly clear that dialup services will be affected. In general, a good notification message should include the following information:

- Exact dates and times of planned change
- Expected length of any outages
- What services are affected (not which servers)
- What related services will not be affected
- Who to contact in case of concerns

Time the release of change notifications that critical servers are going to be rebooted 5 minutes before you reboot them won't give people adequate time to receive and read the notification, let alone prepare for the event. Institute a minimum lead time for noncritical events; 24 to 48 hours is a common time frame, although you might prefer 24 to 48 business hours, in case weekends and holidays prevent people from seeing the notification. You can read more about scheduling outages in Chapter 8, "Service Outages," in the section titled, "Scheduled Maintenance."

Emergency changes not require any lead time. If a Web server is down, you might have to replace it with a new one to restore service. In such a situation, you don't have the luxury of worrying about providing a 24-hour lead time for users. Establish a list of acceptable emergency situations that can override the notification policy and abide by them. Some examples include the following:

- Service is down
- Disaster (fire, flood, and so on)
- Security breach
- Denial-of-service attack

## Change Logging

Change logging is the practice of keeping thorough and accurate records of every change you make to the Unix system. You can reap great benefits simply by keeping records of the changes you make on your systems, whether you maintain change logs through Remedy or a similar expensive workgroup application, or in a simple text file that contains lists of changes.

The primary benefit of keeping change logs is that the logs provide accountability. If an administrator makes a change on your production Web servers at 5:00 p.m. on Friday, and those servers crash at 6:00 p.m. after everyone goes home, the change logs provide a clear trail of events on your system that leads back to the administrator and the changes he made. Change logs are not tools for placing blame, so much as records that provide a path for backing out changes that cause problems; change logs tell you exactly whom to contact and what measures to take in case you experience a problem with a change.

## Combine Notification with Change Logging

When a problem occurs on a system, instead of guessing what went wrong, you can trace back the change logs from the time of failure to the last time you knew the system was functional. Every change along the way is a potential cause of the problem, and—in most cases—you easily determine which change is responsible.

Every change log should include, at minimum, the following:

- Date and time of the change
- Time takes effect
- The person who made the change
- Where the change was made
- Which services are affected
- Reason for the change

## Use Forms to Ensure Adequate Information

Software-based change management should provide forms with those listed in this section. The back-out plan protects you and the other users and will make the logs consistent. A back-out plan is a list of instructions to follow if a change fails.

Following is an example of a poorly written plan for backing out of a Web server:

- 1. Shut down the new server.
- 2. Change the IP address on the old server back to what it should.
- 3. Start Apache and make sure it works.

As the Web server's administrator might understand these, but they're too vague and ineffective to help users. A more appropriate plan might look like this:

- 1. As root, shut down the new server.
- 2. Login to the old server and su to root.
- 3. Change the IP back to the original Web server address (192.168.1.80) by typing `ifconfig hme0 inet 192.168.1.80` up.
- 4. Restart Apache by typing `apachectl restart`.
- 5. Tail the error log in `/usr/local/apache/logs/error_log` to verify that the server started properly.
- 6. Test the site by connecting to `http://www.example.com` with your browser and verify that the home page is displayed.

The deployment of a new Web server should be accompanied by a change log like this:

| Field | Detail |

| Time of change | Mon Apr 5, 12:01:00 EST 2002 |
| Time takes effect | Mon Apr 5, 12:01:00 EST 2002 |
| Responsible people | Jeff, Scott |
| Where the change was made | the web servers apple and orange |
| Which services are affected | corporate web site |
| Reason for change | deploy more scalable hardware |
