# Restricting Services

Most systems connected to a network offer some services to remote hosts. These services can be broken down into the following four categories:

+   - Business-critical services are required for your organization to function normally. Email, DNS, the corporate Web site, and database servers most likely fall into this category for most organizations. These services must always be allowed to run.
- Required services are required for a system to function normally. Services like SSH, serial consoles, and syslog fall into this category for most sites. These services support the infrastructure of a site and should always be enabled.
- Useful system services are not required for the proper functioning of a site, but they are handy for users of a system. You may use the finger service to find out which of your developers is logged into your Web servers, for example; the finger service is just a handy tool, you could just easily as SSH to the Web servers and use the who command. You can choose to allow useful services to exist on your system, or you can be more paranoid and run only critical services—it is a tradeoff between usability and security that you will have to decide.
- Unused services are enabled on your servers but are not used by any person or machine. If your organization is using SSH to access remote servers, services such as Telnet and remote shell should probably be unused. In this day of exploits (discussed in Chapter 13), there is no reason to keep an unused service enabled for all access. Such services offering nothing but a source of worry for system administrators.

Choosing which services to allow and which to restrict draws upon the concept of least privilege, which was introduced in Chapter 13. In this application of privilege, you are not asking whether a particular user needs access to a service; you are asking whether the system needs the service to be functional.

Your security policy should define your organization's business-critical and system services and specify how to handle the other noncritical services. In general, you should disable noncritical services until they are needed, in order to avoid any possible security breach through services. Your policy should also document the procedures for enabling a service. The policies should call for a written justification and an approval process for enabling new services.

# Be Cautious When Enabling Services

Just because a user finds a service useful, you shouldn't feel compelled to deploy the service immediately on your system. After all, every service running on a system is just another potential doorway into that machine. Enable only those services that you deem necessary or truly beneficial to your organization—and compatible with your organization's security goals.
