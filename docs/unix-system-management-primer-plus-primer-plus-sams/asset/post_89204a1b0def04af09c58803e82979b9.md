# INTRODUCTION

Unix system administration has long been considered a black art by most people, best left to the “wizards” who spend most of their time learning the intricacies of Unix, including the arcane commands, procedures, and configuration files that come with the territory. Numerous books, Web sites, classes, and conferences have attempted to bridge this gap by introducing the various commands and applications used to accomplish certain tasks. As a result of the wealth of Unix training and documentation, many people know how to accomplish certain tasks on a Unix system. But attending a few classes will not make a person a good system administrator.

A true system administrator must be capable of doing much more than performing system maintenance tasks, he or she must have well-developed planning and communication skills and be able to think logically and quickly at the same time. A good system administrator is constantly researching new technologies and assessing their viability, and is ready to adapt to new situations based on prior experience. And don't forget that any successful system administrator also must be dedicated to drive to the data center to fix a computer in the middle of the night. These attributes cannot be taught; they are learned through a combination of education and experience.

So do you learn to be a system administrator? Some universities now offer administration tracks within their computer science programs, but these programs are few and far between, and have little time to mature. Moreover, classroom programs do not give you the real-world experience necessary to run a system in an actual company. In more ways than not, system administration is like a trade of the middle ages, in which a person began a career in a craft by becoming an apprentice to a master craftsman. The apprentice watched the master work and assisted in various ways, while learning more and more about the intricacies of the trade. An apprentice shoemaker, for example, didn't learn how to make shoes; or he or she learned how to be a shoemaker.

Toward the same goal, this book teaches you how to become an effective system administrator. Instead of focusing on the usage of commands to perform tasks within your system, this book introduces valuable techniques and concepts that help you become an efficient, competent, and professional system administrator, with topics that range from choosing operating systems to electronic communication methods. You learn how to design, deploy, maintain, and update a Unix system, and you learn how to avoid issues that result in management and user frustration, as well as unnecessary costs and downtime that contribute to them.

Unix System Management Primer Isn’t a reference or a “cookbook,” though many of the important tools and methods it discusses in each chapter are accompanied by examples and demonstrations to give you a feel for how they work. This book is meant to supplement Unix references and cookbooks by giving you an insider’s view of how system administrators function in the real world.

System administrators are in demand, but our jobs are more complex than ever before, receive a great deal of scrutiny, and demand higher accountability. Planning, coordination, and testing are now central to any system administrator's job; we can no longer simply jump into a project and hack it together in a couple of days. By reading this book, you have already realized this, and are ready to enhance your skills both yourself and your organization. The information in this book helps you do just that.

# How This Book Is Organized

Part I starts from scratch and introduces the planning process. Here you learn how to transform a list of business goals into a functional system architecture, and how to make the right decisions in the process. You also learn how to move your systems into production and test them.

Part II focuses on the maintenance of your new systems, as a system is only as good as its support. Topics discussed in this part include support infrastructures, methods for monitoring systems, patching and upgrade procedures, dealing with outages, and recovering from disasters such as fire or crash.

Part III introduces concepts for tuning, sequencing, and automating your systems, as well as methods of planning for future expansion. This part of the book documents techniques that you will find immensely useful on your systems. Many of these tools receive little attention in print, yet they are part of the standard system administrator's tool belt.

Topics in Part IV cover the human aspect of system administration. The topics covered in this part of the book also receive coverage in typical system administration references, and they include communicating with your peers, managing your relationship with your users, and understanding standard policies and agreements common in today's information technology environments.

# For Your Attention

This book uses some special elements to draw specific types of information to your attention. Located throughout the book, you'll find Notes, Tips, and Cautions in graphic boxes. The information in these elements will help you better understand the issues and approaches discussed in the surrounding text.

I've also included a number of Real-World Examples throughout the book. These short vignettes offer you a look at the "front-line" experiences of system administrators in a variety of business, ISP, and academic environments. By learning about the successes, challenges, and mistakes other system administrators in the course of managing Unix systems, you gain some of the valuable insight you might receive if you were learning the job of system administration from the side of a seasoned veteran. You can build upon the information you gain from these real-world system administration experiences to find your own creative solutions and best practices for keeping your Unix systems running smoothly and effectively.
