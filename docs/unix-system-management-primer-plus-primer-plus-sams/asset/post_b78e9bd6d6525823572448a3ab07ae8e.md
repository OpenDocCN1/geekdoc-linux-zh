## Controlling the Data Center Environment

Maintaining a stable, proper environment in the data center is critical to maintaining a stable operation, but it's often one of the most overlooked factors in new deployments. A network's physical environment incorporates a number of qualities, including the following:

- Temperature
- Humidity
- Air quality
- Fire suppression
- Noise

A poor environment can cause hardware to perform unreliably or even fail completely. Taking the time to maintain your facility's environment can save hours of downtime and thousands of dollars in equipment replacement costs.

## Controlling Temperature

If you do nothing else for your data center's environment, maintain it at a constant temperature. All pieces of hardware, from electric shavers to high-end servers, have operating specifications, including temperature limitations. Controlling the temperature around your computer system requires cooling the air surrounding and dissipating the heat that machines produce. You should evaluate this process in any data center you are building or leasing to make sure it meets the needs of your hardware.

Ambient temperature is the temperature of the air flowing around your hardware. Computer hardware needs to be surrounded by cool air because it draws in the surrounding air to cool its internal components. Those components, including processors, generate heat. Heat sinks absorb a lot of generated heat, but fans also play an important role by pulling cooler air over the hotter parts of the machine (including the heat sinks) and transferring the heat energy out of the machine case.

## Heat Sinks and Fans

A heat sink is a block of material that draws heat away from a source like a CPU or memory module to cool it down. These heat sinks themselves can become very hot and other components to overheat, so fans are used in servers to force cool air over them and blow the hot air out through a vent. Both elements are essential as processors and memory continue to generate more and more heat as they evolve.

## How Much Heat Can a Component Generate?

How much heat can something like a processor generate? Tom's Hardware Guide (http://www.tomshardware.com) took the liberty of removing heat sinks from various modern processors and recorded the results. Amazingly, several CPU cores reached temperatures of 600°F, destroying both themselves and their motherboards!

You will probably use a central HVAC (heating, ventilation, and air conditioning) system to surrounding your computer hardware with cold air. Computer equipment works best in constant temperatures, so the HVAC system should be reliable and capable of responding to temperature changes inside and outside the building. Because machines and humans need different environmental temperatures, the temperature controls for the computer equipment should be separate from those of adjacent working areas.

Server hardware typically operates best in temperatures ranging from 65°F to 75°F This temperature range is within the operating specifications of most hardware, and humans can work comfortably within this range. If your computer system functions in a "lights-out" environment, where no people are working, keeping temperatures at the cool end of the temperature range. However, in a company server room or large colocation facility where people work in the computer area for hours every day, you may want to maintain temperatures at the upper end of range.

## Measure Temperature Near the Machines

The temperature of the air near the machines—not the overall room temperature—is important for protecting the machines' functions. If you own your own data center, you can experiment by setting the room temperature at different levels, and then measuring the ambient temperature near the machines at each level to find the setting works best. If you use a raised-floor system, you can pump air directly up through the floor into the racks, rather than dissipating it through the room: In that situation, the temperature in the racks can be quite cooler than the room temperature, focusing the cool air where it is needed.

If your equipment is housed by a large hosting company, you won't need to worry about climate control. Most hosting companies design their HVAC infrastructure for a full-capacity data center, with every rack fully populated. When building your own data center, with an HVAC contractor to make sure you install the system best suited for your environment.

Be prepared to tell the contractor how much your hardware dissipates so if she knows how powerful the HVAC system should be. All hardware manufacturers provide nominal heat dissipation specifications for their hardware, expressed in BTUs (British thermal units) of released heat or watts of energy used (1 watt of power dissipates 3.412 BTU/hour). Add up the BTUs of all heat-dissipating equipment to calculate the total BTUs of released heat your HVAC system must cool. Table 2.1 contains the heat dissipation specifications for various servers.

Don't forget to include the BTU ratings of all of your hardware when calculating total heat generated by your equipment. Tape drives, disk arrays, routers, switches—even the fans used to cool racks—generate heat. ## Controlling Humidity

The optimal humidity for a data center is around 40%. If the humidity is too low, static electricity can build up, which can damage sensitive components. If the humidity is too high, condensation can form on the hardware, which can cause short circuits. You should use a humidifier or dehumidifier to maintain the humidity at the desired level.

## Humidity in Office Buildings

Some office buildings are not equipped with humidity control, which can lead to issues for both people and equipment. It is important to ensure that the humidity in an office environment is kept within a comfortable range.

## Maintaining Air Quality

Many people overlook the importance of maintaining the quality of the air, but unclean air can damage sensitive equipment. Dust, pollutants, and other particles can clog fans and lead to overheating. You should use high-quality filters to remove these contaminants from the air. Air can come from many places: dust from clothes and carpets, cigarette smoke, and the air pulled from outside. Your HVAC unit's filters should block most of these, especially in larger facilities.

## Fire Suppression

Fire suppression is a must in any data center, and you should make sure you or your hosting facility has taken the appropriate precautions. A fire in your data center could cost you millions of dollars in hardware, lost customer data, and downtime. Some common fire-suppression systems include the following:

- Halon Halon gas stops the fuel-burning chemical action of fire. Halon systems expose tremendous amounts of fire-quenching gas in a short period of time and leave behind residue. These systems require extensive infrastructure; data center doors need to be sealed, and smoke and fire detectors need to be connected to a central system so that all power to the data center (equipment and HVAC) shuts off before the halon is released. Unfortunately, halon has been shown to contribute to ozone depletion; in 1993 the EPA banned production of halon, so no new systems are being installed and existing systems must be replaced with recycled halon.

- FM-200 and INERGEN These are two successful alternatives to halon. Both systems force gas into the environment to stop the fire's burning action, and neither leaves a residue or depletes ozone. FM-200 is less expensive to install than INERGEN, but tends to be more expensive to recharge. Like halon, both must take control of the power and HVAC systems to shut them down before activation.
