Fault-tolerant systems are able to recover gracefully from an unexpected hardware or software failure. A server with a UPS is a simple example of a fault-tolerant power subsystem, as it can withstand a power failure without any ill effects.

Highly available systems, on the other hand, are able to recover gracefully from certain failures, but do not guarantee 100% uptime; they may experience service outage while the systems are recovering from a failure. For example, Cisco PIX firewalls can be configured so that one unit can take over for another when there is a failure. However, it takes time for the failure to be detected—sometimes up to 30 seconds—and some additional time for the other unit to take over. During this transition, all or part of the firewall functionality may be unavailable.

More traditional fault-tolerant systems use redundant components in parallel to eliminate the impact of any single component. This methodology is best characterized by the switch, in which pairs of computers must agree on the result of any particular operation to ensure the accuracy of navigational calculations. If the computers are in agreement, operations are handed off to another pair of computers where the calculations are recomputed and compared.

Complete system redundancy ensures that hardware and software failures do not interrupt the normal operations of all—a guarantee that's essential to keeping the crew safe. Vendors make fault-tolerant equipment for those of not privy to NASA-funded projects. One of Sun's most recent is the Netra ft 1800, a fault-tolerant Unix server. Every subsystem in the quad-CPU ft 1800 is duplicated, using multiple power supplies, CPUs, drives, and buses. Hardened device drivers prevent data failures, and then fail over redundant components; special PCI card allow for hot swapping of cards that are not normally swappable. You'll pay a hefty price for this peace of mind, though, as of this writing, the Netra ft 1800 stands at $160,000!

In a highly available system, system failures are not eliminated, but system administrators find ways to reduce system failures and work around those that occur. To an outside client, the only perceptible difference between a fault-tolerant system and a highly available system is its uptime. A fault-tolerant system should theoretically 100% uptime with no interruptions in service. A highly available system would be expected to have, for example, 99.999% uptime (called the "five nines"). This 0.001% of downtime makes all the difference in the world, but is much cheaper and easier to implement in practice. This chapter will focus on the techniques, hardware, and software used to implement high availability solutions on Unix systems.

# Techniques for Providing High Availability

Over the years, system administrators have developed a number of techniques for ensuring high availability. You can apply many of these techniques to your own Unix environment—in fact, you may find them familiar with some of them. The three most important techniques that system administrators use to provide highly available systems are redundancy, failover, and load balancing.
