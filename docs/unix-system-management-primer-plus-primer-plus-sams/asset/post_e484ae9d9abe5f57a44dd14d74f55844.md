# Real-World Example: Compatibility

A large ISP with a significant investment in Sun hardware and Solaris decided that some services would be better handled by Linux servers. Technically, this appeared to be the right choice, but the system administrators ran into a snag when they realized that all of the compiled packages, configurations, account maintenance tools, and load procedures that took six months to finalize on a platform had to be redesigned and redeployed to work with Linux. While the service ran fine on Linux, the infrastructure to support it took two months to complete, bringing the project to a screeching halt. The project was eventually completed, but two months were longer than anticipated by the engineers.

# Choosing Hardware and Software

Once you've ascertained your project's technical goals, priorities, and timelines, you're ready for the next step in your system architecture planning process. That step involves choosing hardware and software. The way you go about making those choices depends upon your approach to system design.

In general, there are two ways to approach system design: the top-down approach and the bottom-up approach. The top-down approach involves starting from the end of your design and working your way down to the system that will make that end result. The bottom-up approach begins by considering the network hardware and infrastructure, and designing up up from that to reach your end result.

To better understand these approaches, consider the pyramid in Figure 1.1. The bottom of the pyramid represents the foundation of your architecture: the hardware and network infrastructure that everything runs on. The top rows represent the pinnacle of your design: the business goal that jumpstarted the project in the first place. The top-down approach starts the process at the top of the pyramid—at the highest conceptual level—and works its way down to the bottom, or the components of the individual systems. The bottom-up approach is the exact opposite: Figure out what hardware you want to use, and design your software to run on that hardware.

In most cases, you use a top-down approach to system design. Rarely will management show you a pile of hardware work half a million dollars and ask, “What can we do with this stuff?” Instead, management will present you with a problem or a need and ask you to find a resolution. Because you know what management wants the system to provide, it makes sense to start with that idea and work your way down to the bits and pieces of hardware you'll need to make the idea a reality.

Using the top-down approach, you work through the business and technical goals and arrive at your first architecture decision—choosing the software for your system. Software comes in many forms, such as web servers, databases, and operating systems, and you must take into account aspects of each type of software when comparing products. When you've chosen software, you're ready to select the hardware upon which the software will run. The following sections discuss the issues involved in making these important decisions for your organization.

# Choosing Application Software

Application software provides the functionality of your product. Web servers, databases, text editors, and compilers are all applications that contribute to your technical and business goals. Using a top-down design approach, the ideas behind your project should translate directly into the functionality of the application software. Web sites need web server software. Accounting departments need financial packages that can manage the books. Online stores need shopping cart software. Public login servers need hundreds of public domain applications.

You may be the one choosing and maintaining this software, someone else in the company may be making the choice, or your company may develop the application software. In each case, you want to work closely with the people running, developing, and/or vending the application. # Simplify Platform Management
The 2000 SAGE Survey reports that system administrators manage an average of five operating systems. Managing many operating systems can be difficult—even when they're just distributions of Linux. Choosing one operating system and sticking with it makes the daily care and feeding of your servers simple and easy; you don't have to repeat processes, rewrite documentation, or mentor patches for each system.

Choose your operating system carefully, because you can't go back and change your mind, at least not without an enormous amount of effort. Here are some of the factors you should consider:

- Application support: Choose an operating system that is supported by your application vendors, where run into problems.
- Your expertise: Go with your strengths and choose an operating system that takes advantage of the expertise you have developed over the years. If you're fluent in only a few operating systems, and none of these is the best choice, make sure you're prepared to upgrade your skills to manage the system you choose.
- Vendor support: What kind of support does the operating system vendor provide? Is there 24/7 phone support where you can talk to an engineer? Can you get onsite support if necessary? How much will it cost?
- Market share: Never underestimate the power of a massive user base! Best of the support comes from the end users; you can often find an answer more quickly on a newsgroup or mailing list than by calling a support line.
- Features: You may choose an operating system because of one or more unique features. For example, a server needs support for efficiently searching directories hundreds of thousands of files may need a specialized file system that requires kernel modifications. In this case, Linux may be the most appropriate choice, as the source code is freely available and can be modified as you see fit. The BSD family of operating systems also falls into this category.
