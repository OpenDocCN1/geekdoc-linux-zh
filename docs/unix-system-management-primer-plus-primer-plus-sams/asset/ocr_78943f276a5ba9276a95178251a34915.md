# Maintaining Air Quality

Many people overlook the importance of maintaining the quality of the air that flows through the data center, but unclean air can damage sensitive computer equipment. If you've cracked the case of a server or workstation that hasn't been opened in months, you probably amply noticed a measurable amount of dust piled on top of the hardware inside. This dust layer interfere with the functions of electronic components and clog intake, exhaust, and CPU fans. Dust and pollutants make the air uncomfortable—and unhealthy—for people working near the computer system,

Air contaminants can come from many places: dust from clothes and carpets, cigarette smoke, and the air pulled in from outside. Your HVAC unit's air filters should block most of these particles from being recycled back into your data center; they should be checked monthly, especially at larger facilities.

Canned air is a popular tool used to clean internal dust buildup in machines, but it forces dust into the air which is just likely to end up inside of another machine. If you use this method to clean your machines, remove the machines from their racks so the process does not introduce dust into other systems. You can use a handheld vacuum or remove the machine from the data center to prevent dust from getting into your data center at all. At the very least, blow the dust in the direction of the HVAC system's intake so the air filters can catch it.

# Fire Suppression

Fire suppression is a must in any data center, and you should make sure you or your hosting facility has taken the appropriate precautions. A fire in your data center could cost you millions of dollars in hardware, lost customer data, and downtime. Some common fire-suppression systems include the following:

+   - Halon Halon gas stops the fuel-burning chemical action of fire. Halon systems expose tremendous amounts of fire-quenching gas in a short period of time and leave behind residue. These systems require extensive infrastructure; data center doors need to be sealed, and smoke and fire detectors need to be connected to a central system so that all power to the data center (equipment and HVAC) shuts off before the halon is released. Unfortunately, halon has been shown to contribute to ozone depletion; in 1993 the EPA banned production of halon, so no new systems are being installed and existing systems must be replaced with recycled halon.

+   - FM-200 and INERGEN These are two successful alternatives to halon. Both systems force gas into the environment to stop the fire's burning action, and neither leaves a residue or depletes ozone. FM-200 is less expensive to install than INERGEN, but tends to be more expensive to recharge. Like halon, both must take control of the power and HVAC systems to shut them down before activation.
