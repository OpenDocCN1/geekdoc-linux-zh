# Using Generators for Emergency Power Supply

Generators can produce electricity to replace a failed power supply for long periods of time, as a result, generators can be indispensable during long power outages. Most generators are powered by gasoline or turbine engines, as you learn in later sections. These systems are inexpensive, and they require monitoring, upkeep, and maintenance to ensure that they'll be ready to step into action when necessary. In addition, they require a UPS to properly condition the power before it reaches your systems, in order to avoid spikes and other aberrations that can potentially harm electronic equipment.

If you rent space in a collocation facility, you probably don't have to worry about supplying (or maintaining) generator backup for your equipment. Most large data centers have their generators to power their own and their customers' systems throughout power loss. Nevertheless, don't assume that your provider's system meets your needs; ask these questions:

+   * At what point during a power outage are generators brought online?
* Do you notify customers when a power outage occurs?
* How long can the generators run on one tank of fuel?
* Who provides the fuel and how often are the generators refueled?
* How often are the generators tested? Is there risk to your equipment during these tests?
* Is the transition from wall power to generator power smooth, or is there a short outage during the switchover? Do you have UPS backup to cover this outage?
* How is the decision to make switch back to utility power?

If you run your own data center in a small office environment, purchasing and maintaining a generator system can be costly. Generally, you can either purchase your own portable generators (they cost anywhere from several hundred to several thousand dollars depending on capacity) for emergencies or hire a company to install a larger turbine generator, usually located directly outside of your building. The risks and responsibilities that accompany each option are discussed in the the sections that follow. If the risks, costs, or resources seem too much for your organization, consider moving your systems to a collocation facility where power management is the responsibility of that facility's staff, not yours.
