If you wanted to store your files in the “music” drive under your home directory, you would change this to “~/music/%Aa/%dd/%n.ogg”.

What do the funny-looking parameters mean? These are detailed in the help file, but to spare you the busywork of looking them up, the %Aa parameter is replaced by the artist’s name, the %dd is replaced by the album name, and, of course, the %n is replaced by the track number.

Where does Grip find this information? Grip looks up an ID3 on the Internet, using a signature contained in most audio tracks. The DiscDB tab has a link to an online database that Grip uses by default.

The ID3 and Proxy tabs contain specialized options that I’ve never had to worry about, but you might need to check them out if you’ve got particular needs. The Misc tab contains two options that I like a lot: “Do not lowercase filenames” and “Do not change spaces to underscores.” Of course, your preferences may vary.

# **Tools to convert formats**

What if you’ve already ripped your entire Barry Manilow and John Tesh collection in a format such as MP3 or WMA? The cynic in me might suggest that you press Delete and acquire some Bruce Springsteen and Rush, but then my wife would have me sleeping on the couch for the next decade or so.

As with images, many tools are available for converting your audio files from one format to another. However, this idea is generally frowned upon. The reason is that compressed formats such as MP3 and OGG achieve much of their benefit by eliminating parts of the track that are either redundant or that human beings won’t hear. However, the MP3 and OGG people use different algorithms, so, in essence, they’re each getting rid of different parts of the data. Thus, converting from one of these formats to another will result in a “double dip” of data loss, to the extent that it could very well harm the eventual playback of the track.

If you insist, however, the following Web site lists some third-party tools:

[http://freshmeat.net/search/?q=convert%20ogg%20mp3](http://freshmeat.net/search/?q=convert%20ogg%20mp3)

You’re on your own, of course, from here.

# **Playing a ripped track from the hard disk**

Once you have a track on your hard disk, how do you play it? Yes, you can use Grip to play tracks, but that’s not its primary purpose, so its features are rather limited. The playback functionality is there primarily so you can verify that the ripping process is working properly.

Fedora Core comes with several music players that provide functionality way beyond simple playback; the one you choose will depend primarily on your personal likes and dislikes.

Rhythmbox is a file and radio-station music player written for GNOME, and is available through Main Menu \ Sound and Video \ Music Player, while XMMS is a cross-platform multimedia player that handles music (as well as videos) quite nicely. XMMS is available through Main Menu \ Sound and Video \ Audio Player.

Both players have the capability to act as a jukebox. You add tracks to a master library, and then create playlists by selecting individual tracks from the library. You could create one playlist to act as background music while you’re working, and another to use during your Friday lunchtime table-tennis match.
