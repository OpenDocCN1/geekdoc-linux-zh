## Digital Images

Working with digital images is the newest mainstream application for the personal computer, and Linux has a variety of tools to help you do so. In this chapter, I cover a range of applications that will help you download pictures from your digital camera, capture screen shots of your computer's monitor, or draw freehand (should that be "freemouse"?). Then I'll show you how to modify the results to suit your needs, and finally, view those images either one at a time or in a slide show.

I love my digital camera. With a houseful of kids who are involved in three zillion activities, I could never afford the film bill that would show up every month if I tried to work with silver halide film like I did in the old days. With a digital camera and a 512 MB flash card, I can shoot hundreds of pictures during an event, knowing that my only costs are a few errant electrons and the minuscule degradation of a rechargeable battery.

When the camera manufacturers finally packed this capability into a very small, yet feature-complete camera about two-thirds the size of a deck of cards, I could carry a camera around with me everywhere—and I do. I never leave the house without my keys, my cell phone, and my camera. This way, I've always got the camera—and that means I can always take that one shot, whereas before I would have said to myself, "Boy, if I just had a camera..." However, hooking up a digital camera to a Linux computer wasn't always the easiest. Between immature software and hardware incompatibilities, one had to go through significant work to connect to a digital camera, and then run the software necessary to download its pictures. With Fedora Core, however, digital photography on Linux is ready for prime time. In fact, I've found that it's actually easier and more reliable than using Windows.

But photos aren't the only digital images I work with on a computer. As you can imagine, I spend a lot of time capturing screen shots of my computer monitor to accompany what I've written, and once in a while I even doodle by hand.

And once images are on a computer, there are oodles of things to do with them—modify and tweak them, for sure, as well as set them up for viewing by others. Here's how to get started with digital images on Linux.

## Connecting and downloading images from your digital camera

One tool that comes with Fedora Core—called gtkam—is a simple and straightforward application that allows you to download images from a digital camera to a computer. Using it takes only a few steps.

First, open the tool by selecting Main Menu | Graphics | Digital Camera Tool. The bare gtkam window appears, shown in Figure 1.

Next, plug your camera into your computer, and turn the camera on, making sure it's set to download pictures. The exact settings, of course, will depend on the specific model of camera. Most recent digital cameras come with a cable that plugs straight into a USB port, and mine, although a couple of years old, was no different. I did have trouble getting the computer to recognize my camera when I tried to plug it into the USB port of my notebook docking station; when I undocked the computer, the computer recognized the camera just fine. Interestingly enough, the same thing happens with a Windows-based notebook from the same manufacturer: The docking station for that computer (a slightly different model) doesn’t have a functional USB port, but plugging the camera directly into the computer’s USB port works fine.

In gtkam, select Camera | Add Camera to open the Select Camera dialog (Figure 2). The Select Camera dialog gives you two options. You can select your camera from a pre-populated list, or have gtkam try to detect your camera itself.
