# Which Linux?

You’re reading this book to get started with Linux, and you’re coming from the perspective of a Windows power user. The rise of Linux in the popular culture has also given rise to a large number of half-truths, myths, and outright lies. These can serve to cause people to set unrealistic expectations, and thus be disappointed by their first exposure to Linux. In order to help you develop realistic expectations, this chapter will provide the expectations for Linux usage that I started out with. Once you create your own set, you can then evaluate your progress in meeting those expectations. In addition, I’ll discuss some common misconceptions about Linux; offer some thoughts about choosing a distribution; and explain why this book covers Fedora Core in print, and other distributions through electronic downloads.

I’ve been building custom software applications on Microsoft platforms since the mid-1980s. I was a Microsoft Most Valuable Professional (MVP) from the mid ’90s until last year, a Microsoft Certified Professional (MCP) during the late ’90s and received the first Microsoft Visual FoxPro Lifetime Achievement Award in October of 2002. In the summer of 2002, I started investigating Linux, and have had a Linux-based computer as my primary desktop since that fall. By any account, that was a radical change, and not one that was made lightly.

While making the decision to switch to a Linux-based operating system, I had to evaluate what I was expecting from Linux, write down my expectations, and later go back and reevaluate, based on the progress I had made.

I also had to look at some of the hearsay that surrounds Linux. Many of my friends in the Microsoft world looked askance at my decision---some to the point of severing ties with me because I was “a traitor,” that I had succumbed to the “lies of open source,” and that I had a “secret agenda” and was using Linux to “further my own means.” You can imagine the conversations we’ve had there.

And then there was the decision of which distribution. According to the Web site [www.distrowatch.com](http://www.distrowatch.com), there are 225 active Linux distributions. That raises the question: “Which one?” Fortunately, I was able to narrow the field down because many of them are specialized or otherwise inapplicable for general-purpose desktop use. Still, a half dozen distributions have mindshare significant enough to warrant some consideration.

Now, about you. This book is actually about you---helping you transfer your Windows skills to Linux. You use personal computers to get something done, not just for the sheer joy of using a computer, but you do enjoy making the machine bend to your will.
