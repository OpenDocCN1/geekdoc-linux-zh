# **Choice**

Open-source software like Linux offers choices—choices that proprietary vendors do not want you to have. Proprietary vendors endeavor to lock you in to their platform; indeed, they have been doing that since the railroad robber barons of the mid-1800s tried to restrict usage of their tracks via the implementation of different gauges of tracks.

It is in the interest of a proprietary manufacturer to keep you tied to their platform, and thus restrict your choices to just those that they will allow. The end result is that you will end up paying higher prices than you would in a free market where there were many choices.

“Open source,” on the other hand, is all about choice. Don’t like a particular browser, office suite, or e-mail client? You’ve got many alternatives to choose from. And because there is competition between the providers of these choices, they need to compete on both features and quality.

A monopoly doesn’t have to concern itself with features or quality, because there’s no reason to.

Every time I upgraded my development tools in Windows, I was forced to upgrade the browser and a myriad of other components, whether I wanted to or not. And there were significant reasons for not wanting to just upgrade those components without a choice, as you’ll see in the next section. With open-source software, you don’t get a set of unwanted or needed components jammed down your throat against your will.

I like being able to choose which religion I practice, which political party to belong to, which way to educate my kids, and which clothes to wear and how long to wear my hair. I like the choice that open-source software offers, too.

# **Security**

I feel open-source platforms offer better security from dangers such as attacks and viruses for many reasons. These reasons include publicly available code, and a lack of conflicting political and economic agendas.

Here’s an example that illustrates some of these reasons. Over a year ago, Microsoft announced that a severe NT 4 vulnerability wouldn’t be fixed. They claimed that they—a company with tens of thousands of very smart people and $50 billion in the bank—didn’t have the resources to fix it.

With open-source software, the code is available for all to inspect. With proprietary software, no one is allowed to see the source code except a few select employees. How can Microsoft’s claim be verified? It can’t.

Microsoft has a vested interest in getting their customers to regularly upgrade their software. They don’t want their customers to use old versions of software. I feel it’s much more likely that they simply wanted to pressure customers who are still using NT 4 to upgrade to not fixing problems with NT 4. By doing so, Microsoft saves money in not producing a fix as well as generates money via upgrades.

A third reason is the simple matter of ego driving quality. I have more confidence in the quality of open-source code itself because it’s produced by people who *want* to do so, not because they *have* to in order to make a living, or because their bosses ordered them to. Who would you rather have perform your heart surgery—a fellow who needs to do the operation because he needs the money to pay off a loan shark by Friday, or a guy who is independently wealthy, and who went through med school because she wanted to become a great surgeon?
