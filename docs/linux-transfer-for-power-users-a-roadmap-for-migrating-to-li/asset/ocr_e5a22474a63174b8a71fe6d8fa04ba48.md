Second, you can decide what attributes you want to save. I just save the photos themselves, but you may choose to save other stuff as well.

Finally, you can choose how you want the files to be named. The files on disk are named IMG_nnnn.JPG, where nnnn runs from 0001 through 9999. I use a custom naming scheme where the first characters are the date in CCYYMMDD format, as shown in **Figure 8**.

If I didn’t do it this way, I’d end up with a file named IMG_0001.JPG (and so on) every time I downloaded pictures. With a custom naming scheme, I don’t have to keep files in separate folders just to avoid naming incompatibilities.

As soon as you click the OK button in the “Save photos...” dialog, gtkam will start the downloading process, as shown in **Figure 9**.

The thermometer bars at the bottom of the gtkam window display the progress downloading each file as well as the progress of the whole collection. An indicator showing which file is being downloaded is also displayed in the status bar, and this indicator can be misleading at first. It shows you the name of the file on the camera, not the file name that is being saved on the computer. Thus, if you have chosen a custom file-naming scheme in the “Save photos...” dialog, those custom names won’t show up in the gtkam window’s status bar, but the files on your computer’s hard disk will be named as you desired.

The gtkam tool has a couple of other interesting menu options. The Camera | Summary menu item displays an informational dialog about your camera, including the amount of space on its storage media. The 256 MB flash card in my Powershot had eight pictures on it when I made a screen shot for **Figure 10**.
