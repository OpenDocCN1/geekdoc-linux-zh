At this writing, the fedora-list mailing list generates between 100 and 250 messages a day. Some people feel that’s a lot of messages, while others aren’t quite as cowed. (I used to belong to a list that generated close to 1,000 messages a day, so 200 doesn’t phase me at all!) Depending on your experience and personality, you may decide to shunt the mailing list messages off into a separate folder in your e-mail client (investigate “filter” options if you’ve not done this before) so that your inbox isn’t continually filled (and you’re continually distracted).

Once you’ve subscribed, you’ll receive a confirmation e-mail that you’ll need to respond to. (That’s so that “friends” of yours don’t subscribe you to the list as a prank, much like kids fill out business reply cards in magazines with the address of their school’s principal.) As soon as you do, you’ll start getting messages from the list.

Oh, and if you’ve decided that a particular mailing list isn’t for you, don’t send “Please unsubscribe me” messages to the list. There’s a “To unsubscribe” link at the bottom of every message on the list.

# ## Getting help effectively

Once you’re subscribed, you’ll want to read some messages and get the feel of the list. Each mailing list has its own personality, and fedora-list is no exception. If you’re not in a terrible rush to get a question answered, read the messages for a few days to get an idea of what questions are being asked, and the knowledgeable people seem to be, and so on.

If you’re new (or inexperienced) at using mailing lists for technical support, and you have to post a question right away, you need to know a few rules of the road in order to make best use of the list.

+   1. Be nice.

Lists are generally frequented by folks like yourself—volunteers in most part, folks who want to help the community by contributing their time to help others, just as they have been helped in the past.

Unfortunately, two factors serve to cause people in need of help to ask for it in a hostile manner. First, they’re under pressure, under stress, and that often makes one irritable. Second, some folks seem to think that the lack of face-to-face contact gives them permission to ignore normal courtesy.

As a result, people will sometimes act impossibly when requesting help. This is a bad idea both because it’s wrong, and because you’re much less likely to get help if you act that way. Be courteous, even though you’re having a problem, and people will react in kind.

+   2. Show that you’ve done your homework first.

People who frequent mailing lists eventually start to see the same questions over and over, and end up getting tired of answering those questions. This results in those questions being ignored or answered in a terse (in other words, less than helpful) manner. The first time someone asks about a problem, someone will likely respond with a long, detailed response, including each step to take, and links to other sources. The 20th time that the question is asked, that person isn’t nearly as likely to provide the same detailed answer.

Show that you’ve done your research: You’ve read the help (if applicable), you’ve searched the mailing-list archives to see if the question has already been asked (see the next section for instructions and an example of how to do so), and you’ve done a general Web search on the topic.
