You can use the same settings that your Windows computer used for Primary DNS and Secondary DNS. If you have access to a Tertiary (that means “third”) DNS, go ahead and enter it as well.

The Hostname field is used to identify your machine—if you entered something here during installation, it’ll show up in the lower right corner of the login screen (see Figure 63 in Chapter 2). It’s also used in other contexts that aren’t important right now, but other people on your network may be able to see it at some point, so some modicum of good taste is probably a good idea.

The Hosts tab contains information about other computers on the network that can be recognized by this computer; those settings don’t apply if your machine isn’t on a network as is presumed here. If you were on a network and wanted to access other machines on the network by their machine names instead of their IP addresses, you’d use the Hosts tab to enter mappings of the names and IP addresses.

Once you’re done with the settings, select File | Save, restart the network services, and you’re done.

# # E-mail

Many e-mail clients are available on Linux. I’ll cover two popular ones—Evolution and Kmail—to get you started. If you want to explore more, look under Main Menu | Internet | More Internet Applications. Graphical e-mail clients such as Balsa, Mozilla Mail, and Sylpheed, and text-based e-mail clients such as elm, mutt, and pine are included in Fedora Core. (They may not be installed, though. You can check by looking under Main Menu | System Settings | Add/Remove Applications.)
