### Why Linux?

I started looking at Linux when the restrictions and problems I ran into on the Microsoft Windows platform gained critical mass for my personal taste. For me, open-source software, of which Linux is often held up as the poster child, embodies four important ideals: freedom, choice, security, and opportunity. Microsoft Windows kept throwing barriers in my way in each of these areas, while Linux provided solutions.

Let's examine each of those in a bit more detail.

#### Freedom

Open-source software like Linux represents freedom.

With proprietary software, such as (but not limited to) Microsoft Windows, your freedoms are severely curtailed, and are becoming more and more limited each day. You're allowed to use the software only in ways that the manufacturer designates, on platforms that the manufacturer designs acceptable, and for time spans or by numbers of users expressly allowed by the manufacturer. You are not allowed to make changes to it so that it works better for your own situation—indeed, you are not even allowed to look at how the software is put together in order to determine how to best make use of it.

If there are problems with the software, you are prohibited from fixing or otherwise resolving those problems in ways other than those permitted by the manufacturer. This includes fixing bugs or making other changes even after they stop supporting the software. You are at the mercy of the manufacturer or their designees for resolution of problems—in effect, they have a monopoly in terms of providing (or not providing) service and support. If you disobey any of those restrictions, you are subject to fines and jail terms.

Some licenses of proprietary software even restrict you from publishing benchmarks or other performance-related information—so you are unable to fairly evaluate one manufacturer's product against another.

Open-source software, on the other hand, allows and encourages the use and modification of software so that it is as useful as possible to the user. The only restriction is that modifications to open-source software that are made available to others must carry the same freedoms. Hardly a restriction!

Some argue that most people won't ever take advantage of these freedoms—that the average Joe user won't recompile his kernel or that most companies won't hire someone to fix a bug. Fine. That's not the point. The point is that they have the freedom to do so, should the need arise.

I like the freedom that open-source software represents.
