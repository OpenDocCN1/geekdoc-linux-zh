
## The original candidates

As of the start of this book, the distributions with the most mindshare came down to a half dozen: Red Hat Enterprise, SuSE Personal, SuSE Professional, Mandrake, Fedora Core, Debian, and Gentoo. I’m sure that many stalwarts would, upon reading this list, weigh in with their opinions, and in a more too polite a fashion: “What? You didn’t include my favorite distribution here? You’re an insensitive clod!”

> As of this writing, a new breed of distributions is getting popular. The so-called “Live CD” distributions, such as Knoppix, allow you to download a single ISO and try it out without having to install anything on your hard drive. You burn the ISO file to a CD, and then use that CD to boot and run the operating system. Nothing is written to the hard disk, so it’s a great tool for starting to learn about Linux if you don’t have a spare machine and don’t want to try setting up a dual Windows/Linux machine.

Nonetheless, the line has to be drawn somewhere, and because it’s my book, I get to do the drawing. In the couple of years I’ve been using Linux, these are the distros that keep coming up again and again.

Red Hat used to provide two versions of its products: the enterprise series and a free distribution called Red Hat Linux, which was also available as a boxed set with paid support. After the release of Red Hat Linux 9.0, the company discontinued production of that line, spinning off Red Hat Linux into a community-supported distribution called Fedora Core (or “FC”). Red Hat is now concentrating on the enterprise product line. Find it at www.redhat.com.

Many regard Fedora Core as “Red Hat Linux 10.0,” because it comes with an even more improved version of the installation GUI and clean desktop that Red Hat Linux was known for. FC is available for free download, but doesn’t include any options for paid support. See www.fedora.redhat.com.

SuSE is the German Linux distribution that was recently purchased by Novell. Highly popular in Europe, with an installation GUI and user interface nearly as clean as Red Hat Linux, it’s available in Personal and Professional boxed sets. Paid support is available as well. See www.suse.com.

Mandrake is the leading French distribution that weathered some tough financial times in 2003 but is back on its feet again. Many feel that Mandrake wins the ease-of-installation and ease-of-use contest, but my opinion is that at this level of the competition, personal preference begins to have a lot to do with your choice. You can order a boxed set or download from the Web site at www.mandrakelinux.com.

Debian is arguably the leading community-supported distribution that has no commercial ties at all (many of Fedora Core’s developers are Red Hat employees). You can download for free or buy CDs from a variety of distributors, usually for little more than the cost of the materials. Debian has had a reputation for being a “techie’s distribution” — it doesn’t have the same smooth installation wizard that Red Hat, Fedora Core, SuSE, and Mandrake do. It also tends to lag behind current versions of the various packages because the Debian maintainers want to make sure their distribution is rock solid and stable. As a result, I don’t recommend it for a first-time user. However, it’s very popular, and many users, once they get comfortable with Linux, choose to move to Debian. Find it at www.debian.org.

Gentoo is similar to Debian in that it is a community-supported distro without commercial ties, but with a strong fan base, and you can download or buy CDs. It has more sophisticated tools for installing and maintaining, but still requires more technical sophistication than the big four, and as a result, I can't recommend it for a first-time user. Again, once you're comfortable with some Linux basics, you may well want to consider it. Find out more at www.gentoo.org.

## Why I chose Fedora Core

My criteria for choosing a distribution were (1) ease of use, (2) free distribution, and (3) stability of the provider.

At the time I started this book, I felt that Fedora Core and Mandrake were neck-and-neck in terms of ease of use, but Mandrake was having some financial troubles. SuSE had just been purchased by Novell, but after a half dozen installs, I just didn't cotton to its user interface. And Debian and Gentoo, while having technical merits that make them outstanding candidates for advanced users, aren't ready for first-time Linux users.

That said, I think SuSE and Mandrake are worthy of your consideration. The concepts covered in this book are the same, but some of the implementation details differ. I've included occasional notes on both of these distributions as part of the electronic downloads. (See "How to Download the Files" in the front of the book for details.) I'll also post updates on the Hentzenwerke Web site.

Remember, you are a power user. You're going to love Linux. Linux rocks!

## Chapter 2: Installing Fedora Core

In mid-2003, Red Hat spun off its mainstream Red Hat distribution into a true open-source project called "Fedora Core," FC, as it's known, differs from Red Hat Linux in a couple of specific ways: First, you can only get it by downloading ISOs (or getting them from a friend), and second, it'll be updated on a regular basis, not strictly through open-source means. Some have taken these changes to mean that FC will become unstable or otherwise unworthy of attention, and thus been reluctant to give it a try. That's not really fair—most people (myself included) think that FC is on par with where "Red Hat 10" would have been at this time. Some people may not be convinced, so to give those doubting Thomases in the audience a preview of FC, I use a liberal number of screen shots from this chapter, as well as detailed supporting explanations to show you how to install Fedora Core from scratch for desktop usage.

I was initially among the skeptics when I heard about Red Hat's plans for Fedora Core. Part of the reason was that they didn't do a very good job at providing a timeline for support on the existing products—they in fact, I had been preparing to buy a number of subscriptions for a couple of recent RH 9.0 purchases; and if I had, those would have been wasted dollars and I would have been an unhappy camper. In other part of my skepticism was that they didn't do a very good job at explaining the differences between Fedora Core and their commercial products; it took me months to determine how an update service was going to work or, indeed, if there was even going to be one, past manually applying patches to individual packages.

As it turns out, I didn't have anything to fear, and generally, you shouldn't either. The most difficult problem for me was burning CDs—after that, the installation was a rather nicer version of the comfortable Red Hat installs I'd been used to with 8.0 and 9.0. I was not running in a couple of hours, most of which was spent watching thermometer bars race across the screen.
