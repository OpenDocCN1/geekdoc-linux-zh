
## Choice

Open-source software like Linux offers choices—choices that proprietary vendors do not want you to have. Proprietary vendors endeavor to lock you in to their platform; indeed, they have been doing that since the railroad robber barons of the mid-1800s tried to restrict usage of their tracks via the implementation of different gauges of tracks.

It is in the interest of a proprietary manufacturer to keep you tied to their platform, and thus restrict your choices to just those that they will allow. The end result is that you will end up paying higher prices than you would in a free market where there were many choices.

“Open source,” on the other hand, is all about choice. Don’t like a particular browser, office suite, or e-mail client? You’ve got many alternatives to choose from. And because there is competition between the providers of these choices, they need to compete on both features and quality.

A monopoly doesn’t have to concern itself with features or quality, because there’s no reason to.

Every time I upgraded my development tools in Windows, I was forced to upgrade the browser and a myriad of other components, whether I wanted to or not. And there were significant reasons for not wanting to just upgrade those components without a choice, as you’ll see in the next section. With open-source software, you don’t get a set of unwanted or needed components jammed down your throat against your will.

I like being able to choose which religion I practice, which political party to belong to, which way to educate my kids, and which clothes to wear and how long to wear my hair. I like the choice that open-source software offers, too.

## Security

I feel open-source platforms offer better security from dangers such as attacks and viruses for many reasons. These reasons include publicly available code, and a lack of conflicting political and economic agendas.

Here’s an example that illustrates some of these reasons. Over a year ago, Microsoft announced that a severe NT 4 vulnerability wouldn’t be fixed. They claimed that they—a company with tens of thousands of very smart people and $50 billion in the bank—didn’t have the resources to fix it.

With open-source software, the code is available for all to inspect. With proprietary software, no one is allowed to see the source code except a few select employees. How can Microsoft’s claim be verified? It can’t.

Microsoft has a vested interest in getting their customers to regularly upgrade their software. They don’t want their customers to use old versions of software. I feel it’s much more likely that they simply wanted to pressure customers who are still using NT 4 to upgrade to not fixing problems with NT 4. By doing so, Microsoft saves money in not producing a fix as well as generates money via upgrades.

A third reason is the simple matter of ego driving quality. I have more confidence in the quality of open-source code itself because it’s produced by people who *want* to do so, not because they *have* to in order to make a living, or because their bosses ordered them to. Who would you rather have perform your heart surgery—a fellow who needs to do the operation because he needs the money to pay off a loan shark by Friday, or a guy who is independently wealthy, and who went through med school because she wanted to become a great surgeon?

As of this writing in early 2004, back-to-back “critical” security holes have been discovered in Windows. One had been discovered late in the summer of 2003 and yet hadn't been acknowledged by Microsoft until February of 2004, when an independent security firm forced them to go public. How many more security holes are known by Microsoft and will never be announced because the media pressure hasn't been applied?

Open-source-software vulnerabilities, on the other hand, when they are discovered (and despite the availability of all of the source code, that doesn't happen very often), are always fixed within days, if not hours, of their discovery. It's a contest of egos to be the first one to provide the patch that fixes a problem. Of course, it's still up to the admins to take it from there. Historically, Linux administrators have been more technically savvy, but that tilt has been gradually changing as Linux becomes more mainstream.

I've been running Internet Information Server (IIS) and Apache Web servers side by side for a couple of years. When I look at the logs on the two boxes, I see the attacks based on Windows exploits outnumber Linux-oriented attacks by tens of thousands to one. Clearly crackers see more opportunity in attempting to exploit Windows security holes than Linux.

Combine this with the forced upgrades of ancillary components such as browsers, media players, database engines, and other packages when you upgrade your productivity or development tool suites. You're now forced onto yet another merry-go-round of patches, security fixes, and unknown vulnerabilities.

I feel more secure with open-source software.

## Opportunity

Open-source software offers opportunities everywhere you look. Linux continues to make inroads both in the back room as a server as well as on the desktop. Netrack's numbers show that Apache, an open-source Web server that runs on Windows and Linux, is continuing to take market share away from IIS. And penetration on the desktop—a place that the pundits claimed would never accept Linux—has now become “just a matter of time.”

Hardly a day goes by without another conversion to open-source software by a former Microsoft customer. The open-source books from Hentzenwerke Publishing have done orders of magnitude better than any of our books on proprietary software in the last couple of years. And we're just getting started.

As Linux continues to make inroads on the desktop, people will get used to using their primary workstation running mostly open-source software. But they're still going to need service and support, they're going to need to customize standard applications, and they're going to need custom applications for their business. The opportunities are only going to grow over the next few years.

I'm interested in growth markets, and I see the markets available to open-source software, such as Linux, to be exceedingly bright.

## Why not Linux? The top 10 myths

As with any new phenomenon, folklore soon arises that often has its roots in truth, but has gotten twisted way out of proportion. Here are 10 common misconceptions about Linux.

- Linux is only for servers.
- Linux is only for techie.
- Linux doesn’t have the software I need.
- Linux won’t run my Windows software.
- You have to use the command line.
- You have to compile everything.
- You don’t have to worry about security.
- I can’t use my existing hardware.
- There isn’t any technical support.
- It doesn’t cost anything.

Let’s examine each of these in more detail.

### Linux is only for servers.

Interestingly enough, Linux started its life as a desktop operating system, albeit one for sophisticated and knowledgeable programmers. As time passed, the core components became more mature, leaving undeveloped the interfaces that less knowledgeable users would require. Because Linux was modeled after an operating system that had its roots in the server room, it was capable of running servers right out of the box, while the desktop usage lagged.

But that was then, and this is now. Mass-market distributions come with all the bells and whistles you need to run a modern desktop. Installing and using popular distributions like Fedora Core, SuSE, and Mandrake on a desktop computer is just as easy, and in some cases, easier than Windows. And while the surface looks a bit different, once you get your hands into it, things work (most of the time) much like you’d expect them to.

### Linux is only for techies.

This myth used to be completely true. Until a few years ago, you had to install Linux using scripts and processes that bore no resemblance to those that a Windows 95 user was used to. To install software after installation, you had to wade through scripts, recompile binaries from source code, and deal with inscrutable files such as tarballs.

Eventually some Linux distributions came with GUI-based wizards, but even they required a considerable amount of technical knowledge. Indeed, as recently as two years ago, the first question you were asked when installing Red Hat Linux had to do with mapping mount points and partitions. Huh?

Once you were up and running, it wasn’t unusual to run into some operation or another that required you to enter arcane commands at a command prompt, edit random configuration files, or, in a fair number of cases, compile software in order to run it.

That situation has changed a great deal over the past couple of years. As mentioned in the previous section, popular distributions come with easy-to-use wizards and widgets that allow you to run Linux virtually all the time without worrying about a command prompt. Installing software is often (although not always) as easy as double-clicking on a file.

However, that doesn’t mean that Linux has been dumbed down. Rather, it’s simply been given an easy-to-use layer on top of the sophisticated internals that have always been there. If you’re interested in getting down and dirty with the guts of an operating system, the opportunity is still there.
