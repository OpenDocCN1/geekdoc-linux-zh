As of this writing in early 2004, back-to-back “critical” security holes have been discovered in Windows. One had been discovered late in the summer of 2003 and yet hadn't been acknowledged by Microsoft until February of 2004, when an independent security firm forced them to go public. How many more security holes are known by Microsoft and will never be announced because the media pressure hasn't been applied?

Open-source-software vulnerabilities, on the other hand, when they are discovered (and despite the availability of all of the source code, that doesn't happen very often), are always fixed within days, if not hours, of their discovery. It's a contest of egos to be the first one to provide the patch that fixes a problem. Of course, it's still up to the admins to take it from there. Historically, Linux administrators have been more technically savvy, but that tilt has been gradually changing as Linux becomes more mainstream.

I've been running Internet Information Server (IIS) and Apache Web servers side by side for a couple of years. When I look at the logs on the two boxes, I see the attacks based on Windows exploits outnumber Linux-oriented attacks by tens of thousands to one. Clearly crackers see more opportunity in attempting to exploit Windows security holes than Linux.

Combine this with the forced upgrades of ancillary components such as browsers, media players, database engines, and other packages when you upgrade your productivity or development tool suites. You're now forced onto yet another merry-go-round of patches, security fixes, and unknown vulnerabilities.

I feel more secure with open-source software.

# Opportunity

Open-source software offers opportunities everywhere you look. Linux continues to make inroads both in the back room as a server as well as on the desktop. Netrack's numbers show that Apache, an open-source Web server that runs on Windows and Linux, is continuing to take market share away from IIS. And penetration on the desktop—a place that the pundits claimed would never accept Linux—has now become “just a matter of time.”

Hardly a day goes by without another conversion to open-source software by a former Microsoft customer. The open-source books from Hentzenwerke Publishing have done orders of magnitude better than any of our books on proprietary software in the last couple of years. And we're just getting started.

As Linux continues to make inroads on the desktop, people will get used to using their primary workstation running mostly open-source software. But they're still going to need service and support, they're going to need to customize standard applications, and they're going to need custom applications for their business. The opportunities are only going to grow over the next few years.

I'm interested in growth markets, and I see the markets available to open-source software, such as Linux, to be exceedingly bright.
