# 1.3 Will my hardware work under Linux?

Not every piece of PC hardware is supported under Linux, but most are, particularly the more standard, older, and popular ones. This applies to SCSI adapters, CDROMs, writable and rewritable CDs (CD-R and CD-RW), video cards, mice, printers, modems, network cards, scanners, komega drives, etc.

The most notable exceptions are the so-called Winmods (=MS Windows models). Avoid these fire-like—maybe be $5 less expensive than full modems, which are crippled and there is a little chance they will ever work under a normal operating system (they may not work with the next version of MS Windows). External modems are never "MS Windows models" so if doubt, purchase an external modem (external modems are more expensive, but they don't drain your PC power supply, are easily portable between machines, look better and dont

Another area of potential problem is the video card. If you have a recent "cutting edge" 3D or uncommon, you may want to check its compatibility at http://www.Xfree86.org.

So the short answer is, in all likelihood your PC will run Linux with no problems. You don't invest much when trying Linux, so the easiest way to make sure is to attempt an installation on your hardware. There are Linux hardware compatibility lists at http://www.redhat.com/support/docs/hardware.html and http://metalab.unc/LDP/HOWTO/Hardware-HOWTO.html if you want to check for your newer or less popular hardware.

When purchasing new hardware, ask your supplier if the hardware is supported under Linux or consider a system with Linux pre-installed. Not too many suppliers offer you one yet (plan to), but you can get one from a smaller vendor.

If a piece of hardware yours is (apparently) not supported in your current Linux distribution, give up. Chances are: 1. It is supported, but you don't know how to set it. (Solution: stay around for Linux for a few weeks or days, when you get some understanding of how your system works, then you may be able to set it up.) 2. You have to go through a more complex setup to support the hardware (for example some cryptic command or a kernel re-compile, which is not as difficult as it seems). 3. An updated (different?) already supports it "out-of-box" (you can usually order it for US$19). 4. There is always an upgrade on the Internet, you will find it, download it, and figure out how to install it. 4. The upgrade will be available next week—Linux development goes really fast!

# 1.4 How do I download Linux?

Do yourself a favor and do not download Linux, get an installation instead. Linux can be downloaded completely from the Internet, but it is a large and sophisticated operating system. The download will take hours or days of download time and you will probably encounter problems trying to stitch the downloaded pieces together.
