2. Why switch from Windows to Linux?

For reasons of data privacy and security. That’s a straightforward summary.

Important tip: Never act recklessly or hastily. Even if the decision to switch from Windows to Linux is already made! Do not start »killing Windows« until you have worked through Chapter 18!

I have been using Windows since version Windows 3.1. Back then, when computers were still exotic, it was all extremely interesting and absolutely exciting. But over the years, Microsoft evolved into a greedy data octopus. Today, we have reached a point where data privacy plays a central role in choosing an operating system. Therefore, in my new book “Linux instead of Windows,” I am informing you about the disadvantages of Windows and the opposing advantages of Linux. It is clear that both systems have pros and cons—but if you say, “The data belongs to me,” one candidate stands out negatively.

Windows, developed by Microsoft and currently the most widely used end-user operating system worldwide, offers numerous features and is installed on countless computers. However, despite its popularity and versatility, there are significant privacy concerns that users should consider. In my book, the main disadvantages of Windows concerning privacy are detailed, including mandatory accounts, the integration of OneDrive, susceptibility to viruses, and update issues.

A central disadvantage of Windows in terms of privacy is the so-called mandatory account. Since Windows 8, Microsoft has increasingly pushed for users to sign in with a Microsoft account to fully utilize the operating system’s potential. This requirement to create an account raises significant privacy concerns, as Microsoft gains access to a multitude of personal data. This includes emails, contacts, calendar data, and even settings and preferences synchronized across various devices. This central data storage carries the risk that sensitive information may be collected and possibly used without the users‹ knowledge or consent. Microsoft cannot credibly claim that this sensitive data is not used for other services or for the development of other services.

Another significant criticism is the integration of OneDrive, Microsoft’s cloud storage service, which is often activated without explicit user consent. Files and documents are automatically uploaded to the cloud, which not only jeopardizes privacy but also limits, often completely removes, users‹ control over their own data. Particularly in environments where sensitive or confidential information is processed, this automatic synchronization can pose a significant security risk. Moreover, we now know that all data uploaded to cloud systems is read and analyzed. If there is data that gets flagged by Microsoft’s filters, accounts can be suspended, and data can be irretrievably lost.

Additionally, Windows is known for its susceptibility to viruses. Due to its widespread use, the operating system is a prime target for malware and hacker attacks. These threats can lead to the theft or compromise of personal and confidential data. Despite continuous security updates and integrated protection mechanisms, the risk remains, especially for users who do not regularly install security updates or use additional protection software.

Another problem is the frequent and sometimes problematic updates of Windows. Microsoft regularly performs updates to close security gaps and improve the performance of the operating system. However, these updates often have unexpected side effects that can affect the stability of the system. Worse still, some updates are carried out without prior notice or user consent, which can lead to unexpected restarts and data loss. This update issue not only represents an inconvenience but can also endanger the security and privacy of users if unplanned system changes lead to new security vulnerabilities.

It becomes evident daily that Windows, despite its numerous advantages and widespread use, has significant drawbacks in terms of privacy. The mandatory account requirement, the enforced integration of OneDrive, susceptibility to viruses and malware, and problematic updates pose serious risks to user privacy and security. It is therefore important that users are aware of these risks and take appropriate measures to protect their data. This includes using additional security software, carefully managing cloud services, and regularly reviewing and adjusting the operating system’s privacy settings. All this leads to a whole chain of further disadvantages: constant, even daily vigilance and checking, paid additional software, and the daily risk of complete system failure. And this is no mere assertion. Entire systems can be rendered inoperative overnight by faulty updates. In my opinion, Microsoft has lost touch with the core and is no longer aware of its position in the world. Microsoft, in my view, represents a significant security problem for the entire world and should not be allowed to operate as a profit-oriented company without government oversight.

4. Do I want to disclose my data?

Given all the negative aspects that Windows, or rather the company Microsoft behind Windows, brings with it, everyone should ask themselves: »Do I want this?«

I will now pose many »Do I want ...« questions to simply show you which questions you should really ask yourself.

Do I want to entrust Microsoft with all my data? Do I want Microsoft to dictate which programs or apps I can use? Do I want Microsoft to make software changes on my PC or laptop without asking me? Do I want to see advertisements in the Start menu? Do I want to see advertisements in folders and settings? Do I want to be patronized by Microsoft?

Do I want Microsoft to install AI systems on my PC? Do I want these AI systems to photograph my screen around the clock? Do I want Microsoft to provide me with a backup system that solely aims to move my data to the cloud? Do I want my files, photos, and videos to be transferred to the cloud and potentially read, sifted through, and used to train AI systems by Microsoft and possibly third parties? Do I want to continue accepting the risk of problematic and poorly tested updates?

Do I want to place my email traffic, documents, and files in a cloud that carries the risk of failure? Do I want Microsoft to continue working on moving my entire PC to the cloud? Do I want to give up all my data?

Then there are a few other questions you should ask yourself: Can I live with not having access to my data because...

... the internet is not working again?

... the Microsoft cloud is crashing again?

... the internet is simply too slow?

... Microsoft dislikes something about my data?

... Microsoft gets hacked again?

... my Microsoft account is hacked?

Do I want to keep paying for software and paying to use Microsoft’s services?

If you can answer all these questions with »Yes,« then just keep Windows. However, if you have concerns and feel that it might not be so good to move all your data to the cloud and willingly entrust Microsoft and others with your data, then let’s explore what options are currently available.

Since Windows is known to collect more and more user information, establish mandatory accounts, and increasingly move its systems to the cloud, the question is: Do you really want that? Do you want all your personal data to be stored somewhere on the internet on servers accessible to Microsoft and potentially third parties, such as hackers? This development raises not only ethical but also security concerns that are worth examining thoroughly.

The digital world we live in today is characterized by constant connectivity and the drive to move more and more data to the cloud. This centralized storage may seem convenient at first glance—you can access your files from anywhere, your settings are synchronized, and your software is always up to date. But all these advantages come at a high price: control over your own data. With every click, with every use of a cloud-based service, you give up a piece of this control and hand it over to a company whose business model does not necessarily align with your interests.

Even more concerning is the shift of systems and data to the cloud. Your personal data—whether documents, photos, emails, or even your entire computer environment—is stored on servers you cannot see or control. These servers are often located in data centers distributed across the globe and are subject to the local laws of the respective countries. In many cases, this means that your data is subject to the legal regulations and enforcement powers of other states, which can lead to a loss of privacy and increased risk.

Another issue is security. Servers that store large amounts of data are an attractive target for hackers. Even though companies like Microsoft invest significant resources in securing their systems, there is no guarantee that your data in the cloud is safe. Data breaches, ransomware attacks, and other cyber threats are pervasive, and even the largest and most secure companies are not immune to such attacks. Should an attack be successful, your data could be stolen, deleted, or misused—potentially with devastating consequences for your personal and professional security.

Furthermore, you should consider what happens to your data once it is stored in the cloud. There is no complete transparency about how your data is used. While most users assume their data is simply stored, there are indications that some companies also use this data for other purposes. A particularly controversial issue is the use of data for training artificial intelligence (AI). AI systems require enormous amounts of data to learn and improve, and the data you upload to the cloud could theoretically be used to train such systems. While this might improve services, it raises serious questions about privacy and control over your personal information.

All these developments lead to a central question: Do you really want this? Do you really want a company like Microsoft—or any other company—to have such deep insight into your personal and digital life? Do you really want your data to be stored on servers you cannot directly access and whose security you cannot monitor yourself? The risks associated with storing your data in the cloud are significant, and it is important that you understand these risks before opting for such a path.

The decision of whether to continue using Windows and accept all these concerns or to opt for an alternative like Linux is up to you. However, it is a decision that should be well thought out. Linux offers you the opportunity to regain control over your data and digital environment. It gives you the freedom to protect your privacy and configure your systems the way you want—without the constant pressure to disclose your personal information. In a world increasingly dominated by the power of a few large companies, this freedom is invaluable.

Since you have already purchased the book, I assume that the decision has basically been made. However, you might not yet know exactly how to accomplish a clean transition. In this book, I offer you a direct transition from Windows to Linux Mint. Why? I will answer that in the next chapter.

5. Internet Problems

In Germany, people frequently experience the shortcomings of the internet infrastructure up close. Whether it’s slow speeds, unstable connections, or even large-scale outages, internet problems are a constant annoyance. Regions that have been left behind in broadband expansion often struggle with particularly poor connections. But even in urban areas, where coverage should theoretically be better, there are often weaknesses. In a digitalized world where we increasingly rely on the internet, this is not just an inconvenience but can have serious consequences.

These problems become even more severe when you consider that natural disasters such as flooding, storms, or heavy snowfall can completely disable the network. Such events are not just temporary disruptions; they can make internet access impossible for days or even weeks. Recent floods in various parts of Germany have shown how quickly the infrastructure can collapse. Roads were flooded, power lines destroyed, and of course, the internet was affected. In such situations, people are left on their own, without access to important online services that play a central role in their daily lives.

When your data is stored in the cloud and the network fails, whether due to infrastructural weaknesses or natural disasters, you lose access to that data. A simple example: It’s enough to sit in a subway in Berlin to experience the everyday issues that come with reliance on cloud-based services. Underground, network coverage is often patchy or non-existent. Suddenly, you cannot access your documents, photos, or emails because they are on a remote server that is only reachable via the internet. This may seem like a minor annoyance at such moments, but the extent of this problem becomes apparent when extending the dependency on cloud services to the entire operating system.

With the increasing adoption of cloud-based operating systems like Windows 365, this issue becomes even more urgent. If the entire operating system is in the cloud and the internet fails, you are completely incapacitated. Your computer becomes an expensive but useless piece of electronics that serves no function without a connection to the cloud. For private users, this may already be annoying enough, but for businesses, it can be existentially threatening. Companies that rely on constant availability of their systems might, in the worst case, have to cease operations entirely if they lose access to their IT systems.

The thought of having your business crippled because an internet connection is interrupted is unsettling. Just look at the global outages of major cloud providers, which we are almost daily affected by, even if they are only briefly mentioned in the news. Such outages can be caused by a variety of factors – technical glitches, cyberattacks, or simply human error. Every time such an outage occurs, millions of users are affected, suddenly losing access to their data and services. This affects not only large companies but also small and medium-sized businesses that rely on cloud services to carry out their daily work.

The risks associated with this increasing dependence on the cloud are substantial. When your data and applications are exclusively stored in the cloud, you relinquish control over your digital life to third parties. You are dependent on the internet being available at all times and everywhere – a requirement that is unfortunately not met in many parts of Germany. Furthermore, your work security depends on the stability and security of the servers where your data is stored. An outage or disruption can have far-reaching consequences beyond just the loss of productivity.

Considering all these factors, it becomes clear that reliance on cloud-based solutions like Windows 365 carries serious risks. These risks should not be dismissed lightly. While the cloud certainly offers many advantages, it is important not to ignore the associated challenges and potential dangers. It’s about finding a balance between convenience and control and being aware that choosing a cloud-based infrastructure can have far-reaching consequences.

In this context, Linux offers an attractive alternative. With Linux, you retain control over your data and your operating system. You are not forced to rely on a permanent internet connection, and your data remains on your own device, where you can manage and protect it directly. Even during times when the internet is down, you can continue to work productively and access your data because it is stored locally. This independence is particularly valuable in a world where internet availability is far from guaranteed.

Ultimately, the question is: Do you want to rely on an infrastructure that is beyond your control in an increasingly insecure digital world? Or do you want to take responsibility for your data and work into your own hands and enjoy the freedom that an operating system like Linux offers? The answer to this question could be crucial in shaping your digital future.

6. Risks of the Cloud

The increasing shift of data to the cloud is often portrayed as convenient, flexible, and modern. Businesses and individuals value the advantages that cloud-based services offer: from simple collaboration across locations to automatic backup of files and systems. However, these conveniences come with risks. One often-overlooked issue is what happens to your data if a cloud provider goes bankrupt, gets hacked, or is acquired by another company—scenarios that have already occurred and can potentially have catastrophic consequences.

Imagine you have entrusted your entire digital life to a cloud provider. All your documents, photos, business data, and perhaps even your computer’s operating system are stored in the cloud. You rely on these data being secure and always accessible. But what happens if the provider you have entrusted with this data suddenly encounters financial difficulties and has to declare bankruptcy? In the past, we have seen cloud providers suddenly shut down their services, whether due to financial problems or a drastic security incident. If this happens, access to your data could be lost overnight. In the worst case, your data could even be irretrievably deleted because there is no plan to recover it if the provider ceases operations.

Another increasingly likely scenario is a large-scale hacker attack on a cloud provider. Such attacks can have devastating consequences, especially if they result in data being encrypted and held hostage. A prominent example of this is the attack on Code Spaces in 2014. Hackers gained access to the company’s Amazon Web Services (AWS) accounts and encrypted all data. After the company was unable to pay the ransom demanded, the data was irretrievably deleted, eventually leading to Code Spaces‹ bankruptcy. Customers who had stored their data in this cloud lost everything—not just their data but also their trust in the security of cloud services. Such cases demonstrate that even the most reputable cloud providers are not immune to cyberattacks and that the consequences for users can be severe.

But even if a provider does not go bankrupt or get hacked, there is another risk: company acquisitions. In the fast-paced tech industry, it is common for one company to acquire another. When this happens, data can be transferred from one provider to another, often across national borders and without comprehensive user notification. This can lead to your data suddenly being stored in a country with completely different data protection laws, where it may be less protected than before. For example, you might imagine a European cloud provider being acquired by an American company. Suddenly, your data could be subject to the less stringent US data protection laws, and without your knowledge or consent.

Even more concerning is the fact that these transfers often occur in an opaque manner. You might never find out that your data has been moved from one provider to another until it’s too late. You might also have no way to object to this transfer or secure your data before it is moved. This raises serious questions about the control and security of your personal and business information. Can you be sure that your data in a foreign country is as well protected as it is in your home country? Can you be sure that the new provider maintains the same security standards as the original one? This uncertainty is a significant risk that is often underestimated.

Let’s compare this situation with something everyday: would you simply leave your wallet at a busy train station and expect it to remain safe? Probably not. You would be concerned that someone might find it and take it without you ever knowing. Similarly, with your data in the cloud, when you entrust your data to a third party, you lose direct control over who can access it, when, and how. You might not know who in which country is storing, processing, or even reselling your data. And just like the wallet at the train station, your data could end up in the hands of someone who misuses it without you noticing.

The idea that your sensitive information—whether personal documents, financial data, or business-critical information—is stored in the cloud without knowing exactly who can access it or where it is stored should prompt you to think critically. The convenience and ease that cloud services offer should not overshadow the significant security and control risks they entail. These risks become even greater when you find yourself in a situation where the provider goes bankrupt, gets hacked, or transitions to another country with different laws.

So, if you are wondering whether it is truly safe to store your data in the cloud, you should also consider how little control you actually have over this data. An alternative to this insecure model is to regain control over your own data—such as by using a local operating system like Linux, where your data remains on your own hardware. Here, you have full control over who can access your data, when, and how, and you are not subject to the uncertainties of an unstable or insecure cloud provider.

In a world where data is becoming increasingly valuable, you should carefully consider whom you entrust with your digital treasures. Losing control over your own data can have far-reaching consequences that go beyond the loss of a wallet. It concerns security, confidentiality, and ultimately sovereignty over your own information. By storing your data locally and relying on solutions that give you control, you can ensure that you can sleep peacefully in an increasingly connected world—knowing that your digital assets are safe and protected.

A particularly critical look should be directed at the largest cloud providers worldwide: Google, Amazon, Facebook, and Microsoft. These tech giants are headquartered in the US, a country with much less stringent data protection laws compared to the European Union. This is not an insignificant difference but a factor that can have massive implications for how your personal and business data is handled. The fact that these companies have almost free rein in how they use your data is often underestimated—and yet, this is exactly what happens every day.

These American companies operate in a legal environment that grants them extensive freedom in the use, analysis, and exploitation of user data. While data protection laws like the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the EU attempt to protect citizens‹ privacy, regulations in the US are much looser. This allows large tech corporations to collect and process data as they see fit. And they take full advantage of this.

Despite these lax data protection practices, Google, Amazon, Facebook, and Microsoft enjoy the trust of millions, if not billions, of people worldwide. These companies have managed to position themselves as indispensable service providers—whether through search engines, social networks, e-commerce platforms, or cloud-based software solutions. Many people use their services daily, often without being fully aware of how deeply these corporations intrude into their personal and professional data. They offer seemingly free or inexpensive services, but the true cost users pay is the loss of control over their data.

A particularly problematic aspect is the extensive data analysis that these companies conduct. The capabilities available to them are far more advanced than anything even experienced tech specialists in the EU could dream of. Every email, every document, every image uploaded to the cloud is filtered, analyzed, and cataloged. This analysis is done not only to improve the service or display personalized ads but also to create comprehensive user profiles that can be used for various purposes—from market research to political influence.

The problem is that these processes are neither transparently traceable nor effectively preventable from within the EU. While the GDPR attempts to regulate and control the flow of data to the US, practice shows that it is almost impossible for users to know exactly what happens to their data once it leaves Europe’s borders. Furthermore, the terms and privacy policies of these companies often span hundreds of pages and are written in language that is difficult even for experts to understand. These documents are designed to be so complicated and comprehensive that most users simply accept them without reading them. By doing so, they consent to practices they do not even remotely understand.

It is only a matter of time before many people and businesses will regret moving all their data to the cloud. For once they have given up control over their data, they are subject to the decisions and practices of these large corporations. If legal frameworks change or these companies decide to alter their business models, users may find that their data has been used in ways they never intended.

Such a day could be triggered by a variety of factors: a major data misuse scandal, a sudden change in terms of service, acquisition by another company that places even less emphasis on data protection, or even a political event that restricts or controls access to this data. In a world where data is the new oil, entrusting this valuable asset to a company operating in a country whose laws do not sufficiently protect this data is risky.

So the question arises whether it is really wise to place so much trust in companies that operate largely outside the EU’s influence and its strict data protection laws. Would you simply leave your wallet lying around and trust that no one will touch it? Probably not. Yet that is exactly what many do when they entrust their data to these cloud services without knowing exactly who can access it, when, and how.

It is therefore crucial to understand the risks and consider alternative solutions. Operating systems and services that allow you to control your own data, such as Linux, offer a real alternative. They provide you with the opportunity to store your data locally and protect it on your own terms, without having to rely on the opaque and often non-transparent practices of large tech corporations.

The digital world is becoming increasingly complex, and it is getting harder to keep track of who does what with our data. But one thing is clear: retaining control over our own data is crucial for our digital sovereignty and privacy. It is time to take this aspect seriously and be aware of what we are giving up when we place our data in the hands of these large corporations. Because the price we pay for it could be higher than we ever imagined.

7. Energy Consumption of the Cloud

While your computer, laptop, or even a server consumes only a few hundred watts or several kilowatt-hours if it’s a larger server, it is hard to imagine how much energy is used by cloud services. Do you have any idea how much energy is needed to upload just 1 GB of data to the cloud? Well, take a look:

The energy consumption of cloud services is enormous and often underestimated. Uploading just 1 GB of data to the cloud requires a considerable amount of energy. This is not only due to the transmission itself but also to the storage and the many associated processes in the massive data centers that operate these cloud services. These data centers consist of thousands of servers running around the clock, which need to be cooled and require a stable power supply. The massive server farms not only consume vast amounts of energy but also contribute significantly to CO₂ emissions.

Data centers, especially those operated by major providers such as Google, Amazon, and Microsoft, require a massive infrastructure to handle the volume of data flowing through the internet daily. These centers are often as large as several football fields and, in many cases, consume more electricity than entire cities. The servers themselves generate heat that must be dissipated by powerful cooling systems, which, in turn, consume even more energy.

A Greenpeace report from 2017 showed that the data centers of the largest cloud providers already consumed more energy than many small countries at that time. Although these companies are attempting to reduce their emissions and switch to renewable energy, the energy demand is enormous and continues to grow.

It becomes particularly critical when considering that many data centers are located in countries where most of the electricity still comes from fossil fuels. 14. What is Linux

Imagine a world where you have control over your operating system without constantly feeling monitored or exploited. A world where freedom, privacy, and adaptability are at the forefront. The history of Linux begins with a vision of freedom and openness. In 1991, when the internet was still in its infancy and Windows was just beginning its rise, a young Finn named Linus Torvalds started working. Torvalds was a computer science student at the University of Helsinki, and he wanted to create an operating system based on the principles of openness and collaboration. His goal was to develop a free, non-commercial operating system that was accessible to everyone and not subject to the constraints of traditional software models.

Torvalds began developing a new kernel, which is the core component of an operating system. This kernel, which he named »Linux,« was intended to provide an alternative to the commercially popular operating systems like Windows and Unix at the time. The name »Linux« comes from its creator, Linus Torvalds, and the fact that it is a Unix-like system. The kernel was released under the GNU General Public License, which allowed anyone to use, modify, and distribute the code.

What started as a small project quickly grew into a global movement. Linux attracted the attention of developers and technicians who wanted to use the system as a basis for their own projects. The open-source philosophy enabled collaborative development, with thousands of programmers worldwide contributing to the project. This community of developers and users played a crucial role in the growth and stability of Linux.

In the following years, more and more Linux distributions were created—custom versions tailored to different needs and preferences. Distributions such as Ubuntu, Fedora, and Debian became popular and helped make Linux more accessible to the general public. For Timeshift to function properly and utilize all its features, the snapshots must be stored on a Linux-compatible file system. This is due to the specific requirements related to managing file and ownership permissions.

File systems such as NTFS or FAT, primarily used by Windows, are not fully compatible with Linux when it comes to storing complex information needed on a Linux system. While these file systems support basic functions like storing files and folders, they cannot store advanced file permissions and ownership rights essential for Linux.

A central aspect of Linux is its permission and ownership system, which ensures that certain files can only be modified by specific users or system services. Each file and folder on a Linux system has assigned owner and group rights, as well as detailed permissions for reading, writing, and executing. These rights are crucial not only for security but also for the correct functioning of many programs and system processes.

Therefore, Timeshift places great importance on accurately saving these rights in the snapshots as they exist on the source system. By storing the snapshots on a file system like ext4, which supports the same rights and ownership structures, Timeshift can precisely restore this information. This is critical to ensure that after a restoration, the system functions exactly as it did before.

File systems like NTFS and FAT do not support these Linux-specific permissions. They only store rudimentary information about files and cannot manage complex permission structures. If you were to attempt to store Timeshift snapshots on an NTFS or FAT file system, these important permission details could be lost. This could lead to the restored system not functioning properly or even security issues.

Thus, Timeshift needs to store its snapshots on a file system that supports the same functions as the source file system. In most cases, this will be the same file system on which Linux is installed—such as ext4.

28.1 File System

The shell is open, and you can use the bolded commands. To navigate the file system in Linux Mint, use the following commands:

cd (Change Directory): With this command, you change the current directory. For example: cd /home/username/Documents takes you to the »Documents« directory within your home directory.

cd .. goes one directory backward.

cd /home/username/Doc and Tab completes the word »Documents« if the folder »Documents« is present in /home/.

ls (List): This command displays the contents of the current directory. Use ls -l for more detailed information or ls -a to show hidden files.

pwd (Print Working Directory): This command shows the full path of the current directory.

28.2 Managing Files, Directories

cp (Copy): This command allows you to copy files and directories. For example: cp file.txt /home/username/targetfolder/ copies the file »file.txt« to the target folder.

mv (Move): This command moves or renames files and directories. For example: mv oldFile.txt newFile.txt renames the file »oldFile.txt« to »newFile.txt«.

rm (Remove): This command deletes files or directories. Be careful, as deleted files are not moved to the trash. Use rm -r for removing directories including their contents.

mkdir (Make Directory): Creates a new directory. For example: mkdir newFolder creates a directory named »newFolder«.

rmdir (Remove Directory): Deletes an empty directory. For example: rmdir emptyFolder removes the empty directory »emptyFolder«.

cat (Concatenate): Displays the contents of a file. For example: cat file.txt outputs the entire content of the file »file.txt«.

less: Displays the contents of a file page by page, allowing you to scroll through the content. For example: less file.txt lets you scroll through the contents of the file »file.txt«.

head: Shows the first lines of a file. For example: head -n 10 file.txt displays the first 10 lines of »file.txt«.

tail: Shows the last lines of a file. For example: tail -n 10 file.txt displays the last 10 lines of »file.txt«.

find: Searches for files and directories in the file system based on criteria. For example: find /home/username -name »*.txt« searches for all .txt files in the directory »/home/username«.

grep: Searches files for specific text patterns. For example: grep »searchTerm« file.txt searches for the »searchTerm« in the file »file.txt«.

28.3 File Permissions, File Properties

chmod (Change Mode): Changes the permissions of a file or directory. Example: chmod 755 file.txt sets the permissions for »file.txt« to »rwxr-xr-x«.

chown (Change Owner): Changes the owner and group of a file or directory. Example: chown username

file.txt changes the owner of the file »file.txt« to »username« and the group to »group«.

stat: Displays detailed information about a file or directory, including permissions, size, and timestamps. Example: stat file.txt outputs detailed information about the file »file.txt«.

28.4 Filesystem Check, Filesystem Repair

fsck (File System Consistency Check): Checks and repairs filesystem errors. Example: sudo fsck /dev/sda1 checks the filesystem on the partition »/dev/sda1«.

e2fsck (Ext2/3/4 Filesystem Check): This command is specific for checking and repairing Ext2, Ext3, and Ext4 filesystems. Example: sudo e2fsck -f /dev/sda1. This command forces a filesystem check on the partition »/dev/sda1«.

tune2fs (Tune Ext2/3/4 Filesystem): Although tune2fs is mainly used to adjust filesystem parameters, you can also use it to schedule certain checks and maintenance of the filesystem. Example: sudo tune2fs -c 30 /dev/sda1. Sets the filesystem on »/dev/sda1« to be checked after 30 mountings.

badblocks (Check Bad Blocks): This command checks a hard drive or partition for bad blocks. It can help identify hardware issues that may damage the filesystem. Example: sudo badblocks -v /dev/sda1. Checks the partition »/dev/sda1« for bad blocks and displays the progress in detail.

smartctl (S.M.A.R.T. Monitoring Tools): This tool is used to monitor and check the status of hard drives and SSDs. It uses S.M.A.R.T. technology to detect potential problems before they occur. Example: sudo smartctl -a /dev/sda. Displays comprehensive information and the status of the hard drive »/dev/sda«.

xfs_repair (XFS Filesystem Repair): If you are using an XFS filesystem, this command is used for checking and repairing it. XFS is a filesystem often used on large file servers. Example: sudo xfs_repair /dev/sda1. Checks and repairs the XFS filesystem on the partition »/dev/sda1«.

btrfs check (Btrfs Filesystem Check): For the Btrfs filesystem, the btrfs check command is used to verify and repair filesystem integrity. Example: sudo btrfs check /dev/sda1. Performs a check of the Btrfs filesystem on the partition »/dev/sda1«.

mount -o remount,ro and umount: Before running some of the above commands, particularly those involving direct filesystem repair, you should remount the filesystem in read-only mode or unmount it to avoid potential data loss. Example: sudo mount -o remount,ro /dev/sda1. Remounts the filesystem on »/dev/sda1« in read-only mode. sudo umount /dev/sda1. Unmounts the partition »/dev/sda1« so it can be safely checked.

But be careful! Always exercise caution when tinkering with the core of your system. It’s not just a simple task to check or repair a filesystem without potential consequences. As soon as you start working with commands like fsck, e2fsck, or even umount, you are making deeper changes to the system. And if you don’t proceed carefully, you could easily get burned – meaning, you could lose data or even disable your system.

Especially important: If you want to unmount a filesystem to check or repair it, you must ensure that no critical processes are running on it. Imagine unmounting a partition where your database server, like MySQL, is running. Boom, everything crashes, and suddenly you’re left wondering why your entire system isn’t working anymore. You need to ensure that you either properly shut down the affected service beforehand or remount the filesystem in read-only mode before doing anything with it.

A good example of this is the umount command. You use it to safely remove partitions from the system so they can be checked or repaired. But be warned: If you accidentally unmount the main drive where critical system services or programs are running, it could have severe consequences. That’s why you should always double-check which partition you’re working on and ensure no active applications are accessing it.

If you want to check or repair the filesystem, the fsck command can help. This command checks and repairs inconsistent filesystems. But again, caution is needed. Only run this command on an unmounted or read-only mounted filesystem. Never work with a running, writable filesystem. Imagine writing something to the hard drive during the process – chaos is bound to ensue. An example would be the command sudo fsck /dev/sda1, which ensures that the partition »/dev/sda1« is checked for errors.

Another important tool is badblocks. This command checks whether your hard drive has defective blocks – areas that can no longer be properly written or read. It’s a good test to detect hardware problems early. But again, be careful. If you have a large hard drive, this command can run for a long time. So don’t use it on a disk that is actively being used, as it could lead to data loss.

And what about S.M.A.R.T.? This built-in self-test, which you can start with the smartctl command, helps you monitor the health of your hard drives. Again, this test is safer than others, but if your drive already has bad blocks, excessive use could worsen the situation. Always have backups ready and plan to replace the hard drive if problems arise.

The tune2fs tool can also be useful for automating filesystem maintenance. With it, you can specify after how many system boots the filesystem is automatically checked. But again, be careful not to make settings that could cause issues during runtime.

For specialized filesystems like XFS, there is the xfs_repair command. XFS is a filesystem specifically designed for large data volumes and high performance. Use this command only if you are certain that the XFS filesystem is damaged, and don’t forget: backup first, check afterward.

Last but not least: For Btrfs filesystems, there is the btrfs check command. Btrfs is a modern filesystem that offers many cool features like snapshots and compression. Again, the same rule applies: Only perform checks on an unused filesystem, and be very careful during repairs – one wrong step can easily lead to data loss.

Remember: As powerful as the shell is, it can be just as dangerous if you’re not careful. Always work thoughtfully and only execute commands that you are absolutely certain about. It’s better to double-check something than to mistype a command and ruin the system.

Even if you enter a command and don’t immediately wreck the system, it’s quite possible that your computer will become unusable for a while. The system isn’t broken, but it’s crippled as long as the operation is running.

Especially if you run these commands on a system that is in use, it can render the computer nearly unusable because the hard drive is fully occupied with the check.

32. History of Windows

The history of Windows begins in 1985 with the introduction of Windows 1.0. At that time, Microsoft was still a relatively small company trying to establish itself in a market dominated by IBM.

Windows 1.0 was a graphical user interface (GUI) built on MS-DOS. It was a radical shift from the text-based input of the DOS era to a visual representation of programs and files. Despite its innovative approach, Windows 1.0 was not very successful and was often described as buggy and limited.

Windows 2.0, released in 1987, brought several improvements, including support for more memory and better management of windows on the screen. This update began to lay the groundwork for future developments but had not yet achieved a major breakthrough.

The real breakthrough came with Windows 3.0, released in 1990. Windows 3.0 was a significant advancement and offered a much more stable and powerful platform than its predecessors. The introduction of the »Program Manager« and »File Manager« allowed for a more user-friendly interaction with the system. Windows 3.1, released in 1992, further improved usability and added the first true TrueType fonts, revolutionizing print quality. This version was a great success and solidified Windows‹ position in the market.

With Windows 95, released in 1995, Microsoft entered a new era of user-friendliness and functionality. Windows 95 introduced the Start menu, a feature that would become one of the system’s trademarks. The integration of Plug-and-Play technology greatly simplified hardware installation. The concepts of the »Task Manager« and the »Taskbar« were also introduced. Windows 95 was a commercial triumph and set new standards for future operating systems.

Windows 98, released in 1998, brought important improvements such as better internet integration and enhanced hardware support. It was the first Windows version to offer native support for USB devices, although this support was not yet fully developed. Windows ME (Millennium Edition), which followed in 2000, aimed to further improve multimedia capabilities and user-friendliness, though it was criticized for its stability issues.

Windows 2000, released in 2000, was primarily aimed at businesses and professional use. It offered a stable platform with improved network security and management tools.

But the real game-changer was Windows XP, released in 2001. Windows XP combined the stability of Windows 2000 with a user-friendly interface and an enhanced system core. The introduction of the »Luna« design provided a visual refresh. Windows XP became extremely popular and remained the preferred operating system for both private users and businesses for many years.

Windows Vista, released in 2007, set a new standard in terms of security and visual design but also introduced significant hardware requirements that led to performance issues for many users.

Windows 7, released in 2009, significantly improved usability and performance. It addressed many of the problems that plagued Vista and quickly established itself as a popular choice among users.

Windows 8, released in 2012, marked a radical design shift that focused on touchscreen interactions and the »Metro« interface. The Start menu was replaced by the Start screen, leading to mixed reactions. Windows 8.1, released in 2013, partially brought back the classic Start menu and offered many improvements in usability and system performance.

Windows 10, released in 2015, brought back many of the best elements from previous versions and introduced a regular update strategy known as »Windows-as-a-Service.« Windows 10 enabled a seamless experience across different devices and integrated virtual desktops, the Cortana voice assistant, and the Edge browser.

Windows 11, released in 2021, introduced a comprehensive visual overhaul and brought new features such as widgets, a centered Start menu, and improved multitasking. It set new standards for design and user experience and aimed to provide a more modern and powerful operating system.

The history of Windows is a chronological journey from a rudimentary operating system to a comprehensive platform that plays a central role in the modern world. From early experiments with graphical user interfaces to advanced features and continuous updates, Windows shows an impressive evolution. Each update and new version has contributed to making Windows the powerful tool it is today. It remains exciting to watch how the operating system will continue to evolve and what new innovations the future will bring.

33. History of Linux

The history of Linux begins in 1991 when Linus Torvalds, a 21-year-old student at the University of Helsinki, released the first version of his own operating system. Frustrated by the high costs and limited flexibility of the then-popular MINIX operating system, which was developed for educational purposes, Linus aimed to create a free and open operating system aligned with the principles of open-source software development.

On August 25, 1991, Torvalds announced the initial version of the Linux kernel in an email to a small group of Usenet users. This first version was quite rudimentary but laid the foundation for the development of one of the most significant operating systems in history.

In the early years, the Linux community grew rapidly. Developers from around the world began contributing to the project, adding code and fixing bugs. The openness of the project allowed anyone to participate, leading to faster development and improvement of the system.

In 1992, the first version of the Linux kernel was released under the GNU General Public License (GPL). Choosing to use the GPL allowed Linux to be distributed as a free and open operating system while protecting the rights of both developers and users.

With the growth of Linux, many different distributions (distros) emerged, each catering to various needs and preferences. The first popular distribution was Slackware, released in 1993. Shortly thereafter, other significant distributions such as Debian (1993) and Red Hat Linux (1994) followed, laying the groundwork for many later variants.

This diversity allowed users to choose an operating system that met their specific requirements, whether for desktop use, server operation, or specialized applications. Each distribution brought its own features and enhancements, contributing to the continuous evolution and refinement of Linux.

During the 2000s, Linux gained increasing prominence in the server sector. Companies recognized the advantages of Linux, including stability, security, and cost-efficiency compared to commercial operating systems. This led to a massive proliferation of Linux servers used for web hosting, databases, and other critical applications.

The advent of Linux in the cloud era marked another significant advancement. Cloud computing providers such as Amazon Web Services (AWS) and Google Cloud Platform (GCP) adopted Linux-based servers to deliver their services. This further boosted the acceptance and use of Linux in the IT world.

While Linux was already well-established on servers, it continued to struggle for acceptance in the desktop arena. Various distributions such as Ubuntu, Fedora, and Linux Mint worked to improve the user-friendliness and accessibility of Linux to appeal to a broader user base.

Ubuntu, released in 2004, was a significant step toward improving usability and user acceptance on the desktop. With its clear user interface and large community, Ubuntu became one of the most widely used desktop distributions and helped establish Linux’s reputation as a user-friendly operating system.

Today, Linux is present in many areas of digital life. In addition to its dominance on servers and in the cloud, Linux has also gained importance in embedded systems and the Internet of Things (IoT). Many devices, such as smart TVs, routers, and even vehicles, use Linux as their operating system, highlighting its flexibility and versatility.

The Linux community remains active and innovative, with continuous developments and improvements aimed at optimizing the system and adapting it to new technologies. With ongoing support from a dedicated community and contributions from businesses, Linux remains a central part of the modern IT landscape.

The history of Linux is a story of visionary innovation, collaborative effort, and the power of the open-source movement. From the humble beginnings of a student project to a global phenomenon, Linux has demonstrated how open-source technology can change the world. Its ongoing development and widespread acceptance across various fields underscore its significance and its ability to meet the challenges of modern technology. It will be exciting to see how Linux continues to evolve and what new opportunities and challenges the future holds.

34. The History of Computers

The history of computers is a captivating journey through a series of revolutionary developments and technological leaps that have fundamentally transformed our world. It starts in the early days of human civilization and extends to the modern era of digital connectivity that shapes our daily lives. Let’s delve into this fascinating evolution.

Our journey begins far back in antiquity when early humans developed rudimentary calculating aids. The abacus, a simple yet effective device, was used in ancient times to perform basic mathematical calculations. These early tools allowed people to tackle complex tasks and laid the groundwork for the development of later computing technologies.

However, the true revolution began in the 17th century with the invention of mechanical calculators. Blaise Pascal, a French mathematician, developed the »Pascaline« in 1642, one of the first mechanical calculators. This device could perform additions and subtractions and was a significant advancement in computing technology. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz built on Pascal’s work by creating a machine capable of multiplication and division. These early machines were impressive technical feats of their time, though they were still far from the computers we know today.

The next major advancement came in the 19th century when Charles Babbage, an English mathematician, designed the »Analytical Engine.« This machine was conceived as a universal computer and represented a revolutionary concept. Babbage’s Analytical Engine could be programmed using punch cards, which is considered the first step toward programming. Ada Lovelace, a mathematician and close associate of Babbage, wrote notes on this machine that are considered the first known computer program. Her visionary work laid the foundation for the development of programming as we know it.

The real era of computers, however, began in the 20th century with the advent of electromechanics and electronics. During World War II, British mathematician Alan Turing developed the Colossus machine, used to decrypt encrypted messages from the German military. This machine is regarded as one of the first true computers of the modern era. At the same time, German engineer Konrad Zuse developed the Z3, which is considered the first fully automatic, programmable digital computer. These early computers were impressive but also enormously large and expensive.

The 1950s brought the next major innovation: the transistor. The transistor replaced the bulky and unreliable vacuum tubes in computers, enabling the development of smaller, more powerful, and more reliable machines. The UNIVAC I, released in 1951, was one of the first commercial computers and helped introduce computer technology to the business world. This era was marked by the introduction of new technologies that further advanced computing.

The 1970s saw the birth of the microprocessor. The Intel 4004, released in 1971, was the first commercially available microprocessor and paved the way for personal computers (PCs). This advancement made computers accessible for everyday use and ushered in a new era of computing. The Apple II, launched in 1977, was one of the first successful personal computers and offered a graphical user interface along with a wide range of software.

The 1980s witnessed the rise of the IBM PC, which debuted in 1981 and set the standard for PC architecture. Microsoft Windows, first released in 1985, played a crucial role in the proliferation of computers by providing a user-friendly graphical interface. With the advent of the internet in the 1990s, the world experienced another revolution. The World Wide Web, developed by Tim Berners-Lee, enabled easy access to information and changed how people communicate and work.

In the present day, computer technology has permeated many areas of our lives. The introduction of smartphones and tablets has made computing more mobile and ubiquitous. Devices like the iPhone, launched in 2007, have revolutionized the mobile computing market and transformed how we interact with technology. Cloud computing has simplified access to powerful computing resources and storage, altering how businesses and individuals use computer resources.

The future of computer technology promises even more exciting developments. Artificial Intelligence (AI) allows machines to learn from data and perform complex tasks, while quantum computing expands the boundaries of computing power and opens new possibilities for solving complex problems.

The history of computers is a story of continuous progress and innovation. From the early abacus to today’s modern devices, it shows how technology has transformed our world. The ongoing advancement of computers offers exciting possibilities for the future and will continue to have a profound impact on our lives. It is fascinating to see how far we have come and what new developments the future of computer technology holds.
