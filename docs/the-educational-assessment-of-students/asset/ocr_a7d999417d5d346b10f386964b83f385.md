# Analytic Scoring Rubrics

An analytic scoring rubric requires that you use an outline of the ideal answer, a list of major elements that students should include in the ideal answer, or several well-defined traits. The analytic scoring rubric for the Ideas/Content dimension shown in the Oregon example is a rubric for only one trait. As such, it is only a part of the analytic scoring rubric for scoring writing. Rubrics for all six traits would constitute an analytic scoring rubric for writing. The complete analytic scoring rubric for writing assessment of the state of Oregon is given in Appendix H.

For subject-matter essays, the analytic scoring rubric works best when the essay question is a focused, restricted response question. When using this rubric, you decide the number of points to award to students when they include each element. An example of an analytic scoring rubric for a restricted response subject-matter essay follows.

# Example

Question: Discuss why it is important to identify various decision contexts when validating educational assessments. In your discussion compare selection, placement, and classification decisions. Be sure to give specific examples of each type of decision to illustrate your comparisons.

# Analytic rubric for scoring the responses

+   1. Reasons for distinguishing decision contexts (4 points)
+   2. Selection decisions
+      a. Description (2 points)
+      b. Example of a selection decision (2 points)
+   3. Placement decisions (2 points)
+      a. Description (2 points)
+      b. Example of a selection decision (2 points)
+   4. Classification decisions (2 points)
+      a. Description (2 points)
+      b. Example of a selection decision (2 points)
+   5. Comparison of the three decision types (2 points)
+      a. Similarities (2 points)
+      b. Differences (2 points)

In this example, the decision to award 2 points or 4 points for certain elements of the answer is based on the teacher's values, as well as the importance of the content to the course. In your classroom, you will have to decide which elements are more important to your learning targets and, therefore, for which you should award more points. When assigning the number of points to each element, be sure the total points match the essay's total numerical value in relation to the overall number of points on the assessment plan.

Usually students' responses will match the scoring rubric to various degrees: A response that is essentially correct receives the full point credit; a completely incorrect answer or an omission receives no points; and a partially correct response receives a partial-credit award. When you assign partial credit in an inconsistent way you lower the reliability of the scoring process. You retain more reliability if you can specify the number of partial-credit points each type of partially correct answer will receive. Teachers are often inconsistent, too, in assigning partial credit to mathematics word problems that require computation. Example 9.2 shows one scoring rubric that adds consistency in awarding partial credit for word problems. The columns at the right show how the partial credits are distributed differently when the total points for the word problem changes from two to five.

Beginning teachers may have trouble crafting partial-credit scoring rubrics because they are not aware of the types of errors and partial knowledge students will display in answering a particular question. After reading a few student papers, however, patterns of errors and misconceptions will emerge. Be alert to these. Then, craft a partial-credit scoring rubric and use it to mark all the papers. It is okay to begin scoring a sample of papers, then to refine your scoring rubric. Go back and rescore the sample, too. In this case, an overall or global impression of the response is the best that can be done. States that do large-scale assessment of either writing or subject-matter essay responses often prefer holistic scoring. The large numbers of papers to be marked often precludes the detailed scoring required by analytic rubrics.

The holistic method is less objective than the analytic method, however, unless you have specified scoring criteria. Two teachers often rate the same student's essay response very differently unless they agree to use the same scoring criteria and have practiced scoring several papers together until they have reached a reasonable level of scoring agreement.
