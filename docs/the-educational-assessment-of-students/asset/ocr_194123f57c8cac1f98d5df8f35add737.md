+   6. Instances or examples of a concept or principle.
+   7. Evidential statements.
+   8. Predictions about phenomena or events.
+   9. Steps in a procedure or process.
+   10. Computations (or other kinds of results obtained from applying a procedure).
+   11. Evaluations of events or phenomena.

Examples of items of each of these types are shown in Table 7.2. Some of the key phrases used to construct items in each category appear in the table, too. You may want to refer to Table 7.2 from time to time to glean suggestions for writing true-false items. However, be sure that you don't follow the table blindly. Be sure that when you use a key phrase the final item assesses your intended thinking skill

# Table 7.2 Types of statements that could form the basis for your true-false items.
Note that to be valid, your items must match specific learning targets

| Type of Statement | Examples of Introductory Words or Phrases | Examples of True-False Items |
|---|---|---|
| Generalization | All...\nMost...\nMany... | Most good true-false items are tests of ability to apply information. (T) |
| Comparative | The difference between... is...\nBoth... and... require... | The difference between the raw scores corresponding to the 45th and 55th percentiles is likely to be smaller than the difference between the raw scores corresponding to the 5th and 15th percentiles. (T) |
| Conditional | If... (then)...\nWhen... | When one has a normal distribution, the standard deviation and standard error are considered equivalent. (F) |
| Relational | The larger...\nThe higher...\nThe lower...\nMaking... is likely to...\nIncreasing... tends to...\nHow much... depends on... | Making a test more reliable is likely to make it less valid. (F) |
| Explanatory | The main reason for...\nThe purpose of...\nOne of the factors that adversely affect...\nSince...\nAlthough... | One of the factors that adversely affect the reliability of an objective test is the amount of guessing the students do in answering it. (T) |
| Exemplary | An example of...\nOne instance of... | An example of a "factual information" question is to ask how horsepower of an engine is calculated. (F) |
| Evidential | Studies of... reveal... | Studies of the marking standards and practices of different faculty members reveal that they tend more toward uniformity than toward diversity. (F) |
| Predictive | One could expect...\nIncreasing... would result in... | One could expect to increase the reliability coefficient of a test from .30 to .60 by doubling its number of items. (F) |
| Procedural | To find... one must...\nIn order to... one must...\nOne method of... is to...\nOne essential step... is to...\nUse... of...\nThe first step toward... | One method of ensuring that scores from a 100-item test and scores from a 25-item test will carry equal weight is to multiply the latter scores by 4. (F) |

| Evaluative | A good...\nIt is better to... than...\nThe best... is...\nThe maximum... is...\nThe easiest method of... is to...\nIt is easy to demonstrate that...\nIt is difficult to...\nIt is possible to...\nIt is reasonable to...\nIt is necessary to... in order to...\nThe major drawback to... is... | It is difficult to obtain reliable scores from a group in which the range of abilities is very wide. (F) |
