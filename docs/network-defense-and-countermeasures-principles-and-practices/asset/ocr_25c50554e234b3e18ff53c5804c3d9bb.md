One legal principle is key to doing forensics in a scientifically sound manner, and is all too often overlooked in forensic books, the Daubert standard. The Legal Information Institute at Cornell University Law School defines the Daubert standard as follows:

Standard used by a trial judge to make a preliminary assessment of an expert's scientific testimony is based on reasoning or methodology that is scientifically valid and can be properly applied to the facts at issue. Under this standard, the factors that may be considered in determining whether the methodology is valid: (1) whether the theory or technique in question can be tested; (2) whether it has been subjected to peer review and publication; (3) its known or potential error rate; (4) the existence and maintenance of standards controlling its operation; and (5) whether it has attracted widespread acceptance within a relevant scientific community.

What means, in layman's terms, is that any scientific evidence presented in a trial has have been reviewed and tested by the relevant scientific community. For a computer investigator, it means that any tools, techniques, or processes you utilize in your investigation should be that are widely accepted in the computer forensics community. You cannot simply make new tests or procedures.

# To Certify or Not to Certify?

Certifications are a controversial topic in IT general, and specifically in security. Some people will tell you that certifications are completely useless, whereas others will tell you that they are the most important things to look for. I think the controversy stems from misunderstanding what certifications are, and this leads to these extreme views. First, understand that a certification, and in of itself, does not make you an expert. A certification indicates you have demonstrated at a minimum level of competence according to some set of criteria. If you understand that, you cannot accept that a certification can be useful in determining if someone has a minor skillset, but cannot determine that person is an expert. In forensics, it is always possible that your investigation will lead to some court proceedings. These can be civil or criminal. I have personally been involved in multiple cases that began as a simple incident investigation and became civil litigation and criminal trials. With in mind, having a certification or two will help show that you actually know forensics. The following list provides a general view of several major certifications in computer forensics:

+   * Computer Forensic Investigator (CHFI): This certification is from the EC-Council tests general forensic knowledge; it is not specific to a particular tool. For more information about this certification, go to https://www.ecouncil.org/programs/computer-hacking-forensic-investigator-chfi/
* Certified Forensic Computer Examiner (CFCE): This certification is from the International Association of Computer Investigative Specialists (IACIS). It is also a general knowledge test rather than a specific tool. See https://www.iacis.org/2016/02/23/cfce/ for details.
