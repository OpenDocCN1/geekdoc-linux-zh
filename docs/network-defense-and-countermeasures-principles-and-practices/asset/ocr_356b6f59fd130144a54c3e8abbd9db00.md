Even with 23 people in the room, you have a 50 percent chance that 2 will have the same birthday.

The probability that the first person does not share a birthday with any previous person is 100 percent, because there are no previous people in the set. That can be written as 365/365.

The second person has only one preceding person, and the odds that the second person will have a different from that is 364/365.

The third person might share a birthday with two preceding people, so the odds of having a birthday from either of the two preceding people are 363/365. Because each of them are independent, we can compute the probability as follows:

```
365/365 × 364/364 × 363/365 ... × 342/365
```

(32 is the probability the 23rd person shares a birthday with a preceding person.) When we convert this to decimal values, it yields (1 × 0.997 × 0.994 × 0.991 × 0.988 × ... 0.936 = 0.49, or 49 percent

This 49 percent is the probability that 23 people will not have any birthdays in common; thus, there is a 51 percent (better than even odds) chance that 23 will have a birthday in common.

The math works out about 1.7 $\sqrt{n}$ to get a collision. Remember a collision is when two inputs produce the same output. For an MD5 hash, you might think you need $2^{128}$ inputs to get a collision. And for a guaranteed collision, you need an exceedingly large number: 34082366909386436347043177e38. The birthday paradox tells us that to have a 51 percent chance of finding a hash, you only need 1.7 $\sqrt{n}$ (being $2^{64}$) inputs. Number is still very large: 31,359,464,925,306,237,747.2. It is much smaller than the effort of trying every single input!

# Differential Cryptanalysis

Differential cryptanalysis is a form of cryptanalysis applicable to symmetric key algorithms. This was invented by Eli Biham and Adi Sham. Essentially it is the examination of differences in an input and how that affects the resultant difference in the output. It originally worked with chosen plaintext. However, it could also work with known plaintext and ciphertext.

The attack is based on pairs of plaintext inputs related by some constant difference. The usual difference is XOR operation, but other methods can be used. The attacker observes the differences in the resulting ciphertexts and is looking for some statistical pattern. The resulting differences are called the *differential*. Another way, differential cryptanalysis focuses on finding a relationship between the changes that occur in the output bits as a result of some input bits.
