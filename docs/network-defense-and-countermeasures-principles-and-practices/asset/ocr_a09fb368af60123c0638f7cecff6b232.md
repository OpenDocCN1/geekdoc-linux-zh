# Defending Against Information Warfare

We have examined the use of computers and the Internet for espionage and for terrorism. Now let's look at a third type of attack. Information warfare certainly predates the advent of the modern computer, and in fact, may be as old as conventional warfare. In essence, information warfare is any attempt to manipulate information in pursuit of a military or political goal. When you attempt to use process to gather information on an opponent or when you use propaganda to influence opinions in a conflict, these are examples of information warfare. Previously we discussed the role of the computer in corporate espionage. The same techniques can be applied to a military conflict in which the computer can be used as a tool in espionage. Although information gathering will not be re-examined in this chapter, information gathering is only one part of information warfare. Propaganda is another aspect of information warfare. The flow of information impacts morale, citizens' outlooks on a conflict, the political support for a conflict, and the involvement of peripheral nations and international organizations.

# Propaganda

Computers and the Internet are very effective tools that can be used in the dissemination of propaganda. Many people now use the Internet as a secondary news source, and some use it as their primary news source. This means that a government, terrorist group, political party, or any activist group could use what appears to be an Internet website as a front to put their own political spin on any conflict. A website does not need to be directly connected to the political organization whose views are being disinfected; in fact, it is better if it is not directly connected.

The Irish Republican Army (IRA), for example, has operated with two distinct and separate divisions: one that takes paramilitary/terrorist action and another that is purely political. This allows the political/information warfare. Sinn Fein has their own website where they disseminate news with their own perspective (www.sinfein.ie). In this situation, it is clear to whoever is reading the information that it is biased towards the perspective of the party sponsoring the site. A better scenario (for the party concerned) occurs when there is an Internet news source that is favorably disposed to a political group's position without having any actual connection at all. This makes it easier for the group to spread information without being accused of obvious bias. The political group (be it a nation, rebel group, or terrorist organization) can then "leak" stories to this news agency.
