# Quantum Computing and Quantum Cryptography

Innovations in quantum computing promise to significantly increase computing power. This will provide advances in numerous aspects of computing including data mining, artificial intelligence, and other applications. However, the quantum computing power will also present a challenge for cryptography. Quantum relies on individual bits to store information. Quantum computing relies on qubits.
The essential issue with quantum computing is the ability to represent more than two states. Quantum computing, using classical bits, can only represent binary values. Qubits, or quantum bits, store data via the polarization of a single photon. The two basic states are horizontal or vertical polarization. However, quantum mechanics allows for a superposition of the two states at the same time. This is something simply not possible in a classical bit. The two states are represented with quantum notation as |0> or |1>. These represent horizontal or vertical polarization. A qubit is the superposition of these two states. This superposition is represented as |ψ> = α|0> + β|1>.
Essentially a classical bit can represent a one or a zero. A qubit can represent a one, a zero, or any quantum superposition of two quantum qubits. This superposition allows for more powerful computing. The superposition allows the qubit to store a one, both and a zero, or a range of values between one and zero. This significantly increases data storage and processing power.
There are already quantum-based algorithms that are far superior at factoring larger numbers than classical algorithms. This is particularly useful because the widely used RSA algorithm is based on the difficulty of factoring a large number into its prime factors. When quantum computers become a reality, that factoring problem will no longer be difficult, and RSA is obsolete. Various key exchanges like Diffie-Hellman depend on the difficulty in solving the discrete logarithm problem. The two most significant improvements to Diffie-Hellman, ElGamal, and MQV (Menezes-Vanstone) also depend on the discrete logarithm problem. Elliptic curve cryptography is based on the difficulty of solving the discrete logarithm problem with respect to an elliptic curve. Quantum algorithms will also make the discrete logarithm problem quite solvable, rendering these obsolete as well.
Essentially all current asymmetric cryptography is based on one of these two classes of number theoretic problems: factoring or solving the discrete logarithm problem. Especially quantum computers become a practical reality, rather than just a research interest, all modern asymmetric algorithms will become obsolete. This is a significant concern for cyber security because all modern e-commerce, encrypted e-mail, and secure communications over a network depend on these algorithms. Currently, the NIST is working on a multi-year study to determine standards for postquantum cryptography. There are several promising cryptographic systems. It is beyond the scope of this chapter to explore quantum cryptography in depth; however, lattice-based cryptography and multivariate cryptography could be good candidates for post quantum cryptographic systems.
