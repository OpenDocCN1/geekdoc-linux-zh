
# Review

## Sakura

Sakura has a devoted following, but will it shine for you?

![](img/c9d26cf718256f071c3af699b9670538_77_0.png)

Sakura’s responsiveness makes it easy to keep track of all of your tabs

### System

With slightly more than minimal dependencies, Sakura runs everywhere (we're using it on a Raspberry Pi B+). There's no real desktop integration – although copy, paste and URL link-following work – but the right-click menu reminded us of the WindowsMaker menus from the Apple Cube.

### Power

Speed is the secret of Sakura's appeal. Some terminal emulators have a noticeable lag when you hit Enter after a command, but not this one. Tabs are quick to load and switch. Although seemingly as minimal as XTerm, it does scroll output correctly with the mouse.

### Languages

On all of our tests with Chinese, Japanese and Greek (ancient and modern), Sakura behaved faultlessly. The days of terminal emulators mangling non-Western European languages are thankfully moving behind us, and those working with code or files from international teams can safely enjoy predictable behaviour here.

### Aesthetics

With standard Gtk libraries and text file settings for custom text colour, font and background images, Sakura makes an effort to go along with your choice of desktop customisation. Its lack of fancy effects is a plus in the performance stakes, but it loses points in this category when even the humble Mrxvt can do transparency of a sort.

### Overall

> Sakura impressed us with its speed. Other terminal emulators lag when executing commands, which you won't notice until you try this one. It gets the basics right, leaving what's fancy to users who can live with bloatware.

## Mrxvt

Lighter but less up-to-date, Mrxvt holds aces for older system users

![](img/c9d26cf718256f071c3af699b9670538_77_1.png)

The old-school menus make Mrxvt stand out, and not in a bad way

### System

Low resource use means it's at home everywhere, aesthetic considerations aside. Not so well integrated as its bloated competitors – no drag-and-drop on our LXDE test machine, though it does accept paste – but for minimal-resource window manager use, it's a good fit in low-memory systems.

### Power

Tabs can accept a bit of individual customisation to commands and titling, or you can broadcast commands to all tabs. Power users will like the keyboard shortcuts, although there's not much done through the menu; you must edit ~/.mrxrc, and also choose of logging through utmp, wrong or lasting.

### Languages

No UTF-8, which wasn't unusual when Mrxvt was seeing more active development; it has support for other character sets – it's quite good with Chinese, Korean and Japanese and works with freetype fonts; you just have to configure your Locale in addition to your default UTF-8 choice.

### Aesthetics

If you run an old-school desktop, the scrollbars can be set in the style of Next or SG1 as well as conventional rvt, Xterm or plain decoration. With true translucent window as well as pseudo transparency, background images, freetype fonts and text shadows, Mrxvt is surprisingly configurable for an old-school terminal.

### Overall

> Despite its customisability, aesthetic and otherwise, Mrxvt still feels a little old-fashioned. Something not helped by the lack of UTF-8. Nevertheless, it's fast, easy to use and perhaps a good second choice in your system.

6

## Tabbed terminal emulators

### Terminator

A Java app, yet a responsive terminal and one with a balanced set of features

![](img/c9d26cf718256f071c3af699b9670538_78_0.png)

- A fast and usable find that works with regular expressions

#### System

Very cross-platform - runs on Windows and OS X and not much more demanding than the GNOME terminal, though it's not as snappy as Sakura. It supported drag-and-drop on our LXDE test machine and works with Nautilus too. Very fuss-free for a Java app, running on Java 6 or above.

#### Power

A find command on an infinite scrollback gives you text and regular expression search across all input and output in your session. Number reinterpretation across bases and proper handling of tab characters make this a great programmer's terminal. Horizontal scrolling is nicely handled where software permits.

#### Languages

UTF-8 is a given and our Greek test files were clearly legible even in small monospaced fonts. The mix of terminal and wide glyphs is a source of pride to the developers, given the space provided on the project's website for comparisons with Mac Terminal.app at non-Latin character handling.

#### Aesthetics

The usual selection of colour themes, configurable fonts and anti-aliased text keeps Terminator at what many nowadays be regarded as the minimum standard even for a terminal emulator. System resources help appearance - Terminator looks very nice on modern distros. Horizontal scrolling instead of wrap-around prevents mangled output in the terminal.

#### Overall

Simpler and more powerful than it first seems, Terminator is a surprise. Hidden behind its GNOME namesake, it has lots to offer. Touches like unlimited scrolling and variable width character glyphs make it a pleasant user experience.

### Roxterm

An outsider perhaps, but surprisingly configurable and easy to work with

![](img/c9d26cf718256f071c3af699b9670538_78_1.png)

- ROX term balances textfile configuration against menu-driven choice

#### System

While it's great on any desktop - and a natural fit on a ROX Desktop, though not many people have embraced that system - GNOME users will be pleasantly surprised by what a well-integrated alternative to GNOME terminal it makes. Drag-and-drop onto the terminal also worked in other environments, including LXDE.

#### Power

Very customisable as long as you don't mind text files - found under `~/.config/`. With everything from D-Bus configurable use of ROX term from other apps to extensive menu-driven configuration, ROX developers have worked hard to give you a powerful terminal in a lightweight package.

#### Languages

UTF-8 is enabled by default and character encoding can be changed in the context-sensitive right-click menu. System of Greek and Japanese fonts were legible, and tab completion worked across character sets. Non-English users should be able to set keyboard shortcuts to other characters.

#### Aesthetics

Menu and GUI-driven configuration choices give plenty of colourful options on a per-tab basis, which may help some needing visual clues to context, when switching between sessions. The colour control is nice but a lack of fancy transparency and effects may be a red line for some users spoilt by the competition.

#### Overall

You've probably never used the ROX Desktop, but its terminal emulator bears closer examination. Nice text file configuration (ent-comprised by quick and easy, menu-driven changeability. It's simple and easy to use.

### AND THE WINNER IS...

#### Terminator

We set out to find the best tabbed terminal emulator — one that's genuinely pleasant to use. That criterion contains a balance between having enough power to make daily management of a Linux system easy, and enough configurability for it to fit into most systems. Too much flashy graphical wizardry slows down what should be an instantly responsive part of your system.

If you've been bitten by flaky Java software before, Terminator is a pleasant surprise. Ready access to debug options in the menu may just be a reflection on the Java toolkit used or an acknowledgement that the software is not entirely without faults, but that said we had little trouble with it.

Speaking of faultless and responsive, if speed is what matters most then try Sakura. ROX terminal was a close second and its scriptability makes it a winner for you. We still run Mrxvt on several old machines, but can no longer recommend it in our UTF-8-enabled world.

Away from the four distros that we tested, we really like FinaTerm (apart from the crashes). Enlightenment's pretty Eterm replacement, Terminology, is also not without its problems.

![](img/c9d26cf718256f071c3af699b9670538_79_0.png)

Keep scrolling back, no matter how many weeks you've had the terminal open

With reasonable and recent hardware we'd suggest giving either a try. But what we're looking at here is the best multitask terminal for as many users and machines as possible. The terminal should be the very name of accessibility and openness of access, hence also our reexamination of compatibility with UTF-8 and non-European character sets - another area where Terminator simply shines compared to its competitors.

The glories of a properly usable find, endless scrollback and nice little touches like looking after tabs properly are things being found in other rivals, but the complete package puts Terminator as our first choice here. Add in a Java app that seems less resource-hungry than GNOME terminal and we have what is a somewhat surprising winner.
