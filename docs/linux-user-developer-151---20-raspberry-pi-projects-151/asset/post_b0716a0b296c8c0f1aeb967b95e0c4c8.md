## Linux 4.0 gets live, no-reboot patching

Torvalds jumps from 3.x, keeps his socks on

Linux kernel 4.0 has been officially released, and while the sudden jump from the 3.x branch may come as a surprise, there’s a welcome new development along for the ride: the ability to patch a live, running kernel without rebooting.

In November 2013, founder and maintainer Linus Torvalds suggested that a jump to a new full-version release was in the works, joking that “we’re getting to release numbers where I have to take off my socks to count that high again.” To “keep the numbers small and easy to remember,” Torvalds suggested that Linux 3.20 would release as Linux 4.0 – and now he has officially made good on that plan.

Linux 4.0 is now the mainline release and comes with a major feature with an interesting development history: the ability to patch the kernel on a live system without rebooting. This feature was originally available to Linux users back in 2008 as part of a project dubbed kSplice. This software used the stop_machine() functionality to halt the system, patch the kernel and restart things again without rebooting.

kSplice proved useful, but was acquired by Oracle in 2011 and now exists as a proprietary service with no plans for further open source releases. With customers clamouring for similar functionality, both SUSE and Red Hat began working on their own implementations – kGraft and kPatch – and released early builds near-simultaneously last year, with each designed to work specifically on their own distributions.

“It pretty quickly became obvious to the interested parties that it’s absolutely impractical in this case to have several isolated solutions for one task to co-exist in the kernel,” explained kernel developer Jiri Kosina in the pull notes addressed to Torvalds for the new live-patching functionality. “During a dedicated Live Kernel Patching track at LPC (the ‘Linux Plumbers’ Conference’ in Düsseldorf, all the interested parties sat together and came up with a joint approach that would work for both distro vendors.

“It provides a basic infrastructure for functional ‘live patching’ – ie code redirection – including API for kernel modules containing the actual patches, and API/ABI for userspace to be able to operate on the patches (look up what patches are applied, and enable/disable them etc)”, Kosina explains.

Described by Kosina as “relatively simple and minimalistic,” the new functionality uses ftrace and other existing kernel infrastructure to reduce its complexity and it exists self-contained. Its initial release is a reference implementation for x86 hardware, with support for PowerPC, S390 and ARM architectures already in the works.

Both Red Hat and SUSE have agreed that with Kosina’s pull request now accepted into the Linux kernel properly, they will begin work to port their own equivalent functionality to the new common infrastructure. This means abandoning their existing out-of-tree projects in favour of something that will be much more cross-compatible to use.

## Elementary OS criticised for calling users cheaters

*When is free not free?*

The developers of elementary OS have been castigated for claiming that those who choose to download the open source Ubuntu spin-off distribution without making a financial contribution are “cheating the system.”

In a recent blog post that explained a plan to force users wanting download the Ubuntu-derived operating system to type in a dollar value (including zero for a free download), the elementary OS team claimed that “we want users to understand that they are pretty much cheating the system when they choose not to pay for the software.”

Users are quick to take the group to task, but despite editing the post to remove the offending sentence, the comment section quickly filled with ire-laden messages. “You called us cheaters, deleted the comment WITHOUT an apology, are now whining – trying to guilt your core user base – about the fact that YOU made the decision to base your concept for an operating system around SOMEBODY ELSE’S WORK,” one commenter posted. “You made your own bad” said someone else. “Call me a cheat,” another stated, “that’s a paddling,” quoting a famous line from *The Simpsons*.

## PyCon UK organiser John Pinner passes away

Much-loved Python community member John Pinner has passed away.

A contributor to the Free and Libre Open Source Software (FLOSS) UK group, formerly known as the UK Linux Users Group (UKLUG), Pinner was a noted Python evangelist.

As well as a Python Software Foundation fellow and Community Service Award, Pinner was known for helping to organise the EuroPython conference from 2004 to 2011 and the PyCon UK conference from 2007 to 2014.

“I had the great honour and pleasure of meeting, and spending time with John at PyCon UK in 2013,” wrote the PSF’s Mary Ann Sutherland in light of his passing. “He was a delightful host, full of energy, knowledgeable about the locale – history, good beers and the best pubs, landmarks, neighbourhoods – and enthusiastic goodwill. He will be sorely missed.”

His passing has left a deep sadness among the Python community.
