## OPEN HARDWARE

# Facebook unveils modular switch
1.28Tb/s of bandwidth available with the new open hardware modular switch

![](img/c9d26cf718256f071c3af699b9670538_9_0.png)

Social networking giant Facebook has announced 6-pack, which it describes as the first open hardware modular switch and is designed for use in its expansive data centres.

Based on a top-of-rack network switch project dubbed Wedge, 6-pack uses a Linux-based operating system called FBOSS with a data centre fabric based around a modular architecture for fast and easy scalability.

Described as the core of Facebook's new data centre fabric, 6-pack uses Wedge as a building block and works as a full-mesh non-blocking two-stage switch across twelve independent switching elements. Two configurations exist at present: one features 18 40-gig Ethernet ports to the front and a further 16 to the back; the other has all 32 ports to the rear for 1.28T of bandwidth and is mostly used for aggregation purposes.

"With 6-pack, we have created an architecture that enables us to build any size switch using a simple set of common building blocks," explained Facebook's Yuval Bacher at the company's announcement. "Because the design is so open and so modular – and so agnostic when it comes to switching technology and software – we hope this is a platform that the entire industry can build on."

Bacher has claimed that the switch design offers greater flexibility than traditional modular switch designs, thanks to its open hardware architecture and Linux-based open source operating system, and has indicated that Facebook has already begun production testing of Wedge, FBOSS and the 6-pack modular switch itself.

Facebook has further confirmed that they have contributed the design of 6-pack to the Open Compute Project, of which it is a founding member.

# Demand for Linux professionals rising
Foundation report points to certification desirability

![](img/c9d26cf718256f071c3af699b9670538_9_1.png)

Above Report claims demand for Linux professionals is rising rapidly

A report from the Linux Foundation and Dice reveals that rising interest in cloud platforms based on open source technologies has led to a growth in the job market for Linux experts.

Compiled from data supplied by over a thousand hiring managers and nearly 3,500 Linux professionals, the report claims that "nearly all hiring managers" (97 per cent of those surveyed) are looking to hire Linux experts soon. Other highlights from the report include that 42 per cent of hiring managers will be looking for experience in OpenStack and Cloudstack, while 23 per cent are looking for security expertise, with a further 19 per cent concentrating on software-defined networking (SDN).

A requirement for formal certification is becoming a trend in the industry, however. The report states that 44 per cent of hiring managers say they are "more likely" to hire a candidate with a recognised Linux certification, with 54 per cent expecting those applying for system administrator roles to have either certification or other evidence of formal training.

"Demand for Linux talent continues, and it's becoming more important for employers to be able to verify candidates have the skillsets they need", claimed Jim Zemlin, executive director at The Linux Foundation, of the report. "Formal training and certifications are a key way of identifying qualified talent and as more people join the Linux community, it will be necessary for professionals to show they stand out."

The full report is on The Linux Foundation's website at linuxfoundation.org.

# VMware violation alleged
Christoph Hellwig and SF Conservancy file suit

Virtualisation specialist VMware is currently being sued by Christoph Hellwig and the Software Freedom Conservancy in the District Court of Hamburg, for the alleged violation of the GNU General Public Licence.

![](img/c9d26cf718256f071c3af699b9670538_10_0.png)

Kernel developer Hellwig has been working to bring VMware's alleged GPL violations in its ESXi hypervisor product to light since 2007, working with the SF Conservancy since 2012. While VMware opened negotiations with the group, progress was halted in 2014 when counsel for VMware rejected proposed resolutions.

“VMware's legal counsel finally informed the SF Conservancy in 2014 that VMware had no intention of ceasing their distribution of proprietary-licensed works derived from Hellwig's and other kernel developers' copyrights,” the charitable FLOSS group claimed in its announcement of the suit, “despite the terms of GPLv2.”

Hellwig and the SF Conservancy are represented by Till Jaeger of JBB Rechtsanwälte, known for his work with Harald Welte on previous GPL violation suits.

VMware has not publicly commented on the suit, but is expected to defend its distribution practices in court.

# Freak attack hits OpenSSL and more
Though not as serious as Heartbleed

Several SSL/TLS implementations, including OpenSSL, have been found vulnerable to a new flaw dubbed the Freak attack.

Discovered by Karthikeyan Bhargavan at INRIA in Paris and the mTLS team, the Freak vulnerability enables an attacker to downgrade the encryption in use by a web browser to an easily-broken and outdated cipher. The flaw is claimed to affect 26.3 per cent of all HTTPS servers live on the internet, giving it serious scope for exploitation to a number of users.

While the decade-old flaw affects numerous SSL/TLS implementations in browsers and servers, security experts have claimed Freak is not as serious as the recent Heartbleed vulnerability.

“To exploit Freak, users need to connect to servers where support for downgraded export keys is still enabled and have an attacker on their network monitoring their connection with this server,” explained Andy Manske, senior product manager at AllenVaulf.

“Users who need to be extra cautious here are ones who, by design, have an entity in the middle of their traffic,” agreed TK Keanini, chief technical officer at Lanceco. “For example, some nation states control Internet gateways in and out of their nation and because of this, topological placement are in an optimal place to exploit daily users.”

Saints were almost as common as books in 6th Century Ireland, which may go some way towards explaining the later dispute about the right to make a copy of a book. Texts were incredibly sacred and in the absence of printing presses, a lot could hang on the variations between one copy and another.

Writing and copying books was usually the work of monks. Columba was one of the most famous scribes of his time and is said to have written, or copied, over 3000 books during his lifetime. The dispute over the copy of the book began in 560. The book in question was

The Psalter of St Jerome, which Columba had borrowed from Finnián of Movilla without Finnián’s knowledge. Columba’s copy was illicitly “made at night in haste by a miraculous light”. Finnián ordered Columba to return both book and copy which outraged Columba and provoked him to take it to court for judgement – and so it was here that the quarrel that led to a war began.

A court was convened under Brehon law, the common law system which governed everyday life in Ireland, and the adjudicator was King Diarmait Mac Certhlaith, who made the famous judgement that “To every cow belongs her calf; therefore to every book belongs its copy”. This can easily be seen as the world’s earliest known judgement that was made in favour of copyright.

Columba was said to be astonished and outraged by the judgement and believed the king to be biased. The sequence of events was complicated when the king had a prisoner apprehended and killed while under the protection of Columba. Columba and his kinsmen raised an army to fight the king, and they met at the battle of Du Crenlinne. Three thousand of the king’s men were killed and only one of Columba’s kin was lost.

As a result of this slaughter Columba was forced into exile on the island of Iona, where he founded a monastery and is credited with bringing Christianity to the Picts. The copy of the book that caused the war is now claimed to be the oldest book in Ireland. The book is assumed to have been returned to Ireland with other relics from Iona, including The Book of Kells, to protect them from invading Vikings, and is known as the ‘Cathach’, or battle book, of St. Columba. Dating evidence suggests the Cathach could be old enough to be Columba’s copy of The Psalter of St Jerome, but of course, it may just be a copy of a copy.
