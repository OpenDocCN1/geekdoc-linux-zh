# # Cracking the
coding skills enigma

Zach Samocha, director at software testing company Coverity, discusses the importance and prevalence of coding in the UK

![](bbox=[0.12, 0.39, 0.26, 0.56])

**Zack Samocha** is a director at Coverity. In his current role he defines the company product strategy and manages the Coverity open source Scan program, which has grown under his leadership to 4,000 projects and 10,000 active users. Zack also initiated research comparing open source code quality with proprietary code.

**2014: the Year of Code** – or at least that's how many people viewed the 12 months that catapulted this precious skill into the limelight. Coding gained huge media attention last year, especially when it was added to the UK educational curriculum in September – a move that many people celebrated.

This was all really positive progress for the IT industry as a whole, even though the new-found attention on coding might take a little while to change businesses. Still, bringing up a wider range of talent with a greater set of skills is definitely a step in the right direction, especially considering the lack of skilled coders out there at the moment. Along with gadgets like the Raspberry Pi, these developments aim to spark a passion in young people early on in their lives about the mechanics of computing. Hopefully, their interest in this topic will grow throughout their teenage years and into their career.

The sheer volume of people learning how to code in the UK is exciting but important at the same time, as it is changing how we think about IT in this country. People are thinking more and more about the possibilities around apps and software, rather than just having to support and maintain the desktops, laptops and printers in offices around the country.

This is all great news for the coding community, but we can't get carried away.

Even though coding skills are becoming more popular in wider society, this popularity doesn't necessarily match up with a growth in code quality. Unfortunately, we have to consider whether the rising amount of programmers and the standard of code that they are producing will lower the standard of contributions to open source projects, for example, as an area that has seen a lot of progress in recent times.

Let's take driving as an analogy here; millions of us in the UK are legally allowed to drive as we have met all the requirements. We passed the practical and theory exams, bought a vehicle and paid road tax in order to maintain the roads we drive on. When coding, you don't need to have a licence, you only need to know how to do it and get your hands on the tools. Coding is available to everyone as there is no formal regulation, and as a result it attracts a lot of individual coders.

'Self-starters', if anyone, can create a range of software and applications without even thinking about having a formal qualification.
