The `localityName_default` 1 is the name of your city. The `organizationUnitName_default` 2 is the part of your company this certificate is for. One of the most commonly misunderstood values in OpenSSL, `commonName_default` 3, is the hostname of the machine this certificate is for, as it appears in reverse DNS. Remember, reverse DNS isn't necessarily the same as the hostname! Your web server might have a nice friendly name, but the hosting company might assign it a totally different name in reverse DNS. Finally, `emailAddress_default` 4 is the email address of the site administrator.

These values all show up in prompts in the OpenSSL command as default choices. Setting them in the configuration file will save you annoyance later.

# ## Certificates

One interesting thing about public-key encryption is that the author and the audience don't have to be people. They can be programs. Secure Shell (SSH) and the Secure Sockets Layer (SSL) are two different ways programs can communicate without fear of intruders listening in. Public-key cryptography is a major component of *digital certificates* used by secure websites and secure mail services. When you open Firefox to buy something online, you might not realize that the browser is frantically encrypting and decrypting web pages. This is why your computer might complain about “invalid certificates,” someone’s public key has either expired or the certificate is self-signed. Today’s protocols encrypt and decrypt with *Transport Layer Security* (TLS) and use *TLS certificates*.
