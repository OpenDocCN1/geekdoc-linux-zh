### The million pound problem
In your column about the NHS spending more money on Microsoft products, I don't think saying they 'decided to throw more money at a company' is the right way to put it. Unfortunately, in the so called "professional" world, which includes places like the NHS, the software installed is often custom-made for them, written by people who are themselves heavily invested in the same OS (or else they wouldn't be developing for only that OS), and set up to, intentionally or not, lock-in the people they sell to into the OS they picked. In the US, a lot of shipping truck repair places have the same issue — they are using stuff that just will not run on newer versions of Windows, never mind on another OS, because, in no small part, certain behaviours of the OS are mandatory for the security and/or function of the program. To replace the program, or even get a version that works in general, either on a new OS, or with the computer in the truck, is literally impossible, because the only people that can do that (either a) haven't yet worked out how to port it, or b) suddenly find that they, quite simply, painted themselves into the same corner, and can't port it, or c) went out of business, and are no longer around to fix their own mess.

Deanne Cooley

It's an unfortunate factor of business that problems like these arise. Extra money would be required for training on any kind of new software as well, whether Microsoft-created or open source. The weird thing is, WINE has better compatibility with older software than modern Windows does -- a bit of a scary thought. The next time they need to do such an upgrade, though, there may have been a bit of a paradigm change in terms of popular operating systems. It will be interesting to see how this plays out.

> *Above The NHS has many money problems – sinking more of it into Microsoft may not pay off in the long term*

### Fragmented universe
In your Steam article you quoted Ron's concern being just fragmentation with the Steam Machines. It's not fragmentation. It's unification. Fragmentation would be if Steam Machines were just another console with another console ecosystem and community. Steam Machines are not that. And I have a feeling that Valve originally hoped to partner with Sony for this endeavour to meld Steam with the living room, but for whatever reason, it fell through and the only thing we saw from it all was Portal 2 (the PS3 version shipped with a Steam key and supported cross-play with the Steam version).

Steam Machines aren't for everyone, but there's a market for them. I want one, though I'll likely end up piecing my own together due to costs. The ability to buy XCOM (for example) just one time and play it wherever I want -- on my Linux laptop, my Windows desktop (in theory; I only run Linux), or my Mac (again, in theory), as well as on my TV in my living room using my Steam Machine console. And not only that, but I have one friends list, one achievements list, one online community... I couldn't do this sort of thing with my PS3 games (Portal 2 being the exception). Point is: this is unification, not fragmentation.

Dana

It's a valid point, though with all the different types of Steam Machine that are planned, there is real fragmentation as well. The cheaper ones are moderately powerful but nowhere near the capabilities of the others.

Buying something for a Steam Machine in a year or two may well have you asking 'Does it work on my specific hardware?', which is the problem Ron was alluding to. It's the same issue we get with Android, as you yourself alluded to. As the hardware isn't really out yet, we don't know if there's going to be some kind of compatibility requirement for these systems to make it easier. Valve likely still has some tricks up its sleeves we've yet to see.

> Below Steam Machines are on the horizon but will they be fragmented?
