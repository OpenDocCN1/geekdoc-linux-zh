For some applications, you need to identify the device files used to communicate with the sound card and other hardware. While your system may not have all these available, the devices that your audio programs use to access audio hardware in Fedora and RHEL include:

+   - **/dev/audio/devaudio1** — Devices that are compatible with Sun workstation audio implementations (audio files with the .au extension).

+   - **/dev/cdrom** — Device representing your first CD-ROM drive. (Additional CD-ROM drives are located at /dev/cdrom1, /dev/cdrom2, and so on.)

+   - **/dev/dsp1,/dev/adsp** — Digital sampling devices, which many audio applications identify to access your sound card.

+   - **/dev/mixer,/dev/mixer1** — Sound-mixing devices.

+   - **/dev/sequencer** — Device that provides a low-level interface to MIDI, FM, and GUS.

+   - **/dev/midi00,/dev/midi** — Device that provides access to midi devices.

> TIP: Notes in the /dev directory, such as /dev/audio,aren't just regular files. They represent access points to the physical devices (hard disks, COM ports, and so) that are connected to your system, or pseudo-devices (such as Terminal). For example, to find out the device of your current terminal window, then send some data to that device. For example, if your name is /dev/pts/0, type:
$ **echo "Hello There" > /dev/pts/0

The words Hello There appear in that terminal. You can sending messages among several terminal windows. If a user is logged on to the computer has terminal windows open, you can send messages to him or her in this way. (I know people who would send a dictionary file to an unsuspecting user's terminal. Although it wasn't destructive, it was annoying if you were trying to get done.)
