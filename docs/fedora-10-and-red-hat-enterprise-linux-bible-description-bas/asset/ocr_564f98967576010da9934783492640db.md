# Internet domains

You can't read a magazine, watch a TV commercial, or open a cereal box these days without coming across a "something.com." When a company, organization, or person wants you to connect to them on the Internet, it relies on the uniqueness of its particular domain name. However, within that domain name, the company or organization to which it has been can arrange its however it chooses.

Internet domains are organized in a structure called the *domain name system (DNS)*. At the top of structure is a set of *top-level domains* (or TLDs). The top-level domains are commonly used in the United States, although they are available for worldwide use. such as *edu* (for colleges and universities), *gov* (for United Government), and *mil* (for United Military sites) were among the most used TLDs in the early Internet. In more recent years, *com* (for commercial sites) has experienced the most growth.

The *us* domain was added to U.S. institutions, such as local governments and elementary schools, as well as individuals within a geographical region of the United States. Recently, new domains such as *info* (for people and business to publish information about themselves) and *biz* (at com for businesses) have been added.

To facilitate the entry of other countries to the Internet, the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) has defined a set of two-letter codes that are assigned to each country; includes codes such as *tv* for Tuvalu and *de* for Germany (Deutschland). Within each country are authorities responsible for organizing the domains. Some domains are organized by categories, others are structured by geographic location. Some locales have offered their domain names for general use, including the *cc* domain (for Cocos Keeling Islands territory of Australia) and *ws* (representing Western Samoa).

Domain names are hierarchical, where there can be subdomains beneath second-level domains, as well as host computers. (Second-level domains are the names directly below the TLDs that are assigned to individual people and organizations.) Each subdomain is separated by a dot (.), starting with the top-level domain on the right and with the second-level domain and each subsequent subdomain appearing to the left. Here is an example of a fully qualified domain name for a host:

```
baskets.crafts.handhistory.com
```

In this example, the top level domain is .com. The second-level domain name assigned to the organization that controls the domain is *handhistory*. Within that domain is a subdomain, or third-level domain, *crafts*. The last name (*baskets*) refers to a particular computer within that third-level domain. From other hosts, it can be referred to as *baskets*. From the Internet, you would refer to it as *baskets.crafts.handhistory.com*.
