
# Myth II: Soulblighter

If you like knights and dwarves and storming castles, Myth II: Soulblighter for Linux might be for you. In Myth II, you are given a mission and some troops with various skills. From there, you need strategy and the desire to shed lots of virtual blood to meet your goal.

Myth II was created by Bungie Software (http://www.bungie.net) and ported to Linux by Loki Entertainment Software (http://www.lokigames.com). This version of the popular Myth game includes improved graphics and new scenarios.

A demo version is available that runs well in Fedora and RHEL. As usual, you will need a computer that is at least a Pentium 133 MHz, with 32MB RAM, 80MB swap space, and 100MB of free disk space. You need network hardware for multiplayer play (network card or dial-up) and a sound card if you want audio. A screenshot of Myth II is shown in Figure 7-9.

## Heretic II

Based on the Quake Engine, Heretic II sets you on a path to rid the world of a deadly, magical plague. As the main character, Corvus, you explore dungeons, swamps, and cities to uncover and stop the plague. The graphics are rich and the game is quite engaging.

You will experience some crashes with Heretic II out-of-the-box. Be sure to check for the update to Heretic II at http://updates.lokigames.com, which should fix most of the problems.

## Neverwinter Nights

BioWare (http://www.biow.com) dipped its foot into Linux gaming waters with a Linux client for its popular Neverwinter Nights game. Neverwinter Nights is a classic role-playing game in the swords-and-sorcery world. You can develop your character and adventuring, or play with others via a LAN or the Internet. You can even build your own worlds and hosts as the Dungeon Master. Neverwinter Nights is licensed by Wizards of the Coast to use Dungeon & Dragons rules and material.

In order to use the Neverwinter Nights Linux client, you must purchase the game itself from BioWare. You must also have access to files from a Windows installation of the game. If you or a friend have the Windows version already, you can grab the files from that version. Otherwise, you'll find them online in a number of locations. See the installation instructions at http://nwn.biow.com for links.

# Summary

With the addition of hot titles like Doom 3, Far Cry and EverQuest2 to the list of playable titles, Linux continues to grow as a gaming platform. You can spend plenty of late nights gaming on Linux. Old UNIX games that made their way to Linux include a variety of X Windows-based games. There are card games, strategy, and some action games for those less inclined to spend 36 hours playing Doom 3.

On the commercial front, Civilization: Call to Power and Myth II are available to use on your Fedora or RHEL system. Unfortunately this will probably disappear because Loki Software (which ported these applications to Linux) went out of business. Fortunately, the future of high-end Linux gaming seems to be in the hands of TransGaming Technologies, which has created Cega from previous WINE technology to allow Windows games to run in Linux. It is also in the hands of cutting-edge developers (id Software) and hardware manufacturers (NVIDIA and AMD) who are expanding driver and application support for Linux.

## Music, Video, and Images in Linux

**In This Chapter**

- Listening to music
- Using Webcams and TV cards

Nearly every kind of audio and video format available today can be played, displayed, encoded, decoded, and managed in Linux. With the development of the Theora video codec, there are now patent-free, royalty-free formats available for every major type of multimedia content. If you are starting from scratch you can legally create, manipulate, and share your own multimedia content from Linux using all applications and codecs.

This chapter covers many different tools that come with Fedora and RHEL for playing or displaying digital music, video, and images. It also takes a swipe at explaining some of the legal issues surrounding playing commercial movie DVDs, MP3 music, and other various/video formats in Linux.

Video content that is readily available on the Internet for playing movie clips, commercial films, and other can be viewed over several different players or available for Fedora and RHEL. Also, you can view television and video using TV cards and Webcams.

Because CD-ROM is the physical medium of choice for recorded music, this chapter describes how to set up and use CD burners to create your own music CDs. After your CD burner is set up to record music, you can use the same CD burner to back up your data or create software CDs. (The tools can be used to burn DVDs as well).

Commercial Linux vendors, including Red Hat, have decided not to add software codecs (which encode and decode multimedia formats), even if they were written from scratch and covered under the GPL, that are encumbered by contentious software patents. After Thompson and Fraunhofer Gesellschaft (which controlled the MP3 patent) began requesting licensing fees in 2002 of $507 for MP3 decoders (per system), Red Hat dropped MP3 support. (See http://www.mp3licensing.com/royalty for details.)

To clarify, I am not talking about copyright else's commercial code and nobody can rightly claim that it is okay to copy someone's code and release it as free software. It would clearly violate copyright laws. What we are talking about patents.

The idea of a patent is to allow to control the rights regarding who can make, sell, offer to sell, use an invention that the patent applicant dreamed up. As it relates to multimedia software in particular, the encoding and decoding of audio and video content are covered by patents. So, even if open source developers write every piece of code from scratch to encode and decode content, it may not be legal to distribute it without paying a royalty to the patent owner.

Major efforts are underway (especially in Europe) to oppose software patents. Refer to the Foundation for a Free Infrastructure Web site (http://fii.org) for further information. The contention is that many related to software are being patented that could severely cripple the ability to innovate (especially for open source developers or small software companies without huge legal teams).

Patents have been granted in Europe for common items that might appear on a Web page, such as selling things over a network, using an electronic shopping cart, and rebate codes (see http://webshop.fii.org). Although there are now laws in Europe that are aimed at preventing further software patents, some software patents are still being granted and existing patents are still being used to discourage innovation.
