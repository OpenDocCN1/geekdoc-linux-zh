+   **Music CDs (CDDA)** — Commercial music CDs are nearly all stored in the Compact Digital Audio system (CDDA). Fedora and RHEL applications that can play music CDs include CD Player (gnome-cd) and KsCD (kscd). Rhythmbox can play music CDs, as well as import to your hard disk (using Sound Juicer) so you can manage your music from one location. Other applications in Fedora that play audio files from CDs includes xms and grimp.

+   **Ogg Vorbis** — If you are compressing and storing music from scratch, Ogg Vorbis is the best choice if you want to avoid completely any royalties. The libvorbis codec is included with Fedora and RHEL and makes it possible to play audio encoded in Ogg Vorbis in a variety of Linux music players, including xms, Rhythmbox, ogg123, and others. The vorbis-tools package also includes utilities for encoding (oggenc) and decoding (oggodec) Ogg Vorbis content to or from WAV and raw music formats. The Xiph.Org foundation develops Ogg Vorbis audio formats and Video formats.

+   **MP3 Audio** — MPEG Layer 3 (MP3) has become the standard format for storing audio files that are transmitted over computer networks (such as the Internet). Because of licensing issues with distributing players, Red Hat does not have codecs to encode or decode MP3 files in any of its distributions. However, Fedora gives you the opportunity to download free, legal MP3 decoders from Fluendo (as described earlier in this chapter).

Another way to get MP3 support, which may not be legal where you are, is to install the xms-mp3 package, which contains software needed by Linux audio players to play MP3 audio files from WAV, AIFF, or raw audio files to play on MP3 players. Many may need the lame package to create compressed audio files Many use the mpg321 command-line MP3 player, which is available in the mpg321 package. (All of these packages are available from the Livna RPM repository. See Chapter 5 on installing software from the Livna repository.)

+   **FLAC Audio** — FLAC is an open source lossless audio format. Lossless means that it compresses the audio as much without losing quality. In comparing the same compressed in Ogg Vorbis and FLAC (using default settings), FLAC files are on about six times the size of the Ogg Vorbis files.

Many of the applications that play Ogg Vorbis files can play audio files as well. Rhythmbox, ogg123, and xms can all play FLAC files. You can encode FLAC audio using Sound Juicer or the flac command, among others.

+   **Other Audio Formats** — While the audio formats mentioned are the most common used for music files, there are other audio formats you may want to play from Linux. Refer to the description of the so utility for several audio formats that are supported by that utility. Use the play command (which comes with the so package) or aplay (which is in the alta-utils package) to play content stored in any of its supported formats.
