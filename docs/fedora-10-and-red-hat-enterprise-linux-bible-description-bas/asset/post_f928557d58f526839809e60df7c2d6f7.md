The example in Figure 8-3 shows a default Fedora system, where PulseAudio is the primary system, alsamixer doesn't control the sound cards directly. It only acts as a master controller for PulseAudio. If you don't have to have GNOME utilities, such as the Volume AppBar, installed, you can use the utilities that come with the al-utils package in Fedora and RHEL. In particular, that package contains the alsamixer utility, which lets you adjust or mute the various sounds. It also lets you select the device from which you can record or otherwise capture audio input.

The alsamixer utility is an application, which means it is viewed graphically from a shell. It can be used to manage sound levels for more than one sound card on a computer, with multiple devices representing it. Type alsamixer from a Terminal window to start in playback mode (to adjust audio output) or alsamixer -V capture (to select an audio capture device and adjust audio capture level).

Here are some to adjust your audio with alsamixer:

- Use the right and left arrow keys to move among the different sounds tracks.
- Use the up and down arrow keys to adjust the levels of the current tracks.
- Press m to mute or unmute the current track (playback mode only).
- Press the spacebar to mark the capture device, for devices such as microphone or line-in that are appropriate for capturing audio. Then adjust the bar to set the level at which audio is captured. (Note that this works in capture mode only: alsamixer -V capture.)

Tracks that are muted appear an MM at the bottom of the slider. When unmuted, 0 appears instead. If more tracks are available than can appear on the screen, the right arrow key enables you to scroll to the right to display additional track bars.

If two channels are available on a track, you can adjust them individually. With the track selected, the q, w, and e keys to adjust the left, right, and channels up, respectively.

Here are a few general rules for adjusting your audio channels:

- To avoid unwanted noise on playback or record, mute any tracks you are not using.
- An icon representing your GNOME Volume Control utility should appear on your desktop panel. The single slider associated with that icon may be to adjust your master volume or headphone port output. Right-click that icon and select Preferences to change to a different port.
- To test your audio channels are working, use the speaker-test -c4 will send a tone to each of four speakers in turn (front left, front right, rear left, and right) to check that each is working. Note that the tone output may surprise you with its loudness.

# Setting your sound card to record

I added this section on setting your sound card to record because people often miss this step. You may run many applications (like Skype) and wonder why nothing records. The reason is that you need to identify the capture device before you record and make sure its level is high enough to work.

The easiest way to use the channel for audio capture is to use the GNOME Volume Control window described earlier. Click the dropdown list and choose the Capture device that controls your microphone (or other input device). Make sure that all audio devices are muted except the one you want to record from. Available devices include microphone, line-in, and DAC. A red X through a microphone input beneath each capture device indicates that it is muted.

Move the slider for your capture device up to an appropriate level. Connect your microphone or input device (to line in) and start the application you want to record from.

# Choosing audio players

There are audio players in Fedora and RHEL for playing music and sound files in a variety of formats. Without adding any software, you can play music CDs and Ogg Vorbis audio (which you can rip and encode yourself). MP3 support can be added to some of these players, while MP3 players outside of Red Hat distributions are also available.

- **KsCD Player (ksed)** — The KsCD player comes with the KDE desktop. To use KsCD, the kmultimedia package must be installed. From the Applications menu (KDE desktop), select Sound & Video → KsCD (or type ksed from a Terminal window). This player lets you cite, track, and artist information from the CD database, and lets you submit information you type in a CD database (if your CD isn't found there).

- **Rhythmbox (rhythmbox)** — Import and manage your music collection with Rhythmbox music management and playback software for GNOME. Rhythmbox uses Gstreamer and Sound Juicer to extract music from CD, and compresses it using Ogg Vorbis, FLAC, or a low-quality WAV (for speech) audio format. Besides allows you to create playlists of your music library, Rhythmbox also has features for playing radio stations. Open Rhythmboxbox from the Applications menu by selecting Sound & Video → Music Player.

- **XMMS (xmms)** — The X Multimedia System (XMMS) audio player provides a simple, graphical player for Ogg Vorbis, WAV, FLAC, and other audio formats. XMMS has a simple windows-like look and feel, which you can adjust using a few dozens. The xmms package is available in the Fedora repository. To add CD player support to yours, install the xmms-crepage package as well.

- **ogg123, mpg321, aplay, or** — If you don't have access to the desktop, you can use the text-based ogg123, mpg321, or play commands. The ogg123 command with the vorbis-tools package, aplay is part of the als-utils package, and play comes with the sock package in Fedora and RHEL. The mpg321 command is available from the Livna.org RPM site. There is a mpg123 project, which is no longer maintained and is not fully covered under the GPL.

The default CD player is Rhythmbox for the current release. One advantage of Rhythmbox, and other Gstreamer audio applications, is that it will work with the free and legal MP3 codes.

> NOTE: If you try some of these CD players, see Troubleshooting CD-ROM for further information.

# Automatically playing CDs

When you put a CD into your CD-ROM, a desktop icon (Rhythmbox) automatically pops up and begins playing the CD. If you don't want to have CDs automatically start or if you want to use a different player by default, you can change that behavior from the Media tab in Nautilus.

# Troubleshooting CD-ROM

If you are unable to play CDs on your CD-ROM, here are a few things you can check:

- Verify that your sound card is installed and working (see “Configuring a sound card” earlier).
- Verify that the CD-ROM drive was detected when you booted. If your CD-ROM drive is an IDE drive, type `dmesg | grep -i cd`. You should see messages about your CD-ROM that looks like `ata2:0: ATAPI: HL-DT-RW/DVD DRIVE GCC-424N, 0201, max UDMA/33`.
- If you see no indication, verify that the power supply and cables are connected. To make sure the hardware is working, you can also boot to DOS and access the CD.
- Check that your CD-ROM drive is not blacklisted because of buggy firmware or other issues. See www.tlid.org/HOWTO/Hardware-HOWTO/cdrom.html.
- If you get the CD-ROM working, but it fails with the message CDROM device: Permission denied when you try to play music as a nonroot user, the problem may be that the device related to medium is not readable by anyone but root. Type `grep media` to see what device name represents the drive. If were `/dev/scd0`, type `chmod 644 /dev/scd0` to enable all users to read it.
