
As a general rule, the best interface type is SCSI, because it's fastest and supports the largest number of devices per interface (7 or 15, depending upon interface type, compared to 4 for IDE/ATAPI). IDE/ATAPI drives are acceptable for workstations or if you're working a tight budget. In the future, USB drives might be acceptable if you need portability, but in early 2001, USB tape drives and Linux are an unproven combination. Floppy-interface drives are old news. You might use one to back up a low-capacity system if you happen to have the drive alike, but for most serious applications, something is more appropriate.

## CD-R and CD-RW

A CD-ROM is a Once/Read Many (WORM) storage medium in which micro-scopic pips are inscribed on the aluminum layer of a plastic disc to represent data. Once inscribed, the tracks formed by the pits are read optically by a noncontact head, which scans in a radial pattern as the disc spins just above it. The head moves at a constant linear velocity, assuring a constant data rate. When the speed is constant, the disc must rotate at a decreasing rate as the data track is scanned from its beginning the center of the disc to its near the outer edge of the disc. (Many modern CD-ROM drives don't use a constant linear velocity, though, and so variable data transfer rates from different parts of the disc)

A CD writer (CD-R or CD Recordable) is simply a CD writer with a reader strong enough to burn optical equivalents of CD-ROM pits into a special CD-R disc that's coated with an optical dye. Some of these discs are intended to be multisession, meaning you can write some portion of the disc and then filling the multisession at a later time, while others are written to and cannot be supplemented later. On a CD-RW (CD-Rewritable), the laser can only write data, it can erase the disc, leaving a clean surface as if the CD-RW hadn't been used. In any of the forms, these discs are quite vulnerable to scratching and heat damage.

Writable CD-ROMs are becoming quite popular for backups as well. CD-RW discs only work in players equipped to recognize them. There is an increase in cost for the CD-RW over the CD-R discs. For the price of 50-pack of CD-R discs with jewel cases starts at around $19. The price for 50 CD-RW discs with jewel cases around $28 and go up as the discs are certified at higher speeds. CD-ROMs hold approximately 650MB of data, so you'll probably need to do a full backup.

CD-R and CD-RW are relatively low-cost options for backups. Each disc will only hold up to 650MB of data, and since hard-disk capacities are always going to exceed that, you are stuck changing discs when one gets full or buying a CD changer in addition to the writer and the media. This eliminates any real cost savings over tape. We prefer to use tape or some other high-capacity option.

## Media Storage

Media storage is important, too. Magnetic media are vulnerable to heat, moisture, and electrical proximity and have a tendency to degrade over time (just as VCR tapes do). Magneto-optical drives, on the other hand, makes the medium more resistant to heat or electrical damage, so storage requirements are less restrictive. Pure optical technologies (CD-R and CD-RW) are the most likely to be archival quality. CD-R storage lifetimes are estimated at 10-100 years, depending on dye type and who's estimating. Certain locations that are characteristically damp, excessively dry, or very hot would pose a hazard to any storage media and should be avoided when planning media.

## Hierarchical Storage Management

In many situations, the media are stored on-site. Unlike as it sounds, if the building burned down, both the computers and the backups could be destroyed. Think of the World Trade Center bombing for you an example. If it is possible, you should store at some portion of the backups in a different location. One option would be to store the monthly and incremental backups on site but store the annual backups off-site. This doesn't give you 100 recoverability, but it is a step in the right direction. Another option is to store weeks 1 and 2 off-site, weeks 3 and 4 off-site, and then to swap every second week. Look at the risks and the cost in employee hours to rebuild what would be lost, and make a smart decision about where to store the backups.
