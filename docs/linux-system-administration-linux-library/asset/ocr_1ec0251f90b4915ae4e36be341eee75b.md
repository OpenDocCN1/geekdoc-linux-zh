# Trojan Horse

You probably remember the story of the Trojan horse from Homer's Iliad. The Greeks sent a giant wooden horse as a present to end their ten-year war with Troy. When it was within the gates of Troy, Greek soldiers emerged from inside and defeated the unsuspecting Trojans.

In security, then, a Trojan horse is a program or a script that is hidden inside an authorized program. This code, when executed, causes an undesirable effect. Many of these are aimed at Microsoft softwares, targeting their mailers. For example, you may remember the “Love Bug” horse from May of 2000. It appeared to be an e-mail letter but, when opened, would cause problems, such as sending itself to your email address book, erasing your files, and downloading another Trojan horse program that would steal your passwords. This particular Trojan horse affected only Windows machines, while Trojan horses are rare on Linux machines, they are not unheard of. In January of 1999, a Trojan horse was found in the util-linux-2.9.gz on some Web sites. This type of attack is difficult to protect against. As a result, Red Hat and many other distributions have begun adding checksums or Pretty Good Privacy (PGP) signatures to the packages they make available for download. Make use of these verification techniques if they are available to you.

# Pretty Good Privacy (PGP)

Pretty Good Privacy is a software package that provides both asymmetric encryption, which is used for e-mail, and symmetric encryption for other files. PGP is generally used to ensure that the e-mail you receive is from its intended author. Mail sent through the Internet can be intercepted, changed, or created as if it were from someone else entirely. With PGP, you can digitally sign your e-mail, generating a mathematical value based on your e-mail's content as encrypted using your private key. If the recipient of e-mail has your public key, the PGP software makes the same calculation, determining that the message has not been altered. Since you only have the private key that encrypted the hash value that was successfully decrypted with your private key, only you could have signed the e-mail. Packages made available for download may be signed the same way.

The difficulty comes with obtaining the public key in such a way as to be sure that the person who claims the key is actually the one who generated it. The most secure way is to meet face to face with the person, exchanging identification and public keys at the same time. Retrieving a signed software package from a Web site is not as secure.
