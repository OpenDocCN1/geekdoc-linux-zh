+   Put the most-used data in the center. Heavily accessed partitions should go in the middle of a range of partitions. Swap partitions typically see a lot of use, so they should be positioned between nonswap partitions.

+   Split OSs across disks. If you have two or more operating systems, you may be tempted to put one OS on one disk and another on a second disk. If you split OSs across both disks, however, you'll achieve slightly better performance because two sets of disk heads are working in both OSs.

+   Isolate critical data. Consider putting particularly important or used-data on partitions from other files. This can reduce the risk of damage to those data a disk error occurs in another part of the disk. Similarly, if a heavily used partition runs out of disk space, the problems can be isolated./home and and some servers, /var and /tmp, on their own partitions are typical examples of this rule thumb.

+   Put the Linux kernel under the disk's 1024-cylinder mark. Until recently, the Linux Loader (LILO) couldn't boot the Linux kernel if it resided in an area past the 102th cylinder. One way around this limitation is to create a small (5–20MB) cylinder under that point and mount it as /boot. You can then store the Linux kernel in that partition and put the rest of the system elsewhere, even past the 1024-cylinder point. The latest versions of LILO (including the version that ships with Red Hat 7.0) don't suffer from this problem, but as of early 2001, it's best to be safe in this respect.

> The 1024-cylinder mark is the point where the 1024th cylinder of the disk, as reported by an EIDE or SCSI host adapter, lies. On modern hard disks, this point works out to just under 8GB, but it can be much lower (mostly older systems). This arrangement is important because the x86 BIOS, and hence a wide range of software that relies upon the BIOS, couldn't read beyond 1024 cylinders. This limit has been lifted in modern BIOSes, but it's taken a while for the chain of utilities that use the BIOS (including LILO) to take advantage of the improved support for larger disks.

You shouldn't take any of these rules as absolute. Indeed, they sometimes contradict one another. For instance, the data-isolation rule is at odds with the keep-it-simple rule. In the end, you'll need to decide which rules reflect your own preferences and needs for the system, and create a partitioning scheme that reflects these factors. For new Linux administrators, we typically recommend a root (/) partition, a /home partition, a swap partition, possibly a /boot partition, and whatever partitions are necessary to support any other OSs that exist on the computer.
