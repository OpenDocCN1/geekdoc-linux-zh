Dave's favorite experience was sending Babbage, the teddy bear mascot of the Raspberry PI, up to 39 kilometers and releasing it for freefall. “The first attempt failed due to a combination of factors — a windy launch meant Babbage's support line wasn't in the right place around the heated resistor to cut him free, and for weight reasons I'd been cautious using a smaller and less powerful battery pack than was ideal,” says Dave. Success is always sweeter after an initial failure, and it was a lovely moment when I knew he'd been released properly on the second flight.

Dave's early projects involved hand-building systems for tracking flights using GPS and for transmitting signals, photos, and other data to the ground. The PI in the Sky kit is now available to provide these offers off the shelf. You can also use the kit in combination with the Sense HAT (Chapter 19) to send sensor data back to the ground. Among other things, Dave's used the PI to predict the landing position on the way down. Part of the challenge of high-altitude ballooning is recovering the payload, given predictions that be off by 5 miles or more.

High-altitude ballooning is a satisfying hobby, but it's one that requires planning and commitment. You will most likely need permission for each flight that you don't endanger aircraft, and you'll need to budget several hundred dollars or pounds for the equipment, and a couple hundred for the balloon, gas and fuel for the chase vehicle on each flight. Plenty of help is available, to help you make a success of it: Start reading Dave's tutorial at www.daveaker.com/?p=1732, join the #highaltitude channel at https://webchat.freenode.net, and visit the UK High Altitude society website www.ukhas.org.uk.

You might have heard of the Mechanical Turk, an elaborate illusion that was claimed to be a chess-playing table, from 1770 to 1854. In fact, a chess master was hidden inside the table, making the moves and controlling the dummy of a chess in Turkish dress who was playing the game. The Mechanical Turk went on to give its name to a service from Amazon where people complete tasks over the Internet, and now to Raspberry Turk, a genuine chess-playing robot.

Raspberry Turk is built around the open source Stockfish chess engine, which plays the games. A Raspberry Pi is used to control a robot arm that moves the chess pieces.
