## Making sound samples

The samples can be recorded on a laptop and moved over to the Pi by USB memory stick. However, with a bit of work, it's possible to do the whole thing on the Pi itself. The Raspberry Pi can record sound, but it has no built-in interface, so you have to provide one in the form of a USB card. You can purchase one for less than $10 (about £6) and you can find others listed at http://linux.org/RPI_VerifiedPeripherals USB_Sound_Cards.

It's best to look at the installation details for the specific card you use. After it's plugged in and connected to a microphone, go to the Desktop menu and choose the Preferences submenu, and then select Audio Devices. From the drop-down menu after, choose your USB card.

Next, you need some software to record the sound. By far, the best free software to do this is Audacity. To install it on your Pi, go to a terminal and enter the following:

```
sudo apt-get update
sudo apt-get install audacity
```

After Audacity installs, it's available from the Desktop menu, under the Sound & Video entry. The screen is shown in Figure 18-6.

> You can find versions of Audacity for the Macintosh and Windows operating systems as well.

Audacity can not only record but also edit it — and even add effects. The controls are just like a tape recorder, with a round, red Record button and a square Stop button. If you've never used it, have a play with first: Just click the Record button and say something (you'll see the waveform plotted), and then press the Stop button, press Rewind, and press the triangular Play button.

Whenever you are making a recording, you need to "top and tail it"—that is to say, remove the silences at the start and end. In Audacity, using the Magnifying Glass icon to enlarge the waveform, and then drag over the opening silence to highlight it. Then you remove it by simply pressing the Delete key on the keyboard. Do the same for the end silence. With that out of the way, you then need to make sure that a sample begins and ends on the zero line, running through the middle of the displayed waveform. If it does not, you will hear clicks when you play it back. To ensure that it does begin and end on the zero line, highlight a small section at the start of the sample, and select Fade In from the Effects menu. Do the same for the end of the sample, but this time select Fade Out. Finally, select the whole sample and select Normalize from the Effects menu. With the top two options checked, select OK. This last step ensures that all samples have the same overall sound level.

To save your sample, you can choose `File: Export Audio` from the main menu, and then choose Ogg Vorbis File from the drop-down menu in the lower right. Type in your filename with a `.ogg` extension and select Save. The .ogg format is Python’s native sound format and is best supported. Close the Audacity window and do not bother with the Save Changes options.
