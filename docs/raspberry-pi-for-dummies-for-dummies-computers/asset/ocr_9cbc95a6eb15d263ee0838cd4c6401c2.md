However, this doesn't take into account that the timing of a press can be very accurate and all six numbers are cycled through thousands of times a second. Controlling a button press with microsecond accuracy is simply not possible for slow humans, but it’s a good source of random numbers. Therefore, in our project, the number is generated by counting 1 to 6 while a push button is held down.

When a push button is first pushed, the contacts come together and bounce apart and then come together in a rapid sequence of contact make-and-break, rather like a ping-pong ball falling onto a table. This happens on all mechanical switches, but some designs are worse than others. A lot of the time in programming, this isn’t important because other stuff happens after a contact is first made. Other times, it could be a problem, but the solution can be quite simple—just delay for a few milliseconds after the first contact before looking at the input again. This strategy—known debouncing an input—is how we show you how to do in project.

# The display

Once you have generated a number, it must be displayed in the pattern of a conventional device. This involves turning on a specially selected pattern of LEDs. This pattern is, of course, for each number. Now, you could use a series of statements to test for each number, and when you find a match, write a series of statements to turn on and off the appropriate LEDs.
