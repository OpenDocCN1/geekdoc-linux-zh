When scientists were first discovering electricity, they knew that it flowed from one terminal to the other. They said the flow was from the positive to the negative, but which was which? Experiments with making a current through a solution of water and copper sulphate showed that copper was dissolved from one wire and deposited on the other. So they reasonably assumed that the metal was flowing with the flow of electricity and the dissolving wire an anode and positive and the wire receiving the metal cathode or negative. They were wrong: the electrons that constitute the current actually flow the other way. However, this notion became so entrenched that we still use it. We call it conventional current, and it flows from the positive to the negative.

In a way, it doesn't matter which direction we think of it as flowing. It's the fact that it is flowing and is important, and we use the terms positive and negative so we know what way round it is flowing. A common mistake beginners make in thinking about electricity is to think that the direction of flow matters “it first flows through one component and then another.” This leads to the erroneous thought that the first component it meets will “use up” or some of the electricity and then path on what is left to connect it the next. This is simply wrong, because current flows through all components in any path. You might also hear the phrase “electricity finds the path of least resistance and flows through that.” Again this is nonsense. Electricity flows through all paths at the same time — it is just that the resistance of a path determines how much current flows through that path.

Power sources, like batteries and power supplies, are all marked with positive and negative symbols so that you can connect it the correct way. This is known as direct current (DC) which carries the current flows in one direction.

The other sort of power supply you can get drives the current in one direction for a short length of time and then reverses the direction for a short time. This is known as alternating current (AC). A favorite trick electricians play on their apprentices is to send them to the store to fetch the nonexistent AC battery.

Switches are used to make or break circuits, an early name for a switch was a breaker.
