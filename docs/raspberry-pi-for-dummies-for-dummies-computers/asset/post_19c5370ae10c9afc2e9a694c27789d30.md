The RFID card (short for Radio Frequency Identification card) is becoming ubiquitous. It’s being used these days for not only building–access control but also travel cards, prepayments at cafeterias, and even antifreeze protection tickets. They can also be used in amusement park wristbands, and even blood donor cards. When your pet is chipped, a small glass-encapsulated RFID tag is injected under the skin at the neck of the animal.

Given all the things that can be done with RFID, you might think they are complex devices, and you would be right. However, if you pack away the complexity in a very smart chip and hide the interaction with that chip in a class driver, actually using them in a project is remarkably easy. There are lots of fun things you can do with RFID cards, and we’re going to show you a few of them in this chapter.

## How RFID Work

There are basically three different types of RFID systems available on the market, mainly distinguished by which frequency range they use. All systems consist of two parts: a tag or a card and a reader. The reader extracts binary bits from a tag or card using radio waves, so no wires are needed between the reader and the tag or card. These tags normally known as passive tags — tags that require no source of power — although a number active tags that require fitting with a small battery. These active tags are used when you need a much longer read range.

The reader sends out a radio signal, and the tag picks it up and uses the power in that radio signal to activate a microchip. Microchip sends back a number of pulses to the reader, which is interpreted as a number. The way the tag sends the data back is different on different types of tag, but the main way is transmitting the data back to the reader on a different frequency.

Here's a list of active tags, with their frequency ranges:

- 125-13 kHz tags: These are the ones used in chipping pets and also in a lot of access control systems. They have a limited storage capacity and are normally restricted to holding a small number of 64 or 128 bits. The tags and cards used most widely today conform to the EM4100 / EM4200 standard. Most of the tags are read-only, but a few (The Hitag tag, for example) allow you to store data using a special programmer.

- 13.56 MHz tags: These are the so-called smart cards, capable of storing not a serial number but also data that can be read or written. There is a measure of security built into the cards so that the data is inaccessible to those who do not know the encryption key. There are many different types of cards, and by far the most common is the one known as MIFARE classic card.

- UHF (860-960MHz): Unlike the other two RFID types, UHF readers are capable of reading more than one tag at the same time. They are also quite long-range, typically 10 to 30 feet. They're designed for bulk inventory taking. Each item on a palette be recorded and counted at the same time. The readers have a very high-power transmitter in them, so much so that they're a hazard, requiring strict time limits for workers operating them in order to avoid long-term radiation exposure. There are other, higher-frequency systems in this class as well.

We use the MIFARE classic card in the projects in this chapter — to give it Sunday name, the ISO/IEC 14443 A/MIFARE mode protocol. The readers are cheap, and the tags come in various shapes ranging from key fobs to cards. The cards that are most convenient for these projects, but it's not essential to use these.

## MIFARE card's structure

A MIFARE card consists of 64 blocks of data, with each block 16 bytes long. Some blocks are for user data, and others hold authentication keys, UID numbers, and manufacturers' ID numbers (A UID, or Unique Identification number, is 4 bytes long. In fact, despite its name, a UID might not be unique, but finding a duplicate is less likely than winning a big lottery. So, for all practical considerations, it can be considered unique.)
