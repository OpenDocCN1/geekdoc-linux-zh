The great thing about these shapes is that they're easy to set up. Each strip has a plug and socket on it, for input and output, thus enabling us to easily chain together as many strips as you want. The triangle is our favorite — use two or three to make a 3D prism. In addition to working with your Raspberry Pi, they can be used on a number of other controllers.

# LED strips

Another popular for getting lots of these LEDs already wired together is to get them on a flexible, printed circuit board. These come in various lengths, from half a meter to 4 meters. Be sure to get the APA102 and not WS2812 type, to ensure that they work with all in the book. You can get 30, 60, or 144 LEDs per meter — as you'd expect, the cost is related to the number of LEDs. These strips also come in a variety of options — black or white backing, coated in silicon, or in a waterproof silicon tube. You can easily cut these strips into lengths, if need, with a sharp blade.

In addition to the RGB light, the APA102 comes in a white-only LED variant. These are good for domestic lighting and can be dimmed easily.

# LED matrix

One form to get these LEDs is in an LED matrix. Adafruit sells a number of configurations — the biggest (and most expensive) model is a 240mm rigged disk. If that's not what you want, you can get an 8x32 grid, 16x16 or 8x8 grid, all on a flexible board. However, our favorite is a high-density 8 x8 grid, on a ridged board. Its main feature is, that instead of using the 5 x5mm standard LED matrix, to use a 1.8x1.8mm package. This means the whole grid can be fitted into an display inch square. This tiny LEDs only take 40mA when fully on, so they take less than the normal-size LEDs. However, this also adds to a maximum of 2.5A. At that amount of current, the board will get quite warm as well. Nevertheless, it can be bright, so keep the brightness down. You will need to do a bit of soldering to connect the wires and the supplied capacitor to the back of the board, but these are all marked. We also wired the 5V and ground output to the 5V and ground input, to get a better power distribution on the board. This is shown in Figure 17-1.

Such a small matrix has many applications, one of which is to make a smart reactive board. We thought we would like to have a go at this, and so we come with some colorful moving–display patterns.
