
## Current limits

Current is the first and biggest limitation when trying to drive LEDs. Sure, one LED only draws a small amount of current, but any small number when multiplied by a big number starts to be important. Suppose that you're running your Raspberry Pi off a 2A power supply—roughly twice the peak current that the Pi needs. You should never keep the maximum rated current at about 80 percent of rated current. With a 2A power supply, that ends up to 1.6A. It is therefore reasonable to say that you have a maximum of 0.6A, or 600mA (milliamps) of current in a typical setup. If each LED draws 60mA, that is 20mA for each color. With that setup, you can drive a maximum of 10 LEDs with a white color at pull.

Now, APA102C are very bright and overpowering at close range. Luckily, there are two ways to control the brightness, and if it's controlled, you use current and can drive more LEDs. The first way is to scale down the PWM values, instead of using a value of 255 for a red, you'd simply use 128 and that will use the full color.

A better way of controlling the brightness of the APA102C is by using the brightness control individually with the PWM values. So, we have shown you how to set the brightness at a fixed level and then ignored it—this is a good strategy because it means you have a great deal of protection from programming errors. The brightness control actually limits the current through the LEDs and is controllable in 33 steps. Full current is given by a brightness of 31, and other values give the number of 31ths of full current. So, if a single LED takes a maximum of 60mA, then with a brightness of 8, it will only take a maximum of 15.48mA. So the spare 600mA can drive 38 LEDs. Even, a brightness of 8 is very bright when looking directly at an LED. A brightness of 2 pushes up to number 155 LEDs and still looks good.

Anything over 155 LEDs and you will have to resort to an external power supply. It's quite easy to use: Just supply the 5V and the LEDs and make sure the ground of the power supply is connected to the ground of the Raspberry Pi.

## Signals and memory

The other limitations you have to deal with concern signals and memory. Fortunately for you, there's no shortage of memory on the Raspberry Pi, unlike other embedded controllers boards, like the Arduino. There is cause for concern, however, when it comes to signals. The Blinkt! board used earlier in this chapter connected the GPIO signals directly into the chain of LEDs. The problem is that the LEDs, because they're powered with 5V, not a 5V logic-level signal to drive them. It just so happens that the LED's specifications state that the minimum level sufficient to be a Logic One is 0.7 times the voltage. For a 5V supply voltage, this means a logic high of 3.5V, which is just over the 3.3 volts you get from a GPIO pin. It turns out it seems to work despite this, the problem is that it is not guaranteed to work in all conditions and temperatures. That means a driver circuit is often needed to boost the clock and data signals to 5V. This becomes more important the further away the first LED is from the Pi and the more interference there is in the local environment from things like motors or fluorescent light fittings. ## Display update

Finally, you have to deal with the update question, or how fast the patterns shown by the LEDs can be changed. Using the bit-banging technique we described earlier in this chapter, it can take between 0.45 and 0.23ms to send out the data to a single LED. This time difference occurs because Linux steals time from any running program, and so the time for a refresh depends on what the operating system is doing at the moment. If this interruption occurs in crucial time when the program is bit-banging the data out to the LED, it can cause flickering.

## LED strips

Another popular way to get these LEDs is on a flexible, printed circuit. These come in various lengths, from half a meter to 4 meters. In addition to the RGB light, the APA102 comes in a white-only variant. These are good for domestic lighting and can be dimmed easily.

## LED matrix

One form to get these LEDs is in an LED matrix. Adafruit sells a number of configurations — the biggest (and most expensive) model is a 240mm disk. If that's not what you want, you can get an 8x32 grid, 16x16 or 8x8 grid, all on a flexible board. However, our favorite is a high-density 8x8 grid, on a ridged board.
