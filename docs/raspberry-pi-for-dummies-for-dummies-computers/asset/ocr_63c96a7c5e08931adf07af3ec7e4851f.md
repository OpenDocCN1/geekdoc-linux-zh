The diagram is quite similar to what we have shown in this chapter, except that’s arranged differently. First, there are two push buttons for the traffic sensor and cross request. Next are five LEDs connected to resistors and GPIO pins. Note here, though, you need different colours of LED — two red, two green, and a yellow. Finally, there’s the sounder. We are using a piezo sounder, but a word of warning is in order here: There are two types of piezo sounders, and these to be universally accepted what to differentiate between the two. The sort you want is one that generates sound when you apply a voltage to it. These could be called self-drive sounders, but sometimes they are red. Other times, they can be referred to as buzzers, but don’t confuse them with electromagnetic buzzers, which take a lot of current — too much for the Pi. A suitable one would work from 3V and less than 10mA. The type you want is the one where you have to supply an electric pulse to it before it makes a noise. This type is called a speaker-type sounder. A simple test is to use the 3V output and ground of the Pi and then make the contact. If it only clicks, it’s a speaker type. If it makes a sound, it’s the type you want to use.(To be honest, in development, we replaced the sounder with an LED and resistor, to reduce the annoying beeps.)

The layout of this circuit on a breadboard is shown in Figure 16-15. We have arranged the traffic–control LEDs like traffic lights, and the cross indicators above each other like you would want on a real-life crossing.
