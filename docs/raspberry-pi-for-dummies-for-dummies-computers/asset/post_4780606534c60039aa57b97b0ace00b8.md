## Chapter 1: Introducing the Raspberry Pi

The Raspberry Pi is perhaps the most inspiring computer available today. Although most of the computing devices being used (including phones, tablets, and game consoles) are designed to stop people from tinkering with it, the Raspberry Pi is exactly the opposite. From the moment you see its shiny green circuit board, it invites you to prod it, play with it, and create with it. It comes with the tools you need to start creating your own software (or programming), and you can connect your electronic inventions to it. If you break it, it won't break the bank, so you can experiment with confidence.

Lots of people are fired up by its potential, and they are discovering exciting new ways to use. Dave Ackerman (www.daveakerman.com) and friends attached one to a weather balloon and sent it nearly 40 kilometers high to take pictures of the Earth from near space using a webcam. (You can read about Dave's ballooning project in Chapter 20.)

Professor Simon Cox and his team at the University of Southampton connected 64 Raspberry Pi boards to build an experimental supercomputer, held together by Lego bricks. In the supercomputer (see Figure 1-1), the Raspberry Pis work together to solve a single problem. The project has been able to cut the cost of supercomputer from millions of dollars to thousands or even hundreds of dollars, making supercomputing much more accessible to schools and students. Others have also experimented with combining the process of multiple Pis. There's even an off-the-shelf kit you can use to combine four Raspberry Pi Zeros with a full-size Raspberry Pi (the Cluster HAT from Pimoroni) so that you can experiment with running programs across multiple Pis at the same time.

The Pi is also being used to make weather stations, fitness gadgets, devices, audiobook players, electric skateboards, and much more, as we discover in Chapter 20.

Although those projects are grabbing headlines, another story is less visible but more important: the thousands of people of all ages who are taking their first steps in computer science, thanks to the Raspberry Pi.

Both of the authors of this book used computers in the 1980s, when the notion of a home computer became a reality. Back then, computers were less friendly than they are today. When you switched them on, you were faced with a flashing cursor and had to do something in to get it to do anything. As a result, though, a whole generation grew up knowing at least a bit about how to give the computer commands, and how to create programs for it. As computers started to use mice and windows, people didn't need those skills any more, and they lost touch with them.

Eben Upton, designer of the Raspberry Pi, noticed the slide in skill levels when he was working at Cambridge University’s computer laboratory in 2006. Students applying to study computer science started to have less experience with programming than students of the past. Upton and his colleagues hatched the idea of creating a computer that would come supplied with all the tools to program it — and would sell for a target price of $25 (about £20). It had to be able to do other interesting things, so that people were drawn to use it, and it had to be robust enough to survive being pushed in and out of schools hundreds of times.

That started a six-year journey that led to the Raspberry Pi you have on your desk as you read this book. It was released in February 2012, and sold half a million units by the end of the quarter. By July 2017, there were more than 14 million Raspberry Pis in homes. It is, by large margin, the best-selling British computer of all time.

## Getting Familiar with the Raspberry Pi

Over the years, the Raspberry Pi has evolved, increasing its memory, improving its performance, and adding features. So which one should you get? Here's an overview to help you decide:

- **Raspberry Pi 3 Model B**: The third generation of the Raspberry Pi, it is 60 percent faster than the previous model and offers the best performance on the desktop. It includes 1GB of memory, four USB ports, built-in Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, and an Ethernet port for a wired connection. It has 40 GPIO pins, which you can use to your own projects. Like previous models, it's about the size of a credit card. If your budget allows, get this one. Its price is around $35 (about £30).

- **Model A**: cut-down model, it is ideal for projects with lower power consumption — typically battery-based. It is suitable for robots and projects in remote locations, where a wired electricity supply isnt. It has one USB port, although you can connect it to a USB hub to use more devices simultaneously. It has the full complement of 40 GPIO pins, so you would find that your projects work with it. Like the Model B, it includes an audio output (headphones-style) socket. This has 512MB of memory and a price of $20 (or £20). The Model A+ is slightly shorter on the long side than the Raspberry Pi 3.

- **Raspberry Pi Zero**: The Raspberry Pi Foundation astonished everyone when it gave this away with the print edition of its magazine The MagPi. The Raspberry Pi Zero measures 6.5 centimeters by 3 centimeters, extremely lightweight, and has 512MB of memory and one Micro USB port. If you want to use the GPIO, you'll need to solder or affix your own pins, available separately. (You can read all about GPIO pins on the Pi Zero in Chapter 16.) You'll also need a connector for the Mini HDMI socket, and for the Micro USB socket, so you should expect to spend a bit more than the price of the Pi (and have a bit more complexity in your setup). Built as the $5 computer, the Raspberry Pi Zero has at times been difficult to get hold of, which is perhaps not surprising given the phenomenal demand for it.
