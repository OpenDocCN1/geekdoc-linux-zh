Note that RAID arrays and mirroring systems will just as happily write corrupt data as valid data. Moreover, if a file is deleted, a RAID array won't save it. RAID arrays are best suited for protecting the current state of a running system, not for backup needs.

# Making the Choice

Only you can decide what is best for your situation. After reading about the backup options in this book, put together some sample backup plans; run through a few likely scenarios and assess the effectiveness of your choice.

In addition to all the other information you've learned about backup strategies, here are a couple of good rules of thumb to remember when making your choice:

+   - If the backup strategy and policy is too complicated (and this holds true for most security issues), it will eventually be disregarded and fall into disuse.

+   - The best scheme is often a combination of strategies.

# Choosing Backup Hardware and Media

Any device that can store data can be used to back it up, but that's like saying that anything with wheels you can take on a cross-country trip. Trying to fit a megabyte worth data on a stack of 1.4MB floppy disks is an exercise in frustration, and using a $5,000 automated tape device to save a single copy of an email is a waste of resources.

Many people use what hardware already have for their backup operations. Many consumer grade workstations have a floppy drive and provide a CD-RW drive, but they typically don't have the abundant free disk space necessary for performing and storing multiple backups.

In this section, you will learn about some of the most common hardware available and how to evaluate its appropriateness for your backups needs. With large storage devices becoming affordable (160GB IDE drives can be had for around $300) and prices falling on DVD recorders, decisions about backup hardware for the small business and home users become more interesting.
