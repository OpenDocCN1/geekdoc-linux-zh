# Sound and Music

Linux has a reputation of lacking good support for sound and multimedia applications in general. Certainly, there is less sound card support than in Microsoft Windows. (However, Microsoft no longer supports the Microsoft Sound Card, but Linux users still enjoy support for it, no doubt to tweak the folks in Redmond.) Unix has always had good multimedia support as David Taylor, UNIX author and guru, points out:

> The original graphics work for computers was done by Evans & Sutherland on UNIX systems. The innovations at MIT's Media Lab were done on UNIX workstations. In 1985, we at HP Labs were creating sophisticated multimedia work environments on UNIX workstations, so Unix is never less multimedia than suggested. Limitations in Linux support doesn't mean UNIX has the same limitations. I think it was a matter of logistics, with hundreds of sound cards and thousands of possible PC configurations.

That last sentence sums it quite well. Unix has a limited range of hardware to support; Linux has hundreds of sound cards. Sound card device driver support has been long lacking from manufacturers, and there's still no single standard for the sound subsystem in Linux. The dominant system, OSS (Open Source Sound) is a combination of freedom and proprietary drivers. A new system, the Advanced Linux Sound Architecture (ALSA), is still under development.

In this section, you learn about sound cards, sound file formats, and the sound applications provided by Red Hat.

# Sound Cards

Red Hat Linux 8.0 supports a wide variety of sound hardware and software. The sound card is one of the most critical sound components in any PC, and two models compete for prominence in today's market:

- OSS, the Open Sound System, which offers free and commercial drivers
- ALSA, the Advanced Linux Sound Architecture, which is entirely open source

Red Hat uses OSS because OSS is the sound architecture for the 2.4 series kernels (the 2.6 series is likely to use ALSA). Red Hat supports a long list of sound cards. You can review the list at http://www.redhat.com/support/hardware/. If your sound card isn't supported in the free version, it might be supported in the commercial version.

# Recording Sound

Red Hat Linux provides a number of tools that enable you to control volume, mix, and other sound recording functions. Kmix sound mixer acts as a virtual mixing board, taking sound inputs from different sources, enabling you to adjust their individual volumes, mute them, and listen to them through your sound card.

If you need to control sound from the command line, use the amix command, which can be used to adjust balance or volume using command-line options. Your main sound channel’s volume can be controlled using one option, -v, followed by a number between 0 (mute) and 100 (make your roommate/neighbor scream). For example, to set your volume at 50 percent, use amix like so:

```
$ amix -v 50
```

The Sound Recorder is provided to enable you to play and record .wav files. You can use the SoX utility (covered in this chapter) to convert the recorded sound if necessary.

The Volume Control utility is similar in function to Kmix; open both both and observe how they interact. Each provides control in slightly different way, giving you a choice of controls.
