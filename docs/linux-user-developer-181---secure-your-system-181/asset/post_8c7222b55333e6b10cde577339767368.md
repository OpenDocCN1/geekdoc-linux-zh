## Fractal geometry

Fractal geometry is a relatively new branch of maths which can trace its origins back to the 1960s, when mathematician Benoît Mandelbrot posed the question "How long is the coast of Britain?" It transpires that the answer to the question depends on the length of the ruler you use to measure it – the shorter the ruler, the longer the coastline. This is because a coastline, like lots of natural forms, has ever more detail at smaller scales. The mathematical term for this is self-similarity at multiple scales and is a simple definition of a fractal. With real-world objects, this self-similarity runs out of steam at the molecular and atomic levels, but a true mathematical fractal exhibits either exact or approximate self-similarity at all levels. In other words, it goes on forever as you continue to zoom in on it.

The first fractals were devised before the term fractal geometry was coined and were only recognised as such when mathematicians turned their attention to fractals in the 1960s and 1970s. A classic example is the Sierpinski gasket, a type of fractal that is referred to as an iterated function system (IFS) fractal. It’s easily generated with just a few lines of code. Start off with a solid (ie, filled in) equilateral triangle. Now, by joining the centre points of the three edges, divide it into four smaller equilateral triangles and remove the centre one. Next, do the same with the three remaining solid triangles, dividing them each into four smaller triangles and removing the centre one. Continue in this way until you get an effect you find pleasing or until you get bored, but bear in mind that to get a true Sierpinski gasket you’d have to continue forever.

Moving on from the Sierpinski gasket, you might like to delve into other IFS fractals including the Sierpinski carpet, the Sierpinski pentagon, the Koch snowflake and the dragon curve; you’ll find no shortage of information online. And if you fancy some 3D solid modelling, there are even three-dimensional versions of some of these fractals, including the Sierpinski pyramid and the Menger sponge.

Alternatively, there’s plenty of free and sometimes open source Linux software that can generate these and other IFS fractals. A good starting point would be the IFS Fractal plug-in for the free GIMP image manipulation package (https://www.gimp.org), which you’ll find in the image window menu under Filters > Render > Nature > IFS Fractal.

In addition to generating very geometrical-looking IFS fractals, this software also lets you experiment with less regular fractals which, although rigorously defined, can produce apparently natural forms such as trees and plants.

While IFS Fractals might make attractive images and have the appeal of the infinite, it’s not exactly surprising that they are self-similar at all levels of magnification given the way they are generated. However, as we turn our attention to the escape-time fractal, although we repeat some mathematical function over and over again, it’s not at all obvious that this process will generate genuine fractals. What’s more, this process produces fractals that are infinite in their nature but which have a degree of unpredictability as you zoom in on them.

The most famous escape-time fractal is surely the Mandelbrot set – let’s see how it’s generated. Generating the Mandelbrot set involves working with complex numbers. As a quick reminder, these are numbers that have a real and an imaginary part; for example, 2 + 0.5i where i is the square root of minus 1. If this is still a mystery to you, we’ll have to leave you to read up on the subject, but it’s really not as difficult as you might fear.

## Picturing the Mandelbrot set

To generate an image of the Mandelbrot set, we need to carry out a test on all complex numbers (actually, you only need to consider points between -2.0 and +0.5 on the real axis and between -1.2i and +1.2i on the imaginary axis), marking the results on the complex plane. The test is simple: Start with zero, square it, and add the complex number being tested. If the result has a modulus of two or more (the modulus is the distance from the zero point on the complex plane), then the point isn’t in the Mandelbrot set. If it’s less than two, do the same again; that is, take that result, square it and add the value being tested. Again, if the result has a modulus of two or more, it isn’t in the Mandelbrot set; but if it’s less than two, carry on the calculation again and continue in this way. Some points, those that aren’t in the Mandelbrot set, soon exceed a modulus of two and head off towards infinity. Other points, those that are in the Mandelbrot set, remain close to zero however many times you repeat this operation (in practice, though, an arbitrary number of iterations, commonly 20, is used).

As you zoom in on areas near the perimeter of the Mandelbrot set, as we did to create the other pictures in **Figure 1**, more and more detail comes to light and the infinite intricacy and zigzag patterns unlike IFS fractals, you'll never see exactly the same patterns at ever greater zoom levels, but you see the same sorts of star bursts, spirals and zigzag patterns and a few times, there's a good chance that you're seeing something that nobody has ever seen before.

Writing code to draw the Mandelbrot set is simple enough, although providing a user interface to allow zooming is a bit trickier. In fact, while zooming would have to be achieved just by filling in cells to define an area of the complex plane, it's an easy job to produce an Excel spreadsheet with a simple macro to do the job. While writing your own software would be an interesting and educational exercise, if you just want to generate impressive images of the Mandelbrot set, you'd be better off getting your hands on some software. One of the most respected Linux packages is called Gnofract4d <http://edyoung.github.io/gnofract4d/> and it's free. As well as the Mandelbrot set, it allows you to explore the closely related Julia set; plus there's the option to define your own formulae, which provides the opportunity to investigate any imaginable escape-time fractal. And if you want to explore 3D fractals (both IFS and escape-time), the free Mandelbulber package (<http://www.mandelbulber.com>) is what you need.

## Chaos theory

Chaos theory concerns systems that are described as having super-sensitivity to initial conditions – let's see what this means in practice. A very simple mathematical model of the growth of a biological species such as a fruit fly is represented by the formula $\mathbf{x}^{\text{t+1}}=a\ \mathbf{x}^{\text{t}}(1-\mathbf{x}^{\text{t}})$, where $\mathbf{x}^{\text{t}}$ is the population in the current generation, $\mathbf{x}^{\text{t+1}}$ is the population in the next generation, and $a$ is a growth rate. The population is what mathematicians call 'normalised to 1', so all values will be between 0 and 1, but it can always be converted to a real-world population by multiplying by a constant. - **Fractal landscapes**
Because so many natural forms – including mountains, coastlines, clouds and trees – have fractal properties, this enables the production of computer-generated images that look realistic while requiring only a small amount of data. This technique has been used in film production, e.g. when an alien-looking sci-fi landscape is required, and it's commonplace in games production.
