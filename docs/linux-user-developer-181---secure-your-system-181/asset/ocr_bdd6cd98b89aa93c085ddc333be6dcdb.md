# MEPs push for EU-wide backdoor ban

Can European Union legislation protect encryption?

Combating crime and terrorism has long been the rationale presented by governments to increase surveillance powers. But when it comes to encryption, there is a problem: encryption works or it does not. There are no half measures.

It’s an issue that is on the mind of a group of MEPs, who have proposed amended regulation to the European Parliament that forbids encryption backdoors for the purposes of law enforcement. In its draft report, the Committee on Civil Liberties, Justice and Home Affairs says that advances in technology have outpaced data protection provisions, resulting in the proposed amendments to the 2002 ePrivacy regulations. If passed, these would impact the UK’s intention to block end-to-end encryption (E2EE) from being used by apps such as WhatsApp.

The draft report states: “The providers of electronic communications services shall ensure that there is sufficient protection in place against unauthorised access or alterations to the electronic communications data, and that the confidentiality and safety of the transmission are also guaranteed by the nature of the means of transmission used, or by state-of-the-art end-to-end encryption of the electronic communications data. Furthermore, when encryption of electronic communications data is used, decryption, reverse engineering or monitoring of such communications shall be prohibited.”

Responding to the draft report, Jim Killock, executive director of Open Rights Group, told us: “The Committee’s call to protect our digital security is very welcome, particularly at a time when there is increasing international pressure for companies to be compelled to weaken the security of encrypted products. The UK already has these powers, which need to be subject to rigorous tests and open legal challenge so they are not misapplied, putting all of us at risk.”

Providing backdoors into encrypted communications would be the responsibility of the developers, who so far have been resistant to pressure from governments. Not only does such a possibility have privacy implications, there is also a strong chance that it could impact the digital economy. However, the argument from governments demanding these backdoors is a strong one. How better to battle domestic terrorism than find out exactly what suspects are discussing online and who their contacts are? In an age of regular terror attacks, these powers, it is argued, are necessary. This debate is likely to be played out in public over the coming months as the European Commission reviews the draft, and the German, French and British governments make their objections. And consider the Brexit situation: if existing EU laws will be incorporated into British law via the Great Repeal Bill, could we see encryption backdoors by the back door?
