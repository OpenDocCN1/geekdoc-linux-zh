# Tech culture

Artists, we note, pick up the playfulness in a lot of maker projects, but also the learning elements seem to run more deeply. We discuss this with Ross Dalziel, who says that he does that *self-consciously*.

Ross tells us that he has “gravitated to the maker world” more and more over the past five years. “What I liked about maker culture was that it reminded me of the more traditional, *pre-internet* making [when I was] one of those people who would *skulk* about in Maplins looking at the catalogue.”

“*Previously*, for Ross “*tech* was not the whole story.” Discussing a project some years ago [Disclaimer: the reporter, Richard Smalley, was a contributor] at FACT, Liverpool’s Foundation for Art and Creative Technology, he recalls: “*The technology* was subtle. I really wanted to get FACT growing things. The sensors and Arduinos were extensions to this [...] I see *tech* as just a part of *nature*, a part of the *culture* – things that we’ve *dug up*, but had to *process*.”

Domestic Science, the artists collective made up of Dalziel, Glenn Bottler from Barlow-in-Furness, and MudLab co-founder Hwa Young Jung, say they are self-conscious about technology. “*Interested not just in using it, but in participating in the culture rather than just exploiting it*.”

Dalziel’s stand at the MakeFest is demonstrating an *interactive text adventure* based on a *Cockle board* – an ESPIG66-based device developed with *ShrimpPing!*’s *Cefn Hoile*, who got involved in the game design and group decisions. The game is the result of an *unusual set of constraints* from an *Arts Council commission* for *The Hadrian’s Cavalry* 2017 exhibition, a *touring project across ten museums along the Hadrian’s Wall site to engage people in the exhibitions*: “*The brief was for a low-cost solution*,” says Dalziel and it drove “*multiple visits across sites, with game sites off-grid, with no mobile reception*. I [We] *discovered the idea of a mobile app*,” recalls Dalziel. “*The result was a text adventure game which allows young people to experience a playful world based upon Hadrian’s Cavalry. It uses tags and the game state is recorded on an RFID tag to carry between the sites. The tech is “hidden in a way, becoming artefacts in the museum*,” Dalziel explains. “*You must look around, carrying the story in your head [as you do with a story in a book], rather than looking down at a phone screen all of the time. Tags prompts it along, with game state in a hand-held object*.”

# Text first

Dalziel has been a fan of *text adventures* (also known as *interactive fiction*) from an early age. He wrote *text adventures* on a *ZX Spectrum*, first with a friend helping with the *programming side*, then with the *Quill* software. “*Later, when I found a Spectrum emulator, I spent a long evening with Quill trying to recreate that same adventure, giving up at the same point that I had when I was 12*!”

Another *text adventure project involves “interactive non-fiction*. Here, participants at events across the *North West* this summer create *text games* using the *Twine* software, which takes their inputs from *internet data, APIs and various webpages*. The games will be put in an *arcade cabinet*. In *September*, a *workshop for practitioners* will be held in *Liverpool*, for *artists and game designers* to be able to create their own projects. This blend of old and new, of *learning and play*, can be seen in the *steampunk costumes* that are out in force at every *Liverpool MakeFest* and the hammering from *The PatternCraft tables*. “*People just see the surface of tech culture, but there’s a rich layer underneath that*”
