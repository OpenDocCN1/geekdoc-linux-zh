Out of the box, the tool's straightforward and intuitive design make it ideal for users with limited or zero understanding of the underlying package management principles. Unfortunately, Mageia no longer provides a direct menu entry to launch the software management app. You must click Menu > Tools > System Tools > Mageia Control Center and then click Install and Remove Software to launch rpmdrake. You can also launch the software app from the aesthetically designed Welcome Screen.

While rpmdrake - the graphical software management utility - still relies on urpmi for resolving dependencies, the developers are also working on DNF dracora, an alternate app to act as front-end for the DNF system, but it won't be available anytime soon. The Mageia project is also heavily invested in collaborating with Fedora's COPR project which aims to provide a PPA-style system for building third-party package repositories.

With Mageia 6, all the apps have been ported to Plasma 5, the successor to KDE 4 desktop. The distro has finally dropped support for Lilo and Grub Legacy and only offers Grub 2 as the bootloader, but you can still continue to use the others if upgrading from an existing installation. Users running Mageia 5 can upgrade to Mageia 6 if they first install all updates available for 5, but they must then use the DVD release of the distro, not one of the live images. The primary reason for the delays was bugs in the installer, but we didn't encounter any in our test installs on a physical machine nor as a VirtualBox guest. But the distro isn't entirely bug-free. If you wish to enable parental controls, you can do so using the Control Center, but the behaviour is erratic and results in crashes. Similarly, while the distro allows creation of snapshots such that you can roll back to a previous state in case of a misconfigured system, the feature results in frequent crashes. The individual live images only offer a handful of everyday office, internet and multimedia apps. But users have access to thousands of additional packages through the Core, Nonfree and Tainted repositories. Proprietary drivers for hardware components such as wireless and graphics cards are offered through the Nonfree repository while multimedia codecs and other packages that may infringe on copyright, patent or other laws are relegated to the third repository. Note however that of the three, only Core is enabled by default.

## Review Free software

### BashStyle-NG 10.0
Customise command-line settings via a GUI

If you've ever come to customise your Bash prompt and, it being a while since the last time you've probably had to Google for the command, perhaps you've wished for an easy GUI way to do it? You'll have dismissed that fancy and contradictory notion - after all, a GUI for Bash? - but here we present a GUI config that handles not just Bash settings, but Readline, Vim, nano and Git. Installation went smoothly once we'd added the Python configobj package to our test machine (it's in the Debian/Ubuntu repositories, as well as via pip). For Bash, most of the settings are for prompts and colours. There's a selection of predefined prompts; custom colours; a GUI for customising the prompt; and colour highlighting in grep. Extras include a customised cd, which can create directories on the fly; a tab completion blacklist (for blocking out backup files); and history settings. The Git settings - name, email, default editor, output colours - will be handy for new and occasional users. Have a dig through the other features of the package, like octal and temperature conversions. This is a niche piece of software, but some users will find it indispensable.

### Mutt 1.8.3
Fast, efficient management of emails is a few commands away

![](img/fb76008333fb7aef815b136f9f1afe5f_89_2.png)

Those of us ancient enough to remember when email wasn't a smartphone app, but rather the main use of the internet, grew up with command-line clients like Elm and its clones Pine and Mutt. The move away from command-line clients has led to no gain in productivity, and with Mutt seeing a steady growth in improvements in the last two years, now could be the time for a revisit (or a first discovery).

It's one of the most efficient ways of dealing with a heavy email load. We need much more than a half-page review to tell you about the power of Mutt, so open the Newbie guide on the Mutt wiki, which also links to excellent third-party tutorials, then the full HTML manual. There is a list of shortcuts across the top line, which changes by context, as you'll see after you arrow down to one of your mails and open it by pressing Enter. Mutt's power comes not just with practice of the basic commands, but in chaining them together to speed up tedious filing options.

Remember, those minutes spent hunting through menus in the likes of Gmail or Evolution and clicking checkboxes to move, archive or delete emails - all add up. Spend the time instead learning a few commands, and you can whizz through tasks without your fingers leaving the keyboard. And when you've shelved into your server - or your Raspberry Pi - you'll be able to fire off an email, or work through the /var/mail/ files in an instant.

- **Pros**
  Productive shortcuts, context-specific settings; keyboard macros automate complex tasks
- **Cons**
  Plenty to learn, but the Mutt slogan is 'All mail clients suck. This one just sucks less!'

**Great for...**
Finally get on your e-mail mountain's under control

## VIRTUAL TABLETOP GAME VIEWER

### Rolisteam 1.8.2
Can't make it to a friend's tabletop game session? Join them remotely

> It started with a post on the French-language Ubuntu forum, asking for help porting some

Dungeons & Dragons software, which turned out to be written with Qt, and in short order was running on Ubuntu 7.10. Nearly a decade later, Rolisteam not only compiles easily under GNU/Linux, but has grown and improved considerably, and is translated into several European languages. It's about more than D&D, working as an efficient way of playing any tabletop game between remote players, as it facilitates viewing all of the necessary character sheets, maps and so on, across the internet.

The wiki and other (multilingual) documentation does cover everything, but you will need to dig through it to get a complete picture. Given that most Dungeons & Dragons devotees put a lot of time into their setups, this won't put many off. One user, usually the game master, sets up as server - or host - and the other players connect to them by server address; the port (default: 6660) needs to be open.

"The goal is to make Rolisteam-managed RPG games as good as RPG games around your table," says the homepage. All the components are there: character sheets; sharing of maps and pictures; vector mapping; dice rolling and secret dice rolling; chat; and ambient music (a mixed blessing). Rolisteam even has a built-in Twitter bot that you can call with tweets like #roll 5d10; you can also run the DiceParser locally in game viewer.

## Pros
Covers just about everything you need for a tabletop game with nifty vectorial mapping

## Cons
Ad hoc network connections can be problematic for any app - look out for firewall issues

## Great for...
Getting a tabletop on for D&D when you can't get together

www.rolisteam.org

## CODING EDITOR

### TKE 3.1
A friendly, feature-full code editor without the bulk

![](img/fb76008333fb7aef815b136f9f1afe5f_90_1.png)

Another text editor? It's easy to start a text editor project, but it takes real drive to keep one going through several versions, so it's worth taking a look at what TKE has to offer. It has syntax highlighting for 85 languages to hand multi-cursor/multi-selection support, and quick navigation through code with the Bird's Eye text viewer. TKE is written in Tcl, with a minimalist Tk GUI.

While installation was easy enough, and syntax highlighting and other features worked across many languages that we tried, there were some problems opening files: We experienced lock-ups of several seconds, or 'File does not exist' messages when other editors had no problems. We acknowledge that our test machines can throw up anomalies of their own and suggest, given that all else was fine, that readers download TKE themselves and give it a go.

While Tcl's Tk toolkit may be familiar to you - it's used in many Python projects, and fairly widely with other scripting languages - you might not be familiar with the Tcl language. A quick browse through the well-organised source files may pique your interest enough for you to try it out in your next scripting project. Extended use of TKE should convince you further.
