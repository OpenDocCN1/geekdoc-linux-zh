## SECURITY

### MEPs push for EU-wide backdoor ban

Can European Union legislation protect encryption?

Combating crime and terrorism has long been the rationale presented by governments to increase surveillance powers. But when it comes to encryption, there is a problem: encryption works or it does not. There are no half measures.

It’s an issue that is on the mind of a group of MEPs, who have proposed amended regulation to the European Parliament that forbids encryption backdoors for the purposes of law enforcement. In its draft report, the Committee on Civil Liberties, Justice and Home Affairs says that advances in technology have outpaced data protection provisions, resulting in the proposed amendments to the 2002 ePrivacy regulations. If passed, these would impact the UK’s intention to block end-to-end encryption (E2EE) from being used by apps such as WhatsApp.

The draft report states: “The providers of electronic communications services shall ensure that there is sufficient protection in place against unauthorised access or alterations to the electronic communications data, and that the confidentiality and safety of the transmission are also guaranteed by the nature of the means of transmission used, or by state-of-the-art end-to-end encryption of the electronic communications data. Furthermore, when encryption of electronic communications data is used, decryption, reverse engineering or monitoring of such communications shall be prohibited.”

Responding to the draft report, Jim Killock, executive director of Open Rights Group, told us: “The Committee’s call to protect our digital security is very welcome, particularly at a time when there is increasing international pressure for companies to be compelled to weaken the security of encrypted products. The UK already has these powers, which need to be subject to rigorous tests and open legal challenge so they are not misapplied, putting all of us at risk.”

Providing backdoors into encrypted communications would be the responsibility of the developers, who so far have been resistant to pressure from governments. Not only does such a possibility have privacy implications, there is also a strong chance that it could impact the digital economy. However, the argument from governments demanding these backdoors is a strong one. How better to battle domestic terrorism than find out exactly what suspects are discussing online and who their contacts are? In an age of regular terror attacks, these powers, it is argued, are necessary. This debate is likely to be played out in public over the coming months as the European Commission reviews the draft, and the German, French and British governments make their objections. And consider the Brexit situation: if existing EU laws will be incorporated into British law via the Great Repeal Bill, could we see encryption backdoors by the back door?

## HARDWARE

### Two new Linux laptops from Entroware

UK manufacturer unveils 13-inch and 17-inch Linux Ubuntu notebooks

Does the world need more Ubuntu laptops? UK manufacturer Entroware (www.entroware.com) certainly thinks so, releasing a pair of Ubuntu 17.04 notebooks in late June, with a choice of features.

First is the Apollo, a 13.1-inch slimline notebook in the MacBook Air tradition. The most basic version (£330 inc VAT) features a Pentium 4415U 2.3GHz dual-core CPU, although Core i3, i5 and i7 Intel Kaby Lake processors are also available. The baseline 4GB RAM and 500GB HDD can also be upgraded, while graphics are controlled by Intel HD Graphics. An LTE-enabled network card can also be selected.

Also new is the hybris, which starts from £810. The default option for this is a Core i5 processor, 4GB DDR4 RAM, a 500GB hard drive and Intel HD Graphics. All of this can be upgraded as above, of course, with an Nvidia GeForce GTX 1050 4GB also among the options.

Ubuntu 17.04 is installed by default on both laptop models, although 16.04 LTS is also available. But why Ubuntu? Entroware’s accounts and technical support manager Mike Wilson tells us that it’s really down to customer satisfaction.

“Ubuntu […] has proven itself to be a staple in the Linux desktop market, through years of stability, familiarity and ease of use. Coupled with the LTS releases and fast implementation of upstream updates, we are able to produce newer hardware to compete with the rest of the consumer IT market.”

“But isn’t it simpler to just install Ubuntu on a Windows laptop? “In the majority of cases, unfortunately, no. Some hardware vendors use components with poor Linux support, which can be problematic. This could mean that the customer would spend a lot of time trying to get their machine to work, if they are even able to do so.”

To overcome this, Wilson says Entroware’s approach is to source hardware to minimise compatibility issues, as well as bundle lifetime software support for each computer sold there. “Entroware’s support for Linux doesn’t end there. “Additionally, we help out the community by sponsoring events ( OggCamp), supporting distributions (Ubuntu MATE) and sponsoring Linux media (Late Night Linux).”

## TOP FIVE
Unity projects

![](img/fb76008333fb7aef815b136f9f1afe5f_8_0.png)

- **1 Yunit – the Unity 8 fork**
  While it failed to gain universal popularity, Unity certainly has its fans and many developers are collaborating on a new fork, Yunit. So far they’ve begun publishing Debian unstable releases for 64-bit systems. Yunit is also collaborating with the UBports project (below) for Ubuntu Touch devices. Find out more at https://yunit.io.

- **2 UBports saves Touch**
  Supporting Ubuntu phone and tablet devices and developing a presence on core devices (such as the Google Nexus 5 2015 edition), the team at https://ubports.com are doing a great job in keeping the Ubuntu Touch mobile OS alive.

![](img/fb76008333fb7aef815b136f9f1afe5f_8_1.png)

- **3 Plasma Unity theme**
  Switched to KDE Plasma and feeling sad about Unity’s demise? Thanks to the KDE Store’s “Loki and Feel” section it’s simple to install a Unity theme, known as the United Desktop. This theme runs on KDE Plasma 5.9.

- **4 Dash to Dock Unity**
  Finally, the popular GNOME extension Dash to Dock has been receiving many new contributions since Unity’s retirement was announced. Among the features to be ported are the launcher backlight and expose. Transferring to GNOME has become even less painful.

- **5 United GNOME theme**
  Amazingly, several Unity themes are already available. Most notable is the United 8-based United GNOME, available on GitHub, offering a faithful recreation of the desktop environment. As with many GNOME themes, United GNOME requires the GNOME Tweaks Tool for installation.
