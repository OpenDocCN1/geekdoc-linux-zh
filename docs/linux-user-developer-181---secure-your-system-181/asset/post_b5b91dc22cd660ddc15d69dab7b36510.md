
# Feature Liverpool MakeFest

SHOW REPORT

# LIVERPOOL • SCIENCE • TECHNOLOGY • CRAFTS MAKEFEST

As the festival celebrating creativity, invention and gadgetry reaches its third year, **Richard Smedley** joins the buzzing crowds.

> **KEYINFO**
> Make fests and Maker faires are where creatives and techies get together to show off their projects to each other and the public.
> Liverpool’s event is a free annual one-day festival supported by Liverpool Central Library and run by volunteers.

Over the last few years the maker scene has not just brought free and open source values to hardware and the emerging world of IoT, it has unleashed a wave of creativity and brought in thousands of people, young and old, to engage playfully with technology. With this in mind, LU&SD attended the third Liverpool MakeFest, one of the most fun-filled events on the tech calendar. We also took the opportunity to speak to local artist Ross Daziel about the role of arts in technology and the maker community, who describes himself as “an artist inspired by maker culture” that has been changed by the “hackspace and open source culture of programming and hardware.” But more on this later.

Held again in the Liverpool Central Library, with its elegant neoclassical facade opening onto a modernised interior – and a contrasting world of books, technology and elaborately costumed steampunks – the Liverpool MakeFest is a feast for the eyes as well as the brain. Here can be seen every facet of the UK’s strong and growing maker movement.

As well as the usual range of fab labs available at educational sites across Merseyside, Liverpool is blessed with two makerspaces. DoES (Do Extrastordinary Stuff!) is a long-time supporter of MakeFest, showing a range of projects. Liverpool Make, already expanded to two spaces, is running an Indiegogo campaign to raise funds for “Make Liverpool Weid”, to “bring open-access welding to the people of Liverpool”.

Many of the makerspaces and hackspaces present vigorously promote traditional crafts, which are also shown and even taught on many stands – from guitar making and jewellery from junk, to sewing and working with smart fabrics. Old and new crafts blended seamlessly and young visitors responded by taking to the traditional and the high-tech with equal enthusiasm.

## Thus one journeys to the stars

Near the entrance, visitors’ attention has traditionally been grabbed by a full-sized Dalek, but this year it had to compete with an array of rockets and DIY satellite chat, from North Wales-based maker, Jo Hincliffe.

From there, we follow two flights of staircases across the ceiling-height open atrium and up on to the roof to telescopes set up by the local astronomy society. Moving down the electromagnetic spectrum, we find representatives of the local amateur radio group – where a culture of learning and making for yourself predates home computing by decades, yet radio amateurs continue to adapt to new technology building software defined radios or having retro fun transmitting Morse code over the internet.

What united many of the diverse maker stands across five floors of the library, were the numerous hands-on events. Everywhere families and children were trying things out, having fun and learning to love the creative possibilities of technology. Education met technology in several unlikely ways, such as Gemma Latham’s PatternCraft, which links textiles, code and Minecraft, using a homemade device to read cards hand-punched by children (and many eager adults) visiting the stand. Python code builds the cards’ 8x8 patterns on a Minecraft map. Nearby, children learn about the evolution of programming via Jacquard looms and early computer-keyboard card readers, and experience the rare pleasure of learning to program through a means more tactile than a keyboard.

## Tech culture

Artists, we note, pick up the playfulness in a lot of maker projects, but also the learning elements seem to run more deeply. We discuss this with Ross Dalziel, who says that he does that *self-consciously*.

Ross tells us that he has “gravitated to the maker world” more and more over the past five years. “What I liked about maker culture was that it reminded me of the more traditional, *pre-internet* making [when I was] one of those people who would *skulk* about in Maplins looking at the catalogue.”

“*Previously*, for Ross “*tech* was not the whole story.” Discussing a project some years ago [Disclaimer: the reporter, Richard Smalley, was a contributor] at FACT, Liverpool’s Foundation for Art and Creative Technology, he recalls: “*The technology* was subtle. I really wanted to get FACT growing things. The sensors and Arduinos were extensions to this [...] I see *tech* as just a part of *nature*, a part of the *culture* – things that we’ve *dug up*, but had to *process*.”

Domestic Science, the artists collective made up of Dalziel, Glenn Bottler from Barlow-in-Furness, and MudLab co-founder Hwa Young Jung, say they are self-conscious about technology. “*Interested not just in using it, but in participating in the culture rather than just exploiting it*.”

Dalziel’s stand at the MakeFest is demonstrating an *interactive text adventure* based on a *Cockle board* – an ESP32-based device developed with *ShrimpPing!*’s *Cefn Hoile*, who got involved in the game design and group decisions. The game is the result of an *unusual set of constraints* from an *Arts Council commission* for *The Hadrian’s Cavalry* 2017 exhibition, a *touring project across ten museums along the Hadrian’s Wall site to engage people in the exhibitions*: “*The brief was for a low-cost solution*,” says Dalziel and it drove “*multiple visits across sites, with game sites off-grid, with no mobile reception*. I [We] *discovered the idea of a mobile app*,” recalls Dalziel. “*The result was a text adventure game which allows young people to experience a playful world based upon Hadrian’s Cavalry. It uses tags and the game state is recorded on an RFID tag to carry between the sites. The tech is “hidden in a way, becoming artefacts in the museum*,” Dalziel explains. “*You must look around, carrying the story in your head [as you do with a story in a book], rather than looking down at a phone screen all of the time. Tags prompts it along, with game state in a hand-held object*.”

## Text first

Dalziel has been a fan of *text adventures* (also known as *interactive fiction*) from an early age. He wrote *text adventures* on a *ZX Spectrum*, first with a friend helping with the *programming side*, then with the *Quill* software. “*Later, when I found a Spectrum emulator, I spent a long evening with Quill trying to recreate that same adventure, giving up at the same point that I had when I was 12*!”

Another *text adventure project involves “interactive non-fiction*. Here, participants at events across the *North West* this summer create *text games* using the *Twine* software, which takes their inputs from *internet data, APIs and various webpages*. The games will be put in an *arcade cabinet*. In *September*, a *workshop for practitioners* will be held in *Liverpool*, for *artists and game designers* to be able to create their own projects. This blend of old and new, of *learning and play*, can be seen in the *steampunk costumes* that are out in force at every *Liverpool MakeFest* and the hammering from *The PatternCraft tables*. “*People just see the surface of tech culture, but there’s a rich layer underneath that needs publicising*,” says Dalziel. “The importance of cultural knowledge and history that events like maker fairs don’t always have the best opportunity to show.

“It’s weird that people think that tech is fixed and new things – fresh copper and breadboards – but there’s hundreds of people’s work behind every chip,” says Dalziel, reissuing the culture and history behind every bit of circuitry. “Me, Glenn and Iiva (the Domestic Science team) are super-fans of tech. Some artists use technical culture. We’re more inspired by it. We want to change our practices.”

He gives an example of the benefits of deeper involvement in a local makerspace. People turn up at DoES (Liverpool’s co-working and maker space) and “Get time on the laser cutter, then leave. I try to put something back in [as well as] getting something out – forming better relationships. We can ask deeper questions and get a more nuanced understanding of tech, for better work down the line.”

## Code Club

Naturally, amid all of the traditional skills, coding and computing hardware still feature strongly at MakeFest: there are coding drones, tinkering with robots and a range of Arduino shields on sale. Many Pi-based desktops could be seen running Scratch or Python, but what really caught our eye was a hybrid of the two: EduBlocks, featuring a Scratch-like interface to drag blocks of Python-based code to build a program, which can then be switched to a view of the full Python code. It was co-created by 13-year old Joshua Lowe, founder and CEO of All About Code, and finalist in the BT Young Pioneer Award category of the Tech 4 Good awards. (Discover more at http://edublocks.org)

Education – and play – are important, but in a disaster situation, more fundamental needs must be met first. Makers are even helping there (see Interview, right). If your Raspberry Pi and Arduino are gathering dust, and you need inspiration, get out to a maker event near you – for creativity, fun [...] and the learning is a bonus.

## Interview: Dr Laura James, Field Ready

Many visitors left MakeFest talking about the stand for Field Ready (www.fieldready.org), an NGO which focuses on humanitarian supplies that are made in the field. We spoke to Dr Laura James, its co-founder and engineering lead.

### What does taking 3D printers to disaster scenes enable that couldn’t be done before?

We can make what’s needed on the ground, meeting immediate needs for local communities or aid workers. In Haiti, we met midwives with no sterile supplies left, using pieces of string to tie off umbilical cords where a sterile closure was needed. In Nepal, clinics damaged by the earthquake were left short of basic medical equipment.

Aid workers operate in unpredictable settings, and can find themselves needing to join unusual pipe sizes in strange configurations. [...] Field Ready, together with the university team, have created an app which allows the user to input the sizes of pipes that need to be and automatically generates a 3D model of a tailored pipe connector, ready for 3D printing.

### We’ve seen the open source way of working influencing many areas outside of software...

We’ve learned that we cannot solve all the design challenges we encounter in the field ourselves, even with brilliant teams of engineers and designers on the ground. So we have formed a cross-sector community, ‘humanitarian makers’ made up of hundreds of technologists, designers and aid workers, and which is an increasingly useful forum to find solutions, share information and find volunteers.

### Are your designs available for other organisations to print out in the field?

Yes, we share our designs openly for others to reuse. You’ll find collections on Thingiverse: (http://bit.ly/FRTthingiverse) and we use 3D-pressa for more detailed instructions. Our designs are generally CC-BY (Creative Commons Attribution licensed). We’ve heard from others reusing our designs, such as a project 3D-printing our tectopic design in Gaza, and sometimes they feed back improvements.

### Are there areas where you’re still looking for help from the maker community?

We’re always happy to welcome more humanitarian makers and are planning new programs for engagement in testing coming up later this year. Sign up at www.humanitarianmakers.org.
