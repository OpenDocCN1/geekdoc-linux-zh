With real-world objects, this self-similarity runs out of steam at the molecular and atomic levels, but a true mathematical fractal exhibits either exact or approximate self-similarity at all levels. In other words, it goes on forever as you continue to zoom in on it.

The first fractals were devised before the term fractal geometry was coined and were only recognised as such when mathematicians turned their attention to fractals in the 1960s and 1970s. A classic example is the Sierpinski gasket, a type of fractal that is referred to as an iterated function system (IFS) fractal. It’s easily generated with just a few lines of code. Start off with a solid (ie, filled in) equilateral triangle. Now, by joining the centre points of the three edges, divide it into four smaller equilateral triangles and remove the centre one. Next, do the same with the three remaining solid triangles, dividing them each into four smaller triangles and removing the centre one. Continue in this way until you get an effect you find pleasing or until you get bored, but bear in mind that to get a true Sierpinski gasket you’d have to continue forever.

Moving on from the Sierpinski gasket, you might like to delve into other IFS fractals including the Sierpinski carpet, the Sierpinski pentagon, the Koch snowflake and the dragon curve; you’ll find no shortage of information online. And if you fancy some 3D solid modelling, there are even three-dimensional versions of some of these fractals, including the Sierpinski pyramid and the Menger sponge.

Alternatively, there’s plenty of free and sometimes open source Linux software that can generate these and other IFS fractals. The most famous escape-time fractal is surely the Mandelbrot set – let’s see how it’s generated. Generating the Mandelbrot set involves working with complex numbers. As a quick reminder, these are numbers that have a real and an imaginary part; for example, 2 + 0.5i where i is the square root of minus 1. If this is still a mystery to you, we’ll have to leave you to read up on the subject, but it’s really not as difficult as you might fear.

# Picturing the Mandelbrot set

To generate an image of the Mandelbrot set, we need to carry out a test on all complex numbers (actually, you only need to consider points between -2.0 and +0.5 on the real axis and between -1.2i and +1.2i on the imaginary axis), marking the results on the complex plane. The test is simple: Start with zero, square it, and add the complex number being tested. If the result has a modulus of two or more (the modulus is the distance from the zero point on the complex plain), then the point isn’t in the Mandelbrot set. If it’s less than two, do the same again; that is, take that result, square it and add the value being tested. Again, if the result has a modulus of two or more, it isn’t in the Mandelbrot set; but if it’s less than two, carry on the calculation again and continue in this way. Some points, those that aren’t in the Mandelbrot set, soon exceed a modulus of two and head off towards infinity. Other points, those that are in the Mandelbrot set, remain close to zero however many times you repeat this operation (in practice, though, an arbitrary number of iterations, commonly 20, is used).
