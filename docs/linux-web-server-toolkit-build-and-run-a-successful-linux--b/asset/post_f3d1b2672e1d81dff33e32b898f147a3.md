## Creating a Plan for Your Site

Once you have the Web site "mission statement" in mind, you can begin to create a plan for getting your site up and running. That plan can include a schedule of when you want things to happen. It can be as simple as a Post-it note or as large as a full-wall Gantt chart.

You may need to wait a little to add the details to your plan: you can fill them as you read the rest of this book. The schedule will likely have changes and input from many members of your organization. Some of the things that you should consider in your plan include the items in the following sections.

Web sites are notorious for growing faster than anticipated. Quick decisions are often required in order to move forward with the site. By creating processes and refining how you will create, manage, and update your site, your site can be responsive to new needs without looking though it was all done in a day.

Your goals for your Web site and the budget and time limits of your organization are just a few of the factors that play into what information can be included on your Web site. One of the key planning steps is to determine the content of your Web pages.

## Who Will Maintain Your Web Pages?

A related but separate critical issue is determining a process for keeping the content of your site—your Web pages up to date. As I mentioned previously, one of the surest ways to keep people from returning to your site is to provide only "old" information. For best results, use the following rule for any active Web site to which you want to draw traffic.

Even if the information remains entirely constant, the "last updated" date should be no more than two weeks old. To follow this rule, you can simply update the main page with comments about how heavy traffic has been, what things are coming soon, what changes were made recently. It makes more like a diary instead of an old newspaper.
