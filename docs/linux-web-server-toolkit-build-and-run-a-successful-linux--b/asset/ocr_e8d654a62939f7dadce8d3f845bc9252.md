# ISDN

ISDN, which stands for the Integrated Services Digital Network, provides two digital channels of 64K each for a total of 128K in its most common configuration. This is almost four times the standard 33.6K modem. With an ISDN connection to your office, you can use one line for a voice telephone and the other for data, or both for data to increase your bandwidth. ISDN is therefore more flexible than either standard phone lines or frame relay connections.

ISDN uses a switched digital circuit like a modem call over a regular voice line (see Figure 7-3), but ISDN circuits provide guaranteed bandwidth and digital reliability.

The downside of ISDN is the cost. Above and beyond the cost of installation, ISDN is almost always charged in time units, and sometime in data units. Put another way, you're charged for every minute connecting via ISDN. While these charges may not be a problem for an end user connecting for an hour each night to browse the Web at home, they may make it unwieldy for a full-time ISDN connection.

ISDN been getting a lot of press for years, and it's finally starting to become a reality in most parts of the country. ISDN has been especially popular in Europe and Japan, where phone booths already include ISDN modem connections.
Linux provides support for numerous ISDN boards and external ISDN methods. Many are manufactured in Europe, however, Linux is big and ISDN have more time to gain popularity. ISDN cards are not very expensive (less than $500).

# T1 AND T3

T1 and T3 connections, and their siblings and pseudonyms, OC3, E3, and other very high-speed digital connections would be the most popular if money were not an issue. A T1 connection provides 1.544MB per second, equivalent to 24 voice telephone lines, or 43 times the bandwidth of a 33.6 modem. A T3 connection provides 45MB per second, or more than 1300 times the bandwidth of a 33.6 modem.
Aah, to dream.

T1 and T3 connections are appropriate for organizations that anticipate huge amounts of Web site and related FTP and e-mail traffic. Large installations with 100,000 hits per day and hundreds of megabytes per day in file transfers should have a T1 connection. A stable T3 connection transfers several hundred gigabytes (liga, not megabytes) each day without choking. The Internet backbone used T3 lines for a backbone. It's moved to bigger things, but larger ISPs regularly use T3 lines to connect to the backbone. If your server traffic requires these speeds and your budget can support them, Linux provides full support for T1 and T3 connections.

A T1 connection can be created using a dedicated leased line to your ISP or a shared technology such as frame relay. Be sure that if you have leased a T1 line to your ISP, the ISP has at least much bandwidth to the Internet backbone.

The stability of T1 lines is very good, as with the other digital services, though maintenance can be tricky because of the special equipment and very high speeds.
