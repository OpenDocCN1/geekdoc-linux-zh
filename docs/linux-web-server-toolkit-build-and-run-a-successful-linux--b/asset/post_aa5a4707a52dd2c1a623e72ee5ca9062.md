# What's a URL Really Made Of?

As of most of you probably know, a URL (You-Are-Else, sometimes called an Earl, but not by me) is a Uniform Resource Locator. It is a standardized format for identifying a location on the Internet and the protocol by which that location is accessed. But you know the different components of a URL and what they do? Here's the breakdown:

1. The protocol, followed by a colon. The most commonly seen protocol is http, which indicates a Web site. Many others are used in Web browser links. (Other protocols are described in Chapter 4).
2. The server on the Internet, beginning with a double forward slash (bottom left to top right). The server can be specified by an IP address or by a domain name, which is what we prefer—something like //www.newmovie.com. (IP addresses and domain names are described in Chapter 4).
3. A file on the specified server with a complete path to reach it, with a single forward slash. A default file is sent if a filename is not given; if the server's home page is indexed, the slash is left off, giving just http://www.newmovie.com. An example of the path is /doc/brochures/index.html.

Several other things can be tucked inside a URL, including the port number to access the server and the username to log in for certain protocols. But you usually don't see these on movie posters.

# Chapter 1: Planning Your Web Site

The statistics are amazing, but not fun as the little things that have changed in the past four years. I was still explaining what a URL was 24 months ago. Now they're everywhere: on the side of every UPS delivery truck, on every movie poster, on every new business card, and on every annual report, no matter what the industry.

In fact, we've already moved beyond standard URL format that includes the protocol, server, and so on. Now you just see mci.com, and they expect you to know that it's for Web Access. And so on, you type it into your browser without the extra stuff (http://www/), and in most cases it works.

One thing that you should keep in mind is the Web is not really ubiquitous. Only about 20 million Americans have Web access, out of a nation of 250 million. But those 20 million represent the demographic everyone in business wants to reach. We've reached the point where you can assume that anyone in business, with a portion of the general public, knows exactly what you mean if you have your annual report on the corporate Web Site. With that infrastructure and understanding in place, the Web can really start to become useful for everyone.

## Why You Need a Web Site

Apart from the fun of browsing museums and new movies, there are some solid business reasons for having a Web Site. To provide some facts in case your boss asks what you're doing with all your free time, here are a few of the serious reasons why you need to have a Web Site for your organization, it a business, nonprofit organization, user group, or about anything else:

- Your customers are asking you when you'll have a Web Site.
- All of your competitors have a Web Site.
- You can provide high-quality, cost-effective customer service.

# Keeping Up with Customer Expectations

Because everyone else seems to have one, the computer-using public (a rapidly growing majority) is beginning to use the Web for all sorts of things, such as reviewing new movies, looking up reports on companies, doing school reports, and more. If they want to find out your organization's products or services, it's becoming more and more likely that they will turn to the Web and just type your company name to see what you have to offer.

That brings us to reason number two for having a Web Site.

## Staying Competitive

If you want really to be depressed, think of your three toughest competitors and then start up your browser and get on the Web (I do this at work, when I fool myself into thinking I have time for a break). You might have to use a search engine to find your competitors' sites, but in many cases, you'll find them.

If your competitors are not on the Web, great! You can beat them to it and have a top-rated site that ruins their day.

Now, I know that's not fair—would never really judge a company like that, just on its advertising or annual report or other details. This brings me to the third reason.

## Providing Cost-Effective, Quality Service

The reason the Web became so popular is not because you can play your favorite game, such as Doom, with someone in Australia. The Web provides services that would be more expensive if it were provided by other means. Companies focus their entire business around their Web site. Learn2.com, shown in Figure 1-1, is an online training company that provides all materials via the Web.

In summary, the Web lets you:

- Provide information that is updated instantly from your company to the's hands.
- Do so with almost (but not quite!) incremental cost.
- Do so while reducing other traditional costs.

In reference to the second reason for having a Web site, your customers are right: if you're not on the Web, you're not using your resources to provide services effectively.

## Other Benefits of a Web Site

Of course, there are other reasons for having a Web site. And in outlining these reasons, I would be remiss if I overlooked the fact that the nature of the Internet encourages people to communicate. From the first days of the Web, people have been putting useful information resources on the Web to share what they have or what they know.
