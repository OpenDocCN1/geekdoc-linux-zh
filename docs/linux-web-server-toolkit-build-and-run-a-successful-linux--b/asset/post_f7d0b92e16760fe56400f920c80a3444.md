## Defining the Webmaster’s Job

Why do Webmasters command such high salaries? Because of the importance of their positions in the organizations they serve, and because of the variety and depth of skills they must have. As you consider who will be maintaining your Web site, the following list of skills and experience for an ideal Webmaster candidate may be helpful.

A Webmaster must be several diverse people at once. For this reason, as with the huge workload on large sites, the job of Webmaster is often handled by several people rather than just one. A Webmaster must be able to either complete or manage the following tasks:

- Plan and maintain an information delivery strategy for all corporate materials.
- Provide system administration for a complete UNIX operating system with hundreds or thousands of users using each day.
- Write coherently to create cover pages, notes, and navigational aids to guide users through the actual site content; and to create reports that inform management of what's happening with the Web site.
- Tag files in HTML format and use various tools to convert from other formats.
- Create attractive graphics to highlight the Web site’s offerings and show the organization in a favorable light.
- Respond politely to numerous e-mails from sometimes befuddled, sometimes rude users about all aspects of the site (content, style, appearance, speed, and so on).

What skills should you look for when hiring a Webmaster to do all these tasks? The following list correlates with the previous list of tasks:

- Ability to write clearly. This is paramount for creating page text that makes sense and is typo-free, for responding to e-mail with clear responses, and for creating reports for management of what’s happening with the site.

## Legal Considerations for Web Sites

First, the obligatory disclaimer: I am not licensed to practice law in any state. Free legal advice is what you pay for it. Okay, you've been warned.

Putting information on the Web is about the most public thing you can do. It's more public (but less embarrassing) shouting something on a street corner. For that reason, I recommend observing carefully the legal rules before the material you want to post. Many times, I've seen people post things to “working areas” on Web sites that they thought no one would see. Soon, they have a dozen e-mails with questions and comments on their half-finished Web pages. As if it weren't bad enough Web survive to dig and explore (even searching HTML code for hidden links on single characters and so forth), the spiders and search engines now index everything that is publicly visible on your site and create a link directly to it.

A couple of other, less obvious points: When you place information on your Web page, you may be making a legally binding statement, like printing an advertisement and being held accountable for what you say.

A refresher on contract language: when you say you warrant something, you are offering a guarantee that something will be done or work a certain way. Then you give them a remedy if the warrant fails. If you say instead that you "represent" to be true, and it turns out not to be true, then that is fraud. Check your contracts for some examples.

In addition, when you create such as a Web page, it is automatically copyrighted all over the world (or most of it). You make that copyright known by using the circle-c symbol: ©. If you have additional legal protection to allow you to prosecute someone for infringing your copyright, file an application with the government.

When you own the copyright to a Web page, you own and control, among other things, the right to display it and copy it.

Another legal notice: that applies to many Web pages is trademark usage. A trademark is a symbol that identifies a product or service to customers. If someone is using your trademarks in such a way that customers are confused as to whether the other person is you, that person is infringing on your trademark.

With that in mind, these are some items you might include either directly on your Web pages (at least your home page, but I've seen it on every page) or as a link to a complete legal information sheet:

- A copyright notice. This could be a single line at the bottom of each page (like something: copyright: (c) 1997 Nicholas Wells) so that it appears on any printed copy of the Web page and is part of the HTML for that page. You can also provide a link so that when visitors click "Copyright Information," for example, they gain access to a page with detailed copyright statements.
- A page with notices about trademarks your organization owns and how they can be used. This may be particularly important because most Web browsers, although HTML provides a method to encode one.

## Summary

This chapter has been an introduction to building a Web site. While much of it may be familiar territory to you, I hope you've found some material that may help you better understand the importance of your Web site and the larger role it can play in your organization.

In this chapter you've learned the following:

- The Web helps you stay competitive and promotes customer loyalty.
- Lets information that can be updated with almost zero incremental cost and a reduction of traditional costs for printing, mailing, and so on.
- A good Web site provides accurate, timely information, offers helpful yet unique services, doesn't waste bandwidth, and may allow customers to purchase products or services.
- Ineffective Web sites are those that are bandwidth-hungry, have unprofessional design and content, and offer only an advertisement.
- Before you plan your Web site, define its purpose and what its "look and feel" should be, and create a schedule and site map.
- Webmasters and staff support must be able to plan and maintain an information delivery strategy for all corporate materials, provide system administration for a complete UNIX operating system, create coherent documentation, tag files in HTML format and use conversion tools, create attractive graphics, and respond politely to numerous e-mails.
- Remember to consider the legal ramifications of making public the information you intend to post.
