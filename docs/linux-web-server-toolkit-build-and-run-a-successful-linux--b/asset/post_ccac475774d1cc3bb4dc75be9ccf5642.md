## Adding Your Site to Search Engines

The previous section described how you can use Internet advertising methods to promote your site. That’s actually the second phase of your efforts. The first phase should be getting a free listing on all the search engine sites.

Search engines offer a service to users by having an index of useful information. It’s in their best interest to have the most complete listing of Web sites that they can. But they depend on you to tell them about yourself. When you do, they are added to their indexes, so that any user who searches for items that include your key areas will see your Web site listed.

Most of these sites have a Submit or Add button right on the first page, where you can start the process of being added to their site index. There is no charge to be added.

You should carefully plan what you want to say before you start submitting information about your site. Review the business decisions you made in the couple of chapters, before you started your Web site. As you plan what you want to say, answer questions like the following:

- Who do you want to appeal to?
- Who is your customer?
- What demographic information will apply to that customer?
- What search words would someone use who was looking for what you have to offer?
- How can you incorporate these words into a brief site description?
- What key message or reason to purchase fits your main business goals?
- What is the most unique thing you offer to those who visit your site?
- How can you most concisely state the benefits you offer users?

If you submit your information, it's also a good idea to explore several of the sites listed in this section to see how they operate. Try to find information on one of your hobbies or business interests. See how you locate location and use that path to help aid people in finding your site. There are many and more services that offer to sign you up for "over 200 search-index sites for one low fee." These may save you some time, but I would discourage you from using them for two reasons. First, 95 percent of users will go to the top ten or search engines—those listed in this section. You can easily fill out submissions to these sites and saving a fee. Second, these services are not likely to tailor your business case according to the types of queries or different markets served by different search engines. If you do it yourself, you get more accurate, carefully crafted inclusion in the indexes. For quality, not quantity. Advertising and search engines are the most popular ways to bring people to your Web site. Once they're there, you can ask them to tell you about themselves. You'll be surprised how many will volunteer information. The next section offers some direction on learning about visitors to your site.

## Gathering User Data

If I'm strongly opposed to random e-mail blasts to reach potential customers on the Internet, I'm in favor of making it easy for people to express an interest in your Web site and thereafter receive more information from you.

The first step in gathering information about the users that visit your site is to use the statistics that can be generated from your Web server's log files.

## Using Web Statistics

Web site statistics do not include users' names or e-mail addresses, but they do indicate a few things that can help you serve your visitors better. One is the type of browser each visitor to your site is using. Having this knowledge allows you to make your pages look great in the top few browsers. Your site may have a lot of Macintosh users or a lot of people on slow connections using a character-based browser, or most visitors may use Microsoft Internet Explorer.

Another piece of information that statistics track is a visitor's domain. These don't enable you to learn geographic boundaries within the United States, but they indicate how many international come to your site. They also let you divide visitors into groups such as .edu, for educational institutions, .gov, for government agencies, and .com, for businesses.

Finally, because statistics are often sorted by the most numerous domain going to your site, you can sometimes evaluate aspects as whether your competitors are visiting your site, whether indexing program is bogging down your server, or whether most of your visitors are aol.com, indicating individual users.

Using Web statistics to your advantage is covered in more detail in Chapter 13.

## Using Registration Forms

You can get a lot of good information from Web server logs but you can get a lot more by asking people to enter their names or other information when they your site. The two common ways of doing this are:

- Registration to receive notification or updates regarding your products or services: to do this, you would collect a name, perhaps a company and title, and some method of contacting the user who is registering, usually an e-mail address. You can indicate on your registration form exactly what a user is registering for, and include guarantees that the e-mail names will not be sold or will be used except for the single stated purpose. Or, you can simply indicate something like "To be on your mailing list, fill this out." People who your organization will fill out, trust you to send them the occasional, brief e-mail about upcoming specials, new products or events, and so forth. Always state clearly how a user can be removed from your mailing list.

- A survey to collect facts or opinions: visitors deserve to know a little about what you intend to do with the information. It's common to post the results of user surveys on your Web site after the survey is over, but you don't have to do this.
