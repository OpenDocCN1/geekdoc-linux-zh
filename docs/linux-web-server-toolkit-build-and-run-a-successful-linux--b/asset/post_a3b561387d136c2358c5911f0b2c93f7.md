## FULL-SERVICE ISP

Many ISPs that make Internet access their mainline business have grown up since the early 1990s, when they had 50 subscribers and a UNIX expert running an old Sun workstation.

A full-service ISP focuses on the larger business market, providing the consulting, high-speed access, and other services that smaller businesses and individual users don't really need. Some full-service ISPs will add Web hosting, HTML design, or related services to their offering; others exclusively on Internet access and refer value-added services, such as HTML design, to a network of specialists who know.

Because full-service ISPs target the business community, they are likely to offer higher speeds, complete service packages, and more competitive rates than a typical reseller ISP. Many new ISPs who want to jump into this business in a big way try to full-service ISPs without the experience to make a real asset in your Web site plans. The challenge is to find an experienced full-service ISP in your area. The items listed in the section "Considering the Right Factors" will help.

## WHOLESALERS

The largest of ISPs do not intend to sell service directly to small businesses, individuals, or even large businesses. That's why I call them wholesalers. Their business is selling large amounts of bandwidth to other ISPs or very busy sites. Examples of wholesalers can even include companies like Sprint, which tries to get large ISPs to connect to the Internet using a Sprint trunk line.

A wholesaler can relocate your Web server on one of its multiple T3 lines or multiple T1 lines into a regional ISP. It has the high-end technical expertise to solve almost any problem. In fact, when part of the Internet has a slowdown or crashes, it's often the people at these very large crossroads who find, diagnose, and solve the problems (OK, they occasionally cause them well).

It's unlikely that you'll work with a wholesaler unless you're creating a very large site. One reason is that you won't need bandwidth in the amounts that these ISPs like to. Another is is a wholesaler expects clients to have a great deal of technical savvy – something like the support staff of a full-service ISP would have. You're likely to want more hand-holding if this is your first venture in providing a Web server of your own.

## BANDWIDTH

You've probably determined how much bandwidth you need for your site, and how quickly you might need to upgrade that connection if things goes well. Now you need to know how much bandwidth a potential ISP has available for you.

The computers that an ISP uses for servers are rarely a bottleneck. The important issues are simply these: How large is the ISP's connection to the Internet, and how many people are using it?

The obvious point is: if you need a T1 connection to the Internet, and your reseller ISP is using a 33.6k modem to connect to the Internet, your T1 is useless. Less obvious might be this scenario: you have a partial T1, with the phone guaranteeing connection to the ISP, and your potential ISP has a full T1, but also has eight customers with partial T1 lines connecting through that full T1 line. You're not likely to get your 256k throughput to the Internet very often.

Ask your potential ISP how many customers and what types are served by what types of Internet connection. If you can find out, determine how saturated the ISP's current connection is.

You should get an answer like this if your ISP is open about business: "We have 800 accounts, half individuals, small businesses, with a few larger businesses. Most individuals a few hours each day via a 14.4k or 28.8k modem. A dozen or so are dedicated connections via 28.8k modem. We have two full T1 lines connected through the Sprint trunk in this area. The T1 circuit that services your permanent virtual circuit (PVC) handles about 80 other accounts. In general, we run about 60 percent on both lines, but it goes to 90 percent in the evenings when everyone is browsing the Web after work. We plan on adding another T1 line later this year."

If you were asking these questions for individual use via your home modem, you would also ask about how many modems they had, areas with local access numbers, and how often the modems were busy. But you're getting a dedicated line, so that information is not relevant.

## EXPERIENCE

The biggest potential headache with your ISP is lack of experience. You may be able to live with high costs, you can even insist on increased bandwidth, but an ISP that can't answer your questions or doesn't know where to direct will leave you feeling all alone – just you and your Internet server.

Like hiring a software engineer, however, it can be a real challenge to determine the experience and expertise of an ISP's staff, especially when you're not yet an expert yourself. That's why you need them to be!

You can drill an ISP technical representative with a few of your hardest questions (don't judge the ISP by the salesperson's answers). But it's also important to look at the following telling signs:

- How long has the ISP been in business? An ISP with four years' experience is an old-timer.
- Does it have a reputation among businesses? Does it have a reputation among area technical specialists? Top technical people will know if a big ISP is blowing smoke without the expertise to back it up.
- How large is the ISP's staff? How experienced are the key members of that staff? A larger staff indicates a commitment to solving problems. Even a small staff of professionals is comfort during a storm.
- Can you contact references of the ISP? Ask for a reference that matches your needs closely as possible. That is, if you're planning a partial T1 connection, have technical but not Internet expertise inhouse, and want to colocate your server at the ISP, ask to talk to someone who's in a similar situation.

## COST

To misquote a phrase, the bitterness of extended technical problems with an inexperienced ISP remains long after the sweetness of low rates is forgotten. The reality of telephone companies, high rates for high-speed digital service is likely to remain part of the Internet access landscape in the foreseeable future.

While competition among ISPs flares up in different areas regularly and causes a shakeout of the latest entrants hoping to cash in the Internet, stable, quality companies with even rates year after year.

Nevertheless, you and should price-shop among those ISPs that meet your selection criteria. Comparing basic access rates for a given speed of connection is fairly trivial; most ISPs produce price lists with these figures. The more important thing to check is the full range of services and fees associated with using an ISP. Check the following list of questions related to services and potential challenges:

- What is the basic monthly line charge for my desired line speed?
- What is the setup fee to connect at that?
- What equipment must I provide for your side of the connection and how much do you anticipate it costing? Can provide discounts on hardware purchased through you or do you recommend a specific model or vendor?
- What are payment terms and methods for my monthly connection fee?
- Do I receive any sort of discount if you experience downtime or equipment problems that block access to my site? What are your policies on that?
