
# Chapter 7: Connecting to the Internet

Of course, you're paying so much to the ISP and the phone company that you can usually get the help you need to keep things running smoothly. Both your ISP and your phone company continually monitor T1 connections and let you know of any problems. If any service interruptions occur, they are responsible for fixing the problem and giving you a full report.

That brings you the only real downside of a T1 line: the cost. A full T1 line with guaranteed 1.544MB bandwidth cost $1500 to $2000 per month. Leased lines between you and another office or your ISP cost even more.

Partial T1 lines (up to T1 speed in a burst, with a guaranteed average of 1/4 or 1/2 of T1 speed) may also be available in your area. These cost less and are very easy to upgrade to full-time guaranteed T1 speed.

T3 lines are likely to cost over $25,000 per month. You might consider buying stock in the telephone company when you get your line.

## ADDING UP THE COSTS

The previous sections present some Internet connection options for your Web server, but you may have already decided what connection type you want. The costs associated with an Internet connection, especially a high-speed digital connection for a large Web site, can be very high. The lists and tables in this section help you get a complete handle on what to expect as you budget for your Web site and make decisions about the trade-offs between speed, cost, stability, and scalability.

The following costs are associated with obtaining an Internet connection; not all apply to every connection option:

- *Web server hardware*: The obvious costs are your Intel-based PC and monitor. The hardware required for your Internet connection can include an expansion card for your system, maybe a separate CSU/DSU unit, and even a separate router if your server is not also acting as your router, which Linux usually does unless you anticipate a lot of traffic.

It's worth pointing out here if you were using any operating system other than Linux, you'd have to have a larger PC for the same performance, maybe even a UNIX workstation at $20,000 to $75,000, and definitely additional equipment such as a CSU/DSU (the CSU/DSU for your Linux server can be part of the frame relay card).

- *Web server software*: The CD-ROM included here is sufficient for a complete Web server. Any other software needed for your hardware will be available from the hardware vendor or the Internet via an FTP site.

Again, I want to point out that if you weren't using Linux, you would spend several thousand dollars for the complete Web server software package (with OS, Web server, utilities, networking, and so on) included with this book.

- *Communications line fees*: These are the fees you pay to the telephone company to install and configure the full-time connection you will use to connect to your ISP.

- *ISP start-up fees*: These are the fees that the ISP charge to set up your account, configure your hardware at its site, configure your communications line to work correctly with its equipment and use its IP addresses, and consult you on getting things running smoothly.

- *Consulting*: You might need additional consulting help if you are creating a large site. This includes design and creation of Web pages as well as technical consulting on connection recommendations and implementation. The faster your communications line, the more likely it is that you'll need consulting beyond what your ISP will provide as part of your installation and setup fees.

The following costs are associated with maintaining an Internet connection from month to month; not apply to every option:

- *Communications line fees*: These are the monthly fees you pay to the telephone company for your communications line.

- *ISP fees*: These are the monthly fees you pay for your ISP to connect to its site.

- *Consulting*: You should have little need for ongoing consulting, but large sites will want to know who they can call.
