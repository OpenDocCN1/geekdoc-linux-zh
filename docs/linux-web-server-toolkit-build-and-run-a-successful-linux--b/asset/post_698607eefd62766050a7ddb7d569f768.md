## Choosing an Internet Connection Type

With so much press about the Internet, many people may think that connecting to the Internet is a single, monolithic process, like filing your tax return. (And about as difficult, would guess.) It can certainly pose some obstacles along the way, but there are many ways you can connect your system to the Internet. Some are expensive, others are very reasonable. Some are extremely stable, others require more oversight.

In the sections that follow, I describe the key decisions and trade-offs that you'll make as you connect to the Internet. You'll learn what being connected really means, and the different ways you can get connected.

## What Being Connected

Connecting to the Internet means very simply that you are connected to the world-wide network of computers that we commonly call the Internet. Depending on the security restrictions you place on your system, you can connect to anyone else's computer that's connected to the Internet and anyone else's computer on the Internet can connect to your system. It's a lot like a peer-to-peer network in your office using a networking operating system such as Windows for Workgroups, Lantastic, or Personal NetWare.

## TERMINAL AND PEER CONNECTIONS

Remember in Chapter 4 the discussion about peer connections and terminal connections? A terminal connection lets you see the output on your screen from a program that is running on another computer, which may be connected to the Internet. If you're using only a terminal-emulation-style connection, no one on the Internet can connect to your computer, and you can't provide services - a Web page - to anyone on the Internet. All you have a "window down," to, because you're not on the Internet, or a terminal to, a machine that can do those things because it's connected to the Internet.

A peer connection, on the other hand, means your computer has equal status with all the other computers on the Internet. As a peer, you can exchange files between your computer and other Internet computers to which you have access rights.

## CLIENTS AND SERVERS

When people use a gateway or server in their organization to browse the Web or access their e-mail, they are, in a certain way, connected to the Internet. Depending on their network, they may have several different types of access, but they are accessing the Internet only as a client. They are using only client software, such as a Web browser; they are not using a Web server.

This is also true of many online services, such as America Online, which provide client access to the Internet for users to read newsgroups, browse the Web, and so forth. They don't allow users to provide any services to other computers and users on the Internet. They may provide a separate service called Web hosting (covered in this chapter) but they are not providing a complete connection to the Internet.

## FULL-TIME OR PART-TIME

The Web is always open. This means people can access it at any time of the day or night, no matter what part of the world they are located in. A Web server and the information you place on it aren't a grocery store, where you can place a “Closed” sign up so you don't have to staff the store 24 hours per day. On the Web, if you're closed, you don't exist at all, and a customer is likely not to trust you when you might be open.

This is another difference between a Web server and the client, many people are more familiar with: the client connections are part-time. People log in, read e-mail, and log out. After that session, they have no connection whatsoever to the Internet.

A Web server requires a full-time connection, which means it is available 24 hours per day, seven days per week. A Web server requires a different type of connection from the dial-in connection you might use to browse the Web. ## HOSTING

One option many people use for their Web presence is called Web hosting. This refers to your ISP having a dedicated area on its server where Web pages are stored. It just looks to your customers as though you have your own, but you don't have.

## Linux Web Server Toolkit

If you're thinking of using Web hosting, setting up a Linux server as an in-house staging area can still make sense. You can create all the pages exactly as you want them to appear, then have them uploaded to your ISP. But you don't have a high-speed, permanent internet connection.

Personal users who want a home page or a small business Web hosting as a low-cost means of achieving a Web presence. The ISP server is always up and running and provides good bandwidth for those reading your pages. Small Web pages can be added to your home page without extra charge; these personal Web sites often allow up to 5MB of HTML and graphics files. Because they are hosted on your ISP's computer, a personal Web site like this will usually have a URL that refers to your domain name. For example, if a large ISP is hosting Web sites for several businesses, they might each have a home page URL that looks like this:

```http://www.fastnet.com/~travelers/index.html
```

Not all ISPs are equipped to provide Web hosting, but larger sites can offer the additional service of your own domain name, which is administered by the ISP and points to its server. This always costs extra (usually at two to three times as much), because a domain name implies a business account. But it's certainly impressive for a business to have a URL such as the following:

```http://www.travelers.com
```
