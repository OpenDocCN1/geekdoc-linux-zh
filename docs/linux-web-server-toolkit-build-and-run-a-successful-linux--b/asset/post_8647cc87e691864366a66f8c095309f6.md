Although HTML Analyzer doesn't check a wide variety of things like other tools, it does one thing and does it very well. The HTML Analyzer runs three checks on your Web pages:

- *Validity*: Checks for links that are poorly formatted or broken. HTML Analyzer actually contacts the URL referred to that any server errors are reported as a broken link. Your users will appreciate when all the links on your pages actually go somewhere.

(Note that links to services like telnet cannot be checked because they require a user's input to process. Also, local links such as HREF="#index" and empty links are not checked or reported back.)

- *Completeness*: Ensures that every time a certain phrase occurs in your Web pages, it has a hyperlink. For example, in your online manual, everywhere you refer to a certain chapter, you want to have a link to that chapter. If you have the link in place once, this check will note any other locations that the same text occurs in the document but is not linked to the other chapter.

This provides greater ease-of-use for your visitors by ensuring that they have many cross-referenced links as possible, with every possible occurrence of a link pointing to the same location.

- *Consistency*: An extension of the completeness check that ensures there is a one-to-one correspondence between text phrases in your pages and the pages they link to. This means that every identical occurrence of a phrase that you've hyperlinked points to the same location.

Note that this test won't report phrases that aren't linked but should be, only those phrases that appear as if they point to the same location but don't, or shouldn't point to the same place but do.

## Backups and Disaster Recovery

AN OLD SAYING IN the computer world goes something like this: there are two kinds of people – those who have had a system crash and those who will have a system crash. The implication is important: at some time, everyone is going to be faced with hardware or other failures that threaten-systems. This chapter describes how you can be prepared so that system failures will have the least impact possible on your Web site. Topics in this chapter include the following:

- Determining your level of need
- Implementing a plan for backups

## Determining Your Level of Need

All systems are going to have trouble at some time, even if it's years down the road. Preparing for trouble is the last thing most people think about. Why? Because it takes you away from the day-to-day things that are immediately a-concern and makes you think about things that might be a concern sometime down the road. As people allocate their time and money, they prefer not to spend it preparing for events they think will never happen. But all insurance is down a rat hole until you need it.

So let me play insurance salesman for a moment and tell you that you will need it. The issue is determining the level of importance that backup planning should play for your organization.

## How Important Is Our Web Site

The basic question you have to answer is simple: How important is our Web site to the continuing operation of our organization and happiness of our customers? Let me mention a couple of examples.

You might be operating a medium-sized association with a Web site that receives hits from members around the world. All your agendas and newsletters are on the site, and they're updated every couple of days as new announcements are prepared Linux Web Server Toolkit for the association members.

If your Web site is down for a couple of hours, no one will really be concerned, and you probably won't lose too much sleep over it. In fact, if the site were down for several days, your members might call or think something was wrong, but it wouldn't materially affect the organization's future.

On the other hand, think of a company that does business only on the Internet, such as an online bookstore. If the Web server of such a company goes down for five minutes, the company loses a fixed percentage of its sales volume and lose more later, as customers concerned about the site's stability try another online bookstore. If the site were down for a day or two, it might be mentioned in the computer press as an indication of unstable the Internet can be – witness the nationwide announcements when America Online has problems. It would certainly spend a very bad sales months.

Of course there are many levels of Internet dependence, so the general question becomes a simple cost/benefit analysis: How much will a failure of your Web site cost your organization? How much does failure prevention cost? How much does continuous uptime benefit or earn for the organization?

## Possible Web Server Problems

Just to give you some ideas to think about, the following are some possible problems that keep your Web server from being live and accessible:

- * Your hard disk could fail (hardware failure).
- Linux could crash (this is rare, but occurrences such as running out of hard disk space will still cause it).
- The Web server could crash.
- The power could go out in your building.
- Your telephone connection to your ISP could be cut off.
- Your ISP could have any one of these problems.

You should also consider how much it's worth to you to avoid avoid problems that might bring down your Web site. For example, you'd like to have an uninterruptible power supply for your server, but you have to balance how likely the the power is to go out in your area with how much these power supplies cost. Do you want ten minutes to shut down the Web server gracefully (which is useless if it happens in the middle of the night), or do you want 12 hours of backup for several thousand dollars?

In a wider sense, you should also consider disasters such as the following, which are harder to plan for but still possible scenarios:

- * An ex-employee destroys your Web site.

## Preparing a Disaster Plan

Without wanting to sound alarmist, the best way to avoid losing sleep over a system failure is to plan on having one. That is, prepare a plan for what you will do in case of each foreseeable failure. Implement that plan by following the actions that you decide. The plan can be a sticky note – the phone number of your friend who can fix anything—or in a red binder behind glass. The following sections outline two plans, one simple and a more complex. These plans will give you some ideas to base your own.
