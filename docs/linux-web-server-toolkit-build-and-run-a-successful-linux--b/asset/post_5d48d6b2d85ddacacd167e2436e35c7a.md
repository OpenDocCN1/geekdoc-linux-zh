
# Chapter 19: Maintaining Your Web Pages

THE LARGER YOUR Web site becomes, the more important it is to your organization. But large Web sites are a challenge to maintain effectively. When you have numerous people preparing documents for your Web site, maintaining your HTML pages can be a challenge. This chapter will help you inspect and update Web page content more effectively. Topics include:

- Recognizing problems
- Implementing maintenance processes
- Using spell checking tools
- Using HTML analysis tools

## Recognizing Potential Problems

By their very nature, large Web sites are extremely dynamic – that's why they're so
useful. But that's also why it takes so much work to maintain them. A major site with several dozen navigation and introduction pages and hundreds or thousands of other pages of data typically requires several full-time Webmasters busy building and maintaining pages.

Many Webmasters find it impossible to stay on top of constant information updates and changes in technology. As a result, any or all of the following may occur:

- Information on your site is not updated consistently. For example, product and promotional descriptions, prices, and dates are not current.
- There's a lack of interoffice communication, so vital information is not posted on time.
- Your pages contain errors in HTML code, which cause pages to display incorrectly or not at all on many Web browsers.
- Links to other sites are invalid because of HTML syntax errors or the page referred to that has moved or changed.
- Page creation was rushed, resulting in the posting of pages that contain bad grammar and spelling errors.

Of course, any one of these problems makes your site appear less than professional. So, what can you do to catch and correct them before your site gets an honorable mention in a "worst of the Web" list?

## Implementing Maintenance Processes

A good way to solve Web site maintenance problems before they arise is to implement an organized plan for creating and maintaining information on your Web site.

The unfortunate truth is that Web sites are often created by a lone visionary who is overwhelmed with the success of the project. As everyone rushes to participate, the basics, such as spell checking and spring cleaning, are often overlooked.

Each Web page, whether it is created within your organization or by an outside firm, needs to be checked for a variety of things, as listed in Table 19–1. The table includes some questions to keep in mind, but you should also consider questions geared towards your particular organization.

### Table 19–1 Web Page Analysis Questions

| Type of Analysis | Sample Questions |
| :--- | :--- |
| Graphics/Look and feel | Is the look of this page consistent with other pages on our site?<br>Is the page attractive?<br>Does the page place an undue burden on people with slow links? |
| Writing | Is this page well written?<br>Has it been spell checked and double-checked by a qualified proofreader? |
| Messages/Positioning | Does the information on this page fit our overall goals and messages as an organization?<br>Does the information on this page contradict anything we've said publicly (especially if it's on another page on our site)?<br>Have the necessary people (management) approved the content/messages of this page? |
| HTML | Have all cross-browser considerations been resolved?<br>Does this page contain HTML coding errors that will cause some elements to display incorrectly in major browsers?<br>Will this page support text-only browsers?<br>Are META tags adopted by our organization as standards used consistently?<br>Has the page passed an automated HTML check? |
| Legal | Do we have legal rights to any images or graphic elements?<br>Have any trademarks or other legal issues (quotes, etc.) been mentioned/linked/to/approved? |
| Links (After posting to the site) | Have all links on this page been checked to see that they are:<br>- Valid (correct HTML code)<br>- Correct (we refer to the page we expect to refer to)<br>- Active (actually display the page when selected; especially important to check when linking to Web sites other than yours)<br>- Worded consistently across pages |

Creating a plan for Web page maintenance can be as easy as specifying responsible parties for each of the checks listed in Table 19-1 and being very specific defining the tasks each person or persons are responsible for. You should also make sure to organize the flow of pages from creation to posting and beyond.

Following a plan can help you be more organized, but keep in mind that you may never be able to predict what will happen if your organization must move quickly to react to competition or changes in technology. You may find that, though you have a plan in place, the checks mentioned in Table 19-1 will be skipped because deadlines are tight, or perhaps your Web pages are posted two months after they were created (because you had to wait for approval from various departments), and so the information is outdated before it goes online.

It goes without saying that if pages are created as carefully and consciously as possible—messages are approved, the design is standardized, and the writing is good—checking several new Web pages should be done in no time.

When considering workflow, if a master editor is managing the Web page, current checks are fine; if no single person is responsible for the entire effort, it may be better to pass the pages in sequence to each person who must review or approve them, with a turnaround deadline to keep things moving and a person to contact if problems prevent granting approval.

The following steps outline a sample process for getting each new Web page approved. This example process might be overkill for your organization or it may not include everyone who needs to sign off on new pages.

- 1. Internal Web page creator, or design agency, supplies pages to Webmaster as .HTML and corresponding files and a print-out.
- 2. Webmaster posts the pages to the internal Web site, linked from the testing page (for example, http://internal.hotel/website/testing).
- 3. Webmaster sends e-mail to editor and marketing manager with URL and name of pages to review and deadline for completion of review.
- 4. Editor receives e-mail and reviews pages online and checks for grammar and spelling. Minor corrections are sent in e-mail; larger corrections may be done in the browser editor and a new page submitted. Editor notifies Webmaster that review is completed.
- 5. Marketing manager also reviews pages online, checking for appearance (appeal and consistency) and corporate messages. Any changes are sent in e-mail to the Webmaster. Marketing manager notifies Webmaster that review is completed.
- 6. Webmaster incorporates changes sent by editor and marketing manager.
- 7. Webmaster runs an HTML validation tool to check HTML coding of edited page, if it's perfect and then posts the page or fixes it if it isn't.
- 8. Webmaster makes sure that the links on all recently modified pages (fewer than two days since last modification) are checked during the slowest slowest period of use with other HTML validation tools.

> TIP
> The spell and HTML checking tools described in the next section can be a great help, but nothing can substitute for a skilled editor’s pen or the marketing manager’s careful review of messages.

## Using Spell Checking Tools

One of the most unprofessional errors on a Web site is also one of the easiest to correct: spelling errors. If an employee finds a spelling error in an internal company memo, it’s not a big deal, but a user finding one on a corporate Web site is equivalent to spelling errors in the annual report or in a weekly news magazine like Time. It catches your eye and says “These folks aren’t very careful—how can I trust them if they can’t spell?”

## Professional Tools

Whether your Web pages were created by an agency outside contractor or by someone in your organization, the final responsibility of making sure they are up to par rests with your organization. The old saying goes, never assume that the fellow who runs the printing press can spell it’s not his job to prove documents.

All that said, spelling is a lot easier to check now than ever before. Most of the commercial tools for developing Web pages like Adobe Site Mill, HotMetal, Pro, or various MS Word templates incorporate corporate spell checking into their systems. These professional tools will even ignore HTML tags and check the value of attributes without trying to check the names of the tags themselves.

Numerous stand-alone tools are also available specifically to spell check HTML skipping tags and only looking at the words that will appear on the screen in the browser. Several of these are mentioned in the section that follows about checking for correctness of HTML.
