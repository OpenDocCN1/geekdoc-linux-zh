UNIX system administration skills. Although this can be delegated in some cases, the Webmaster should have at basic UNIX skills. These include moving and copying files, changing access permissions, and editing text files. These basic operations are covered later in this book.

If not the Webmaster per se, someone nearby must have very strong UNIX skills – the kind I'm teaching in this book, or still for large sites. Because Web sites generate so much traffic and exposed to so many security risks, their maintenance can be much more rigorous than a simple UNIX host with 50 users.

+   Strong understanding of HTML. As with system administration, HTML tagging may be delegated, the Webmaster must know enough to spot problems when typos or errors in HTML code are reported.

+   Graphical ability. If your Webmaster is artistically challenged, keep a designer close at hand. Web sites rely so much on consistent graphics presentations that you don't want to wait on a designer nor have a nongraphical person creating the look and feel for your home page.

+   Maturity and discipline. A Web site may be the most visible part of your company. A wild-eyed Webmaster can do a great deal of damage to your image in an afternoon. Only responsible individuals who understand the magnitude of their “office” and can be trusted with that power should be managing your Web site.

This list of skills is not perfect for every organization, but it should give you an idea of the range of skills needed to run a good site. It also explains why Webmasters' earnings are so high (averaging over $60,000 annually in late 1996), and why many of them oversee a staff of a dozen people.

# Legal Considerations for Web Sites

First, the obligatory disclaimer: I am not licensed to practice law in any state. Free legal advice is what you pay for it. Okay, you've been warned.

Putting information on Web is about the most public thing you can do with. It's more public (but less embarrassing) shouting something on a street corner. For that reason, I recommend observing carefully the legal rules before the material you want to post. Many times, I've seen people post things to “working areas” on Web sites that they thought no one would see. Soon, they have a dozen e-mails with questions and comments on their half-finished Web pages. As if it weren't bad enough Web survive to dig and explore (even searching HTML code for hidden links on single characters and so forth), the spiders and search engines now index everything that is publicly visible on your site and create a link directly to it.
