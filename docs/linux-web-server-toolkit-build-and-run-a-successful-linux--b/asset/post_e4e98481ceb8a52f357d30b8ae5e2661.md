## Understanding Business on the Web

The Internet is changing the way people conduct business. I prefer not to think of it as the incredible revolution that the press declares it to be. Remember what I said in Chapter 1—less than ten percent of the American public are active users of the Internet. But regardless of that statistic, the Internet is affecting our country and the world. One of the best ways to judge trends is to see what the wealthy are doing, and like it or not, Web users outside business are generally in very high income and education brackets.

The changes ushered in by the Web are not shocking and sudden, as many investment bankers would have you believe when they discuss the latest Internet technology stocks. Television, failed by visionaries as a mighty change to our way of life, to develop networks, programming, signal distribution systems, and sales channels for receivers, advertising, and so forth before it was more than a novelty by many people, and before its effects approached the magnitude of what had been predicted. Furthermore, the effects of television were not exactly what futurists had foreseen. Their pictures of the future were either much more benign or more utopian (depending on whom you listened to) than reality turned out to be.

## Speed of Interchange

The Internet in general (via e-mail, chat rooms, and other services) and the Web in particular change the way the world works in several ways. Let’s back up little and consider the computer as a business tool.

The computer changed how businesses worked. One person could process so many orders, write so many more letters, and so forth. Expectations rose and business processes changed. But that was all within a single office.

Things like the fax machine and overnight package delivery continued to change business processes, this time between offices and companies. Changes don’t come easily for most people, of course. When a Harvard graduate first proposed an overnight package delivery service, his professor ridiculed the idea. Who would want things overnight?

The Internet is the culmination of this trend. You can exchange a letter, photographs, or any document with anyone in the world. You can do it instantaneously via e-mail or the Web. And you can do it at very low cost. You can post information where millions can view it, or encrypt a message so only the recipient can read it. All of these transactions take place without interference of customs officials or delays of mail. The Internet differs from the telephone and fax because you can send any type of information at low cost in virtually real time. A manager based in Hong Kong can click an icon and print a report that resides on a server located in London. A user can write a message in New York and have it on the screen of 500 colleagues all over the world in literally three minutes.

The Internet changes how people view delays and what they expect from the people they do business with. It’s no longer acceptable to blame the Post Office or even Federal Express. If something is ready, you can e-mail it to me immediately so that I can start reviewing it. There is no time buffer left. I’ll leave it to other specialists to examine the stress-induced illnesses this might cause.

The next logical step is a Star Trek transporter.

## Geographic Boundaries

Government boundaries always remain, but individuals are not nearly eager to separate themselves into groups that exclude others. The size of the world and the rules governing interchange among nations have always played a part in commerce. Customs duties, tariffs, export restrictions, and other similar rules interfere with what the business person would call free trade (this is a very Republican view, but it fits my discussion).

The Internet removes geographic boundaries for both individuals and businesses. Using the Web, a business in Bangkok seems as close to you as the you're in. Particularly in businesses that sell services or information, a company's market can immediately become the entire online world.

Of course, regulations still exist. Netscape can't export its browser freely, and you can't ship products anywhere just because you had a conversation via e-mail. The Internet remove geographic boundaries for both personal relationships and business relationships.

## Pull Versus Push

Advertisers are accustomed to finding the location of a target market and making themselves visible in that location, usually through advertisements. It might be a football field, a classic movie on TV, or a technical magazine. In common parlance, this is called a push: you get in the customer's face.

The Internet doesn't really allow this, because it's a medium where users go only where they want to go. There aren't many opportunities to drop your slogan in front of someone. Instead, the Internet uses what's called a pull: Each user chooses what he or she wants and pulls it to his screen. You can't push anything to the Web page visitor, because he or she is selecting specific information.

> TIP If you've been to business school, you'll recognize that these terms are the reverse of your marketing textbook. A standard push marketing strategy gives you an incentive to push a product to a customer. The salesperson helps the customer decide that he or she wants the product. A standard pull strategy means you advertise to the end user so that he goes into the store and pulls the product off the shelf (pulls through the distribution channel), without any pushing from the salesperson; the customer already knows he or she wants it.

Don't be discouraged by the pull nature of the Internet. It actually provides a great benefit: any users who do see your offerings are likely to be very interested potential customers. And there are ways to push your message to potential customers. Some are great (see the section on search engines in this chapter), and some are deplorable.
