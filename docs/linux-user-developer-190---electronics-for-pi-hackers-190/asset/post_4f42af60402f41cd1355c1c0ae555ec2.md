## equal medical devices

People in the Gaza Strip are dying through lack of medical care. **Chris Thornett** talks to the doctor using open source to combat this

Dr Tarek Loubani
Tarek is a physician and assistant professor at the University of Western Ontario. He also has the Glia projects.

**KEY INFO**
Tarek and the Glia team (https://glia.org/) develop high-quality, low-cost, open source medical hardware that is universally accessible. Recently, their work has focused on equipment to provide adequate medical care in the Gaza Strip.

What inspires Dr Loubani?
Opencode (openscod.org): "Who makes it a really hard time finding funds," says Tarek, "the fact that they underwrite many projects that we rely on."

![](img/0e3dcad8b24bb8f7fe3ae3a4d499db3b_31_0.png)

> "I see open source as my religion," says Tarek.

Tarek Loubani was back in Gaza when we came to speak to him about Glia, where he started open source hardware projects focusing on high-quality, low-cost medical. Tarek may live and work in Canada – he is an assistant professor at the University of Western – but his second life is with humanitarian work as a volunteer physician along the Mediterranean Sea, and as part of the Palestinian territories with the West Bank. Gaza is densely populated with over 2.5 million Gaz Palestinians living in 140 square miles.

Anyone who follows international news will know that it's part of the region that is under blockade and is governed by the Islamist group Hamas, which won the election in 2006. So soon in June in 2007, both Egypt and Israel imposed a blockade – and that has prevented the flow of commercial goods into the territory. Although restrictions have been reduced over time, any goods that are deemed capable of making weapons against Israel are banned, and basic supplies such as, food and medicine are in short supply. This has seriously damaged Gaza's economy and the welfare of its people. Regardless of the complexity of the situation – where the history of the conflict ranges over the years, Tarek says, the situation in Gaza showed us what was needed. "We needed open source tools that could be manufactured locally," he explains. "I worked in places where you get the feeling that this is best we can do, but in Gaza Strip it did feel like we hit the limits."

While Tarek was talking about this in 2012, he found himself working in a crisis: "All of a sudden there were hundreds of people coming; as the Israelis were building a building, and suddenly you'd have 50 people show up. There would be 10 of us physicians, maybe 100 patients and one stethoscope or oxygen intubation. The intention is that you use it to put a little comfort and help someone on life support."

# Expensive equipment

When it comes to medical devices, the question of why $300 exists immediately springs to mind. The invention is 120 years old and the last relevant patent was in the 1960s, why does a stethoscope cost $300? Largely because no high-quality generic products exist. Tarek says they went back to those that were used in the 1800s, which were very simple. This method was the generic for the invention of the stethoscope in the first time: Paris Rene Laenic had to put his head to the ears, he rejected the stethoscope and his fellow had had a few reasons to be reluctant, though; "People were full of blood," he says "My ear bled. I had to keep it off and I'd have been covered for 10 days afterwards. It was just to myself!"

"Creating a stethoscope that would be good as any other the next step," recalls Tarek and this turned out to be much easier than you might think. "It cost 10,000 euros to engineer and off we went. We validated it, compared it to the beat in class."
