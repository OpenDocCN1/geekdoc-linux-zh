Something else to bear in mind is that switches do not turn on and off cleanly. When you push a button, it might turn on and off several times in a rapid sequence. If you want to use it to turn an LED on and off it might not produce the desired effect. To make the operation reliable, it needs to be 'de-ounced', which causes the software to ignore any rapid changes. However, a better approach is to use software de-bounce, which is commonly provided below.

If your external circuit uses the same voltage as your computer - probably +5V or +3.3V and doesn't require much current, you can power it from the appropriate pins on the GPIO header. Better to check that your circuit won't exceed the power the GPIO pins can supply. If your circuit needs a higher voltage or will draw a higher current - as with motors or LEDs - you'll need an external power supply, although your circuit will still need to connect to an OV pin on the GPIO.

# Higher-current limits

LEDS can be driven by a GPIO pin using a current limiting resistor, but other loads, such as solenoids and motors, require more current and perhaps a higher voltage than SBCs are able to use. To turn high-current

loads on, you'll need to use an NPN transistor to drive a circuit like the one shown left. Outputting a current from the GPIO pin applies the voltage to the transistor, turning it on. It's important to connect the GPIO to the base via a current limiting resistor, otherwise it will draw too much current and damage the SBC. The transistor is effectively at OV, so the lowest value of the resistor must be calculated using

Ohm's law, if the GPIO pin is +5V when switched on, and the GPIO's maximum output current is 16mA (0.016A), the minimum resistor value is R = V / I = 5 / 0.016, which is 312Ω. In fact, unless a large value is required, a higher value of resistor could be used, and this will reduce current on the GPIO.

For example, let's assume a case where we want to drive a solenoid which draws a maximum of 1A. If we use a transistor with less than 1A, and a current limiting factor of no less than 100 (which the figure appears on, datasheet), then to turn the solenoid on a base of 10mA (0.01A) is needed. Back to Ohm's law, a resistor of R = V / I = 5 / 0.01 = 500Ω is needed. Given that the current of the transistor will be more than 100mA, we choose the next standard resistor value of 560Ω.
