# OPEN SOURCE GAME ENGINES

Like the Revenant in Doom II. Jonni Bidwell brings the corpses of lost video games back to life with a lil' bit of FOSS magic.

One of us Linux Format are as young as we used to be. But our considerable age means we remember some fantastic games.

Some of these go really back: Exile, Elite, Reptun, and the model Bundium and Maniac Mansion on the C64. Most of our youth playing Arena games, Monkey Island taught us to decompile, and who could forget those Bitmap Brothers classics: Xenon2, Speedball 2, and The Chaos Engine? Just talking about them makes us want to fire up an emulator and relive halcyon days.

But emulation can be clunky, and getting hold of ROMs and the like is tricky. Even with DOSBox the magic of open source though we can do better. Many classic titles have had their engines reverse engineered or revamped, so that the original game assets can be used seamlessly in a modern system. This can make for a completely authentic experience, or allow new features (graphics, proper network play) to be added.

More importantly, it enables us to show those young gamers what gaming was like back in the day. Before the days of running in a slick 3D environment, chasing in-game purchases, and to swear in Russian by listening to in-gamer chat.

The LucasArts adventure games have achieved legendary status. Its first graphical, 1987's Maniac Mansion, a new standard for the genre featured a bitmap-scrolling engine, immediately titled Script Creation for Mansion (SCUMM), which provided high-level routines for game primitives: characters, locations, and inventory. This not only made coders happy, as they could now write readable scripts rather than assembly, but also made the task of porting the game easier or less tedious to be.

SCUMM became famous because many SCUMM programmers were originally amateur programmers who had to learn a new language to get their ideas to work on the platform. By writing clear and portable code for SCUMM, coders Ron Gilbert and Gary Manson were able to write in a flexible scripting language that could be rapidly compiled for multiple platforms: C64, Amiga, PC and Mac. SCUMM also made possible kinds of things never seen before in the industry, such as interactive 'click' gameplay, multiple characters and background tasks/animations.
