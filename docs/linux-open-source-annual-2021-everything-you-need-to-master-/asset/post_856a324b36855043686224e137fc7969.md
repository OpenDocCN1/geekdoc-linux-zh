
## Video editors
The Verdict

With the exception of accidental overwriting or deleting a file, there is precious little that can go wrong when you test drive a new software, right? While this may be true for most benign software, such as text editors and web browsers, it certainly isn't the case with video editors of the ilk like that we have featured in this issue.

All the features involve a myriad combination of menus, tabs, sidebars and buttons. It would be unfair to work with these tools for us to declare any one style or appearance as superior to the others, because each of these tools will vary among user communities, which ultimately will favour your user experience.

More than enough, your choice of application will influence your workflow, because they are all well matched to which ones of what you can achieve with. Furthermore, your choice is something that will vary for individuals. If you're all like most people, you'll likely work with one tool, and as time you become comfortable with the art of film editing, you will find the different offers of each tool useful.

If you're a novice just starting with video editing, you might feel dazed by the sheer pool of possibilities. This is not without surprise, and it's the biggest reason for choosing Kdenlive as the winner.

Flowblade documentary wildly conflicts with its content, which makes it unlikely for new users to get very familiar with it at first. Although it is powerful, we can't recommend it to those who need it. For this reason Kdenlive would suffer a similar feel.

Openshot and Shotcut are nearly identical in all tests. The only real difference is that the latter has a novel feature, which each user should consider adopting. We've recently pushed LIVES off the option today to account for its lack of binaries for different distributions.

## ALSO CONSIDER

A quick look at the Wikipedia page for video editors will reveal quite a dozen active open source applications. Couple this with the new offers we present you you get a sizeable number of alternatives to choose from.

Cinelerner is a great-grade editor for absolute beginners. Aviden is fairly straightforward to use, but provides basic functionality. It's not as actively developed as the tools featured in this Roundup.

While we focus on the more established applications in this Roundup, Natron is a viable alternative. First since 2014 its interface is influenced by professional-grade software such as BlackMagic Fusion and Nuke.

Although it isn't a new release since 2016, Pitvi is another alternative. Available as a Flatpak package, the application was tightly integrated into Gnome desktop, but now works flawlessly with all desktops.

## Vector graphics

Vector-based drawing software is ideal for creating technical and business diagrams and illustrations. **Mike Bedford** put five packages to the test.

## HOW WE TESTED...

The most obvious differentiator between vector drawing packages is their more advanced graphics features, so it could be argued that any testing strategy should concentrate on them. However, a significant majority of users have little need for many of the more esoteric features; they really need just the basic tools for creating illustrations and diagrams for technical, scientific or business applications. Therefore, we have our emphasis.

That doesn't mean, however, that all software is equal. While the basic drawing tools might be similar, differences in areas related to production testing have also been considered. This goes far beyond the design of the user interface, including ease-of-use, for which the availability of tools and good documentation is vital, and features that simplify collaboration and sharing of artworks are vital.
