When evaluating algorithms at this level, they are classified by their order of growth as the input size grows. This provides insight into how well the algorithm will scale across input sizes, but disregards potentially significant disparities in performance.

For instance, consider two algorithms $A_0(n) \in O(n)$ and $A_1(n) \in O(n)$, that is, algorithms who belong to the linear asymptotic efficiency. By definition, the cost of both algorithms, in the worst case, is bounded by $c,d\exists n\neq0$ such that $A_0(n) \le c*n$ and $A_1(n) \le d*n$. Despite being in the same class, the difference between the two factors, $c$ and $d$, can be significant.

As a result, never rely on asymptotic classes alone to judge performance. As all other things, measure and verify.
