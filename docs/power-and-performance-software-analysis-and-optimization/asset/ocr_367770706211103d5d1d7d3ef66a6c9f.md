Variance can also be introduced by running the benchmark on a system running other processes that are consuming significant amounts of resources. This type of system is often referred to as a noisy system. These other processes can lead to issues such as increased CPU migrations or thermal issues. One way to mitigate this issue is to boot the Linux kernel in user mode, by adding “single” to the kernel command-line, or by adding init=/path/to/shell to the kernel command-line. Once the system has booted to this minimal configuration, the benchmark can be without interference from other processes. If the benchmark requires other processes to run, such as a graphical application requiring an instance of Xorg running, those will need to be started manually.

# 5.2.2 UNCONTROLLABLE EXTERNAL VARIABLES

As mentioned in Section 4.4, it is important, in benchmarking, to expose external variables that can affect performance. However, when designing experiments, it is also necessary to recognize that many external factors are outside of our immediate control.

Since these factors cannot be mitigated, they must be accounted for in the design of the experiment. To accomplish this, the benchmark should be run multiple times, and then multiple data points should be analyzed to quantify the effects of the external variables on the scales.

The number of data points required to be collected, in order to provide an accurate measurement, depends on how significantly the benchmark is affected by the uncontrollable variables. As a general rule of thumb, measurements at a smaller level of granularity require more runs than at a higher granularity. When in doubt, the author errs on the side of collecting too many data points, which wastes time but provides a more accurate measurement, rather than too few, which saves time but provides a potentially misleading measurement.

As in statistics, the distinction is between calculations involving the population and involving samples. The population represents the entire set of elements, whereas samples involve a subset of the population. To calculate the population mean of a benchmark would require averaging the result of every single run ever, an infinite set. Due to the impossible nature of this task, a sampling of the population is used. Statistics are calculated from the sample set in order to reason about the properties of the population. The law of large numbers dictates that as the number of data points within the sample increases, the sample’s behavior approaches the behavior of the total population (Renze and Weisstein, 2014).
