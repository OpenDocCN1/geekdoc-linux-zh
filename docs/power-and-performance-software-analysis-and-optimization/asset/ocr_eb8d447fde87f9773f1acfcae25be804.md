In order to accomplish this, Intel worked closely with the IEEE committee responsible for developing a standardized format for binary floating point arithmetic, the IEEE 754 standard. This standard, along with the international IEEE 559:1989 standard, which is identical in content, defines the floating point format and arithmetic behavior, as well as the minimum precision and environment required for a compliant implementation. The original IEEE 754 format was finalized in 1985 and remained unchanged for twenty-three years, until the standard extended in 2008. The 2008 revision to the standard remained most of the 1985 standard, and thus its content, along with the design of the 8087, still govern the format and behavior of floating point computations today, as of 2014. It is important to note that, despite the fact that the standard did much to improve the situation of floating point portability and consistency, the standard does not guarantee that different systems produce identical results.

Because the full 754 standard wasn't finalized until 1985, and the 8087 was released in 1980, the 8087 didnt technically implement the full standard. Despite this, the 8087 implemented the current draft at the time of its release and both the hardware and Intel played an important role in the development of that. Notably, William Kahn, who was on the IEEE committee and is an expert in numerical analysis, was contracted by Intel to help design 8087. The author recommends the reader visit Dr. Kahn's faculty at Berkeley, located at http://www.eeks.berkeley.edu/~wkahan/, and read some of his publications on the rationale and history behind the standard, and on accuracy and analysis in floating point computations.

Unlike an unsigned 16-bit integer, which only allows for the representation of whole numbers between $[0,2^{16})$ or $[-2^{15},2^{15}-1]$ respectively, floating point allows for representation of large and small numbers beyond the integer limits. As the name implies, floating point utilizes scientific notation, which decomposes a number into a significand and a signed exponent. For example, the number 0.0005 would be represented as $5.0 \times 10^{-4}$.

However, scientific notation introduces a problem, where the same number can be represented in multiple ways. $5000.0 \times 10^3$, $500.0 \times 10^4$ are all the same number. Obviously, this could be problematic when comparing two floating point numbers. In order to remedy this, floating point requires that the scientific format is normalized. A normalized scientific notation has only one single non-zero digit before the decimal point. In the previous example, $5.0 \times 10^{-3}$ would be the normalized format.

Up to this point, all of the previous examples of scientific notation have been in base 10, hence the $\times10$ component, however, all floating point numbers assume a base of 2. In order to encode a fraction in the significand in binary, each bit,$n$ represents $2^{-n}$. Because of the normalized binary format, it is unnecessary to physically represent the leading bit of the significand. Therefore, this bit is implied and an extra bit of precision is gained. The exception to this case, 0, is handled with a reserved exponent of 0.
