Novell wanted to be able to offer the same set of services as before, running on Linux and leveraging the power of the Linux development model, rather than being tied purely to its own development team. At the same time, Novell saw that many customers wanted Linux for its own sake and wanted to play in that market. It made a lot of sense to buy a Linux company and at the same time put a massive effort into porting all the traditional services associated with the name of Novell to Linux.

Novell's strategy is:

+   * To offer pure Linux to those who want them, with the assurance of the maintenance and support services that are available on SLES.
* To offer an upgrade to existing NetWare customers based on Linux, including the same services and configuration tools that they have become used.
* To offer this enhanced hybrid solution to new customers.

Novell recognized the power of the open source development method, and in general pleased the open source community by many of its actions in releasing various code bases as open source. However, at the same time the end of the software stack offered by Novell is quite unashamedly proprietary.

Open Enterprise Server (OES) consists of those proprietary elements, currently running on a choice of either Linux or NetWare. Novell has made no statements about how long the NetWare option will continue to exist, but one can only assume that in time (though the transition period may be long) Linux will eventually be the only choice for the underlying operating system.

In this chapter we take only a brief look at OES and what it offers. It is a large subject, encompassing most of Novell's large pieces of software. As the saying goes, it requires a whole book to itself. fact, Novell has already produced two large books on the subject, and more books from third parties are expected in the near future. In this chapter we also look only at the Linux-based version of OES.
