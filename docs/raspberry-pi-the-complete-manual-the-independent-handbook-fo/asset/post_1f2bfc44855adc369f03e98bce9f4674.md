## iBeacon scanning on Android

Various apps are available for Android users planning to communicate with their Raspberry Pi iBeacons. These include Locate Beacon, Beacon Detector and Beacon Scanner. As well as being compatible with the iBeacon, they also provide support for Eddystone, the Android answer to iBeacon, and AltBeacon, an open specification iBeacon alternative. Pictured at the lower-left of the opposite page is iBeacon & Eddystone Scanner (aka Beacon Scanner), which will scan for a nearby beacon following a simple button press. ## PiBeacon projects to consider

Various projects for Raspberry Pi-powered iBeacons can be developed, including systems that can assist with any smart home builds you have planned. Among these include things like garage door automation, switching on a device such as a lamp, or simply tracking the whereabouts of your Raspberry Pi. Note that the vast majority of projects will work with whatever mobile device you are using, so overlook the platform-specific details of detection whenever you’re researching such projects. To get you started, we’ll use the remainder of this tutorial to take a look at a couple of iBeacon projects you can build today.

## Making your iBeacon smart

You can take what you’ve done with the iBeacon so far even further with some smart interaction between your smartphone and Raspberry Pi, but where should you start? Rather than going headfirst into a DIY task, it pays to take your time finding the right app for your smartphone — one that will enable you to launch specific apps based on the presence of an iBeacon.

The tools we’ve looked at above are simply for detection and measurement, and the data they reveal is useful for developing trigger apps. But how do you launch an app when your phone is near an iBeacon?

## What an iBeacon can’t do

As things stand, for better or worse, you’re not in a Phillip K. Dick novel. This means that for the time being (at least), iBeacon technology cannot launch a web page on your phone without a third party tool (such as those mentioned above).

It is not beyond the realm of possibility that such a feature might be integrated into iOS or Android in the foreseeable future, a situation that might see an advert displayed on your phone as you pass an iBeacon, perhaps including a discount for shopping at the advertising store.

## iBeacon Trigger Apps

Whether you’re using iOS or Android, you’ll find a trigger app on the corresponding app store that can be set up to launch an app when your smartphone comes into proximity with your iBeacon. Android users should take a look at NFC Beam, available free from Google Play and intended for devices equipped with Bluetooth 4.0 hardware and running Android 4.3 or later. Meanwhile, iOS users should take a look at Beacon, available for a pittance (£1.59) in the App Store. This app has support for IFTTT, Philips Hue, HomeKit and Lux lights integration.

## Choosing iBeacon vs NFC

As you may have gathered by now, there are some similarities in use for an iBeacon and NFC, especially when it comes to smart home applications.

So which one should we use for your project?

Adept produces an NFC breakout board to add NFC functionality to the Raspberry Pi, which can prove very useful for any project that requires contact between an NFC tag and a piece of hardware — we grabbed ours from ModMyPi. And this is really where the distinction lies. If your project requires physical contact, then you should use NFC. Otherwise, the iBeacon solution should be completely adequate.

## Preparing for smart home hardware

Although a very useful addition to smart homes, the PiBeacon isn’t an instant miracle fix. While you might want to use it to open your garage door — perhaps with an IFTTT command that is sent once your smartphone comes into close proximity — without the right mechanical hardware connected to your Raspberry Pi, this sort of functionality simply won’t be possible to achieve.

Similarly, you’ll need to employ suitable electronic hardware to your PiBeacon to aid in the switching on and off of connected devices, such as a lamp. The PowerSwitch Tail II is such a device, and ships with jumper connectors for hooking it up to your Raspberry Pi GPIO.

## Weather-proof your PiBeacon

Smart home projects using Beacon technology may well need a bit of weather proofing. This might mean securing your Raspberry Pi away inside a suitable all-weather case, for example, or placing it somewhere sheltered, away from the elements.

Additionally, if no standard power supply is available, you’ll need to add a battery pack. You might consider a standard portable USB power supply, or prefer to use a 5V solution, such as that demonstrated in LUD6 issue 154. This explained how to power your Raspberry Pi with AA batteries, and covered the inclusion of a UBEC, a voltage regulator that protects the Raspberry Pi from being damaged by the batteries.

## PiBeacon, low profile

Thanks to the fact that a PiBeacon project really only needs a power supply and USB Bluetooth BLE dongle, you can rely on the less-featured or popular Raspberry Pi devices for any PiBeacon projects.

If you have an old Model A or Model A+ lying around at home, this would be ideal. And if you’re fortunate enough to have bagged a Raspberry Pi Zero for just £4, this too would be suitable. All you would need in addition is a USB to micro-B USB adaptor, which would then be connected to the Pi Zero’s micro-B USB for data port.

## Remote access

Type `ifconfig` into your Pi’s command line to find the IP address. At this point you can unplug the monitor and set it up remotely, but either way you can now access the Pi by typing:

```bash
$ ssh [user]@[IP address]
```

...and entering your password to log in.
