## Crop

The Crop tool works by enabling you to choose an image area and eliminate any unwanted edges by cutting them off, like trimming a photograph with scissors.

Open an image and click the Crop tool to activate it. Move the cursor over your image to the place that you want to begin the crop.

By clicking and dragging diagonally across the image area, you should notice rectangular lines forming an outline to show the selected crop area, as shown in Figure 6.4. As you release the mouse button, a crop window pops up on the screen with information about the crop area, including the size of the crop width and height, which can come in handy if you need an image to be a specific size. The crop window also shows the X and Y origin of the upper left point of the crop. Watch the rulers in the image window as you drag the crop area. The X (horizontal) origin is the amount from the left side of the image area, whereas the Y (vertical) origin is the pixel amount from the top of the image window to the top of the image crop area.

By pressing the Crop button located at the bottom of the crop window, you automatically trim off the extraneous edges from your image and crop it down to the chosen size. There is also a Selection button. This works by snapping the crop lines to the edges of an active selection area; it comes as close as can to the actual selection, yet still maintains the rectangular crop shape. You can then click the Crop button to perform the crop.

Two extra crop functions are in the menu. With an image open, right-click and go to Image, Transforms, and you can see autocrop, which automatically removes any solid colored borders from an image. The other is zealous crop, which cuts off areas of highly contrasting color surrounding an image, such as a border.

## Rotation

The first option you will look like is Rotation. This works pretty much as you would expect from the name, enabling you to rotate the image or selection by clicking and pulling it in the direction you want to. As you move, you should notice a small window pop showing the angle of the rotation. The Rotation tool is especially useful when working with scanned images that might not have been scanned perfectly straight. Look at Figure 6.6 to see a before-and-after version of an image that needed to be straightened with the Rotation option.

There are extra Rotation options in the menu. Right-click your image and go to Image, Transforms and you should find options that enable the rotation to be performed by degrees of 90, 180, and 270.

Holding down the Ctrl key while rotating, you can turn your image in 15-degree increments.

### FIGURE 6.6

![](img/0183f053b347a6407a2381be231d0345_97_0.png)

The Smoothing option of the Transform tool works somewhat like antialias as you perform the different transformations, blending the image as it changes. If you perform many rotations in one row, it can sometimes result in a blurry image because it is blending each one in turn. After you know the degree of rotation you want the image to end up with, you can get a sharper look if you go back to the original and rotate only once to the desired angle.

## Scaling

This sounds interesting, but I promise you won't find any snakeskin here. The Scaling function works by scaling your image or selection to a specific size. With scaling activated, the Scale options, as shown in Figure 6.7, show up as soon as you click your cursor on any corner of the image to begin the operation. The information includes the original image width and height, the current width and height which changes as you scale the image, and the X and Y ratios. If you want to merely scale the size of the image without stretching it out of shape one way or another, you must make sure the X and Y ratios match.
