# JOHN MORLEY FOUNDER'S DAY ADDRESS

(Abridged)

Carnegie Institute, Pittsburgh, Pa., November 3, 1904.

What is so hard as a just estimate of the events of our own time? It is only
now, a century and a half later, that we really perceive that a writer has
som to say for himself when he calls Wolfe's exploit at Quebec the
turning point in modern history. And to–day is hard to imagine any
rational standard that would not make the American Revolution—an
insurrection of thirteen colonies, with a population of 3,000,000
scattered in a distant wilderness among savages—a mightier event in many
of its aspects as the volcanic convulsion in France. Again, the rebuilding
of your great West on this continent is reckoned by some the most
important world movement of the last hundred years. But it is more
important than the amazing, imposing and perhaps disquieting apparition of
Japan? One authority insists that when Russia descended into the Far East
pushed its frontier on the Pacific to the forty–third degree of latitude
that was one of the most far–reaching facts of modern history, it almost
escaped the eyes of Europe—all her perceptions monopolized by
affairs in the Levant. Who can say? Many courses of the sun were needed

counter–reform. The center of gravity is forever shifting, the political
