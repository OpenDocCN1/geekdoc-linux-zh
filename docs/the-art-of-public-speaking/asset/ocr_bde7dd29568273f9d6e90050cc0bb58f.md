# CHAPTER IV

Selections for Practise Note: In the following selections, those passages that may be delivered in a moderate pitch are printed in ordinary (roman) type. Those which may be rendered in a high pitch—do not make the mistake of raising the voice too high—are printed in italics. which might well be spoken in a low pitch are printed in CAPITALS.

These arrangements, however, are merely suggestive—we cannot make it strong enough that you use your own judgment in interpreting a selection. Before doing so, however, it is well to practise these passages as they are marked.

_Yes, all men labor. RUFUS CHO AND DANIEL WEBSTER_ labor, says the critics. But every man who reads of the labor question knows that it means the movement of the men who earn their living with their hands; _THAT ARE EMPLOYED, AND PAID WAGES: gathered under roofs of cottages, sent out on farms, sent out on ships, gathered on the walls._ In popular acceptance, the working class means the men that work with hands, for wages, so many hours a day, employed by great capitalists; that work for everybody else. Why do we move for this class? "Why," asks a critic, "don't you move FOR ALL WORKINGMEN? BECAUSE, WHILE DANIEL WEBSTER GETS FORTY THOUSAND DOLLARS FOR ARGUING THE MEX CLAIMS, there is no need of anybody's moving for him. BECAUSE, WHILE RUFUS CHO GETS FIVE THOUSAND DOLLARS FOR MAKING ONE ARGUMENT TO A JURY, there is no need of moving for him, or for the men that work with their brains,—that do highly disciplined and skilled labor, invent, and write books. The reason why the labor movement confines itself to a single class is that class of work _DOES NOT GET PAID, and _MORE THAN ADEQUATELY protected. IT CAN SHIFT ITS CHANNELS; it can vary according to the supply and demand._
