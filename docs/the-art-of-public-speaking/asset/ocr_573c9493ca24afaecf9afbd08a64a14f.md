# CHAPTER II

It is useless to shoe a dead horse, and all the sound principles in Christendom will never make a live speech out of a dead one. So let it be understood that public speaking is not a matter of mastering a dead rules; the most important law of public speech is the necessity for truth, force, feeling, and life. Forget all, but not this.

When you have mastered the mechanics of speech outlined in the next few chapters you no longer be troubled with monotony. The complete knowledge of these principles and the ability to apply them will give you great variety in your powers of expression. But they cannot be mastered and applied by thinking or reading about them—you must practise, practise, PRACISE. If no one else listen to you, listen to yourself—you must always be your own critic, and the severest of all.

The technical principles that we lay down in the following chapters are not arbitrary creations of our own. They are all founded on the practices that good speakers and actors adopt—either naturally and unconsciously or under instruction—in getting their effects.

It is useless to warn the student that he must be natural. To be natural may be to be monotonous. The little strawberry up in the arctics with a few tiny seeds and an acid tang is a natural berry, but it is not to be compared with the improved variety that we enjoy here. The dwarf oak on the rocky hillside is natural, a poor thing compared with the beautiful tree found in the rich, moist bottom lands. Be natural—improve your natural gifts until you have approached the ideal, for we must strive after idealized nature, in fruit, tree, and speech.
