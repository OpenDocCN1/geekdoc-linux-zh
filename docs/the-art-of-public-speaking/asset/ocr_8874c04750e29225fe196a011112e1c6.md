More good and more ill been effected by persuasion than by any other form of speech. _It is an attempt to influence by means of appeal to some particular interest held important by the hearer._ Its motive may be high or low, fair or unfair, honest or dishonest, calm or passionate and hence its scope is unparalleled in public speaking.

This "instment of conviction," to use Matthew Arnold's expression, is naturally a complex process in that usually includes argumentation and often employs suggestion, the next chapter will illustrate. In fact, there is little public speaking worthy of the name that is not in some part persuasive, for men rarely speak solely to alter men's opinions—the ulterior purpose is almost always action.
