If you extemporize you can get much closer to your audience. In a sense, they appreciate the task you have before you and send out their sympathy. Extemporize, and you will not have to stop and fumble around your notes—you can keep your eye afar with your message and hold your audience with your very glance. You yourself will feel their response as you read the effects of your warm, spontaneous words, written on their countances.

Sentences written out in the study are liable to die and cold resurrected before the audience. When you create as you speak conserve all the native fire of your thought. You can enlarge on one point or omit another, just as the occasion or the mood of the audience may demand. It is not possible for every speaker to use this, the most difficult of all methods of delivery, and least of all it be used successfully without much practise, but it is the ideal towards which should strive.

One danger in this method is that you may be led aside your subject into—paths. To avoid this peril, firmly stick to your mental outline. Practise speaking from a memorized brief until you gain control. Join a debating society—talk, talk, TALK, and always extemporize. You may "make a fool of yourself" once or twice, but that is too great a price to pay for success?

Notes, like crutches, are only a sign of weakness. Remember that of your speech depends on some extent the view your audience holds of you. General Grant’s words as president were more powerful than his words as a Missouri farmer. If you would appear in the light of an authority, one. Make notes on your brain instead of paper.

Joint Methods of Delivery A modification of the second method has been adopted by many great speakers, particularly lecturers who are compelled to speak on a wide variety of subjects after day after; such speakers often commit their addresses to memory but keep their manuscripts in flexible book form before them, turning several pages at a time. They feel safer for having a sheet-anchor to windward—but it is an anchor, nevertheless, and hinders rapid, free sailing, though it drag never so lightly.
