The other five [examiners] were furious.... "You little tattie doolie," Cathro roared, "were there not a dozen words to wile from if you had an ill—will to pickle? What ailed at manzy, or—

"I thought of manzy," replied Tommy, woefully, for he was ashamed of himself, "but—but a manzy's swarm. It would mean that the folk in the kirk were buzzing together like bees, instead of sitting still."

"Even if it does mean that," said Mr. Duthie, with impatience, "what was the need of being so particular? Surely the art of essay–writing consists in using the first word that comes and hurrying on."

"That's how I did," said the proud McLauchlan [Tommy's successful competitor]....

"I see," interposed Mr. Gloag, "that McLauchlan speaks of there being a mask of people in the church. Mask is a fine Scotch word."

"I thought of mask," whimpered Tommy, "but that would mean the kirk was crammed, and I just meant it to be middling full."

"Flow would have done," suggested Mr. Lonimer.

"Flow's but a handful," said Tommy,

"Curren, then, you jackanapes!"

"Curran's no enough."

Mr. Lorrimer flung up his hands in despair.

"I wanted something between curran and mask," said Tommy, doggedly, yet almost at the crying.

Mr. Ogilvy who had been hiding his admiration with difficulty, spread a net for him. "You said you wanted a word that meant middling full. Well, why did you not say middling full—or fell mask?

"Yes, why not?" demanded the ministers, unconsciously caught in the net.

"I wanted one word," replied Tommy, unconsciously avoiding it.

"You jewel!" muttered Mr. Ogilvy under his breath, but Mr. Cathro would have banged the boy's head had not the ministers interfered.

"It is so easy, too, to find the right word," said Mr. Gloag.

"It's no; it's difficult to hit a squirrel," cried Tommy, and Mr. Ogilvy nodded approval.

And then the odd thing happened. As they were preparing to leave the school [Cathro having previously run Tommy out by the neck], the door opened a little and there appeared in the aperture the face of Tommy, tear-stained but excited. "I ken the word now," he cried, "it came to me a' at once; it is hantle!"

Mr. Ogilvy ... said in himself, "He had to think of it till he got it—and he got it. The laddie is a genius!"

# CHAPTER XXVIII

# MEMORY TRAINING

Lulled in the countless chambers of the brain,
Our thoughts are linked by many a hidden chain; Awake but one, and lo! what myriads rise! Each stamps its image as the other flies!

********

Hail, memory, hail! in thy exhaustless mine
From age to age unnumber’ treasures shine! Thought and her shadowy brood thy call obey,
And Place and Time are subject to thy sway!

> --SAMUEL ROGERS, Pleasures of Memory.

Many an orator, it Thackeray, has made the best part of his speech to himself—on the way home from the lecture hall. Presence of mind—it remained for Mark Twain to observe—is greatly promoted by absence of body. A hole in the memory is no less a common complaint than a distressing one.

Henry Ward Beecher was able to deliver one of the world’s greatest addresses at Liverpool because of his excellent memory. In speaking of the occasion Mr. Beecher said that all the events, arguments and appeals that he had ever read or written to pass before his mind as oratorical weapons, and standing there had but to reach for his hand and "seize the weapons as they went smoking." He had to repeat all he had written. Scaliger memorized the Iliad in three weeks. Locke says: "Without memory, man is a perpetual infant." Quintilian and Aristotle regarded it as a measure of genius.

Now all this is very good. We all agree that a reliable memory is an invaluable possession for the speaker. We never dissent for a moment when we are solemnly told that his memory should be a storehouse from which he can draw facts, fancies, and illustrations. But can the memory be trained to act as the warder for all the truths that we gained from thinking, reading, and experience? And if so, how?

Twenty years ago a poor boy, employed as a dish washer, wandered into the library and began to read. His memory was not good, but he began to train it. He found that by paying attention, he could remember what he read. He became a great memory expert. He could tell the population of any town, the date of any important event, and the names of strangers who had just been introduced. He could recall the events of history in order. He found that by paying attention, he could remember what he read. He became a great memory expert. He could tell the population of any town, the date of any important event, and the names of strangers who had just been introduced.

The secret of remembering is attention. When we pay attention to something, we remember it. If we do not pay attention, we forget it. This is true for everything, not just for memory. The more attention we pay, the better we will remember it.

The over-use of unusual words is a common mistake. It is better to use simple words than to use difficult ones. It is better to be clear than to be clever. The goal of writing is to be understood.

The, is not to force the memory.

Again, it is hurtful to force the memory by learning words by rote. For years our schools were cursed by this system—not only because it is inefficient but for the more important reason that it hurts the mind. True, some minds are natively endowed with a facility in remembering strings of words, facts, but such are rarely reasoning minds; the normal person must train the memory to acquire ideas.

Finally, do not become a slave to a system. Knowledge of a few simple principles of memory will set you to work at the right end. Use these principles, but do not bind yourself to a method that tends to lay stress on the way to remember than on the development of memory itself. It is nothing of ridiculous to memorize words in order to remember one fact.
