Not only must we discern the difference between human force and more physical force, but we must not confuse its real essence with some of the things that may—and may not—company it. For example, loudness is not force, though force at times may be attended by noise. Mere roaring never made a good speech, yet there are moments—moments, mind you, not minutes—when big power may be used with tremendous effect.

Nor is violent motion force—yet force may result in violent motion. Hamlet counselled the players:

Nor do not saw the air much with your hand, thus; but use all gently; for in the violent torrent, tempest, and (as I may say) whirlwind of your passion, you must acquire and beget a temperance, that may give you smoothness. Oh, it offends me to the soul, to hear a robust periwig-fated fellow tear a person to tatters, to very rags, to split the ears of the learnings[2]; who, for the most part, are capable of nothing inexplicable dumb show, and none would have such a fellow whipped for o’er-doing Termagant; it out—herods Herod. Pray you avoid it.

Be not too tame, neither, but let your discretion be your tutor: suit the action to the word, the word with this special observance, that you overstep not the modesty of nature; for anything so overdone is from the purpose of playing, whose end, both at the first, and now, and is, to hold, as ’tere, the mirror to Nature, to show Virtue her own feature, Scorn her own image, the very age and body of the time his form and pressure. Now, this overdone, or come tardy, though it make the unful laugh, cannot make the judicious grieve; the censure of the which one must, in your allowance, o’weigh a whole theater of others. Oh, there be players that I have seen pray—and others praise, and that highly—not to speak profanely, that having the accent of Christians, nor the gait of Christian, pagan, or man, have strutted and bellowed that I have thought some of Nature's journeymen had made men, and not made them well, they imitated humanity so abominably.[3]
