I saw one of these scarecrows once and never tire thinking about it. I was at Niagara Falls lecturing, and after the lecture I went to the hotel, and when I went up to the desk there stood a millionaire's son from New York. He was an indescribable specimen of anthropologic potency. He carried a gold-headed cane under his arm—more in his head than he had in him. I do not believe I could describe the young man if I should. But I still must say that he wore an eye-glass he could not see through; patent leather shoes he could not walk in, and pants he could not sit down—dressed like a grasshopper. Well, this human cricket came up to the clerk's desk just as I came. He adjusted his unseen eye-glass in this wise and lisped to the clerk, because it's "Hinglish, you know," to lisp: "Thir, thir, will you have the kindness to fuhnish me with papah and those envelopes!" The clerk measured that man quick, and he pulled out a drawer and took some envelopes and cast them across the counter and turned away to his books. You should have seen that specimen of humanity when the paper and envelopes came across the counter—he wants had always been anticipated by servants. He adjusted his unseen eye-glass and he yelled after that clerk: "Come back here, thir, come right back here. Now, thir, will you order a servant to take that papah and those envelopes and carry them to yond debth." Oh, poor miserable, contemptible American monkey! He couldn't carry paper envelopes twenty feet. I suppose he could not get his arms down. I have no pity for such travesties of human nature. If you have no capital, I am glad of it. You don't need common sense, not copper cents.

A.T. Stewart, the great princely merchant of New York, the richest man in America in his time, was a poor boy; he had a dollar and a half went into the mercantile business. But he lost eighty—seven and a half cents of his first dollar and a half because he bought some needles and buttons to sell, which people didn't want.

Are you poor? It is because you are not wanted and are left on your own hands. There was the great lesson. Apply whichever way will it comes to every single person's life, young or old. He did not know what people needed, and consequently bought something they didn't want and had had the goods left on his hands a dead loss. A.T. Stewart learned the great lesson of his mercantile life and said, "I will never buy anything until I first learn what the people wanted; then I'll make the purchase." He went around to the doors and asked them what they wanted, and when he found out what they wanted, he invested his sixty-two and a half cents and began to supply "a known demand." I care not what your profession or occupation in life may be; I care not whether you are a lawyer, a doctor, a housekeeper, teacher or whatever else, the principle is precisely the same. We must know what the world needs first and then invest ourselves to supply that need, and success is almost certain. A.T. Stewart went until he was worth forty millions. "Well," you will say, "a man can do that in New York, but cannot do it in Philadelphia." The statistics carefully gathered in New York in 1889 showed one hundred and seven millionaires in the city over ten millions apiece. It was remarkable and people think they must go there to get rich. Out of that hundred and seven millions only seven of them made their money in New York, and the others moved to New York after their fortunes were made, and sixty-seven out of the remaining made their fortunes in towns of less than six thousand people, and the richest man in the country at that time lived in one of thirty-five hundred inhabitants, and always lived there and never moved away. It is not so where you are as you are. But at the same time if the largeness of the city comes into the problem, then remember it is the smaller city that furnishes the great opportunity to make the millions of money. The best illustration that I can give is in reference to John Jacob Astor, who was a poor boy and who made all the money of the Astor family. He made more than his successors have ever earned, yet he once held a mortgage on a winery in New York, and because the people could not make enough money to pay the interest and the rent, he foreclosed the mortgage and took possession of the store and went into partnership with the man who had failed. He kept the same stock, did not give them a dollar capital, and he left them alone and went out and sat down upon a bench in the park. There out on bench in the park he had the most important, and to my mind, the pleasantest part of partnership business. He was watching the ladies as they went by; and where is the man that wouldn't get rich at that business?

But when Jacob Astor saw a lady pass, with her shoulders back and her head up, as if she did not care if the whole world looked on her, she studied her bonnet; and before that bonnet was out of sight he knew the shape of the frame and the color of the trimmings, the curl of the—something on a bonnet. Sometimes I try to describe a woman's bonnet, but it is out of style. So John Jacob Astor went to the store and said: "Now, put in the show window just such a bonnet as I describe to you because," said, "I have just seen a lady who likes just such a bonnet. Do not make up any more till I come back." And he went out again and sat on that in the bench, and another lady of a different form and complexion faced with a bonnet of different shape and color, of course. "Now," said he, "put such a bonnet as that in the show window." He didn't fill his show window with hats and bonnets which drive people away and then sit in the back of the store and bowl because the people go somewhere else to trade. He didn't put a hat or bonnet in that show window the like of which he had not seen before it was made up.

In our city especially are great opportunities for manufacturing, and the time has come when the line is drawn very sharply between the stockholders of the factory and their employees. Now, friends, there has also come a discouraging gloom upon this country and the labor men are beginning to feel that they are being held down by a crust over their heads through which they find it impossible to break, and aristocratic money—owner himself is so far away that he will never descend to their assistance. That is the thought that is in the minds of our people. But, friends, never in the history of our country was there an opportunity so great for the poor to get rich as there is now in the city of Philadelphia. The very fact that they get discouraged is what prevents them from getting rich. That is all there is to. The road is open, and let us keep it open between the poor and the rich. I know that labor unions have two problems to contend with, and there is only one way to solve them. The labor unions are doing as to prevent its solving as are the capitalists to—day, and there are positively two sides to it. The labor union has two difficulties; the first one is that it begins to make a labor scale for all classes on a par, and they scale down a man that can earn five dollars a day to two and half day, in order to level up to him imbecile that cannot earn fifty cents a day. That is one of the most dangerous and discouraging things for the working man. He cannot get the results of his work if he do better work or work longer; that is a dangerous thing, and in order to get every labor man free and every American equal, let the labor man ask what he is worth and get it—not let any capitalist say to him: "You shall work for me for half you worth." Be a man, be independent, and then shall the labor man find the road ever open from poverty to wealth. The other difficulty that the labor union has to consider, and this problem they have to solve themselves, is the kind of orators who come and talk to them about the oppressive rich. I can in my dreams recite the oration I have heard again under circumstances. My life has been with the labor man. I am a labor man myself. I have often, in their assemblies, heard the speech of the man who has been invited to address the labor union. The man gets up before the assembled company of labor man and he begins by saying: "Oh, you honest, industrious labor men, who have furnished all the capital of the world, who have built all the palaces and constructed all the railroads and covered the ocean with her steamships. Oh, you labor men! You are nothing but slaves; you are down in the dust by the capitalist who is gloating over you as he enjoys his beautiful estates and as he has his banks filled with gold, and every dollar he owns is coined out of the heart's blood of the honest labor man." Now, that is a lie, and you know it is a lie; and yet it is the kind of speech that are all the time hearing, representing the capitalists as wicked and the labor men so enslaved. Why, how wrong it is! Let the man who loves his flag and believes in American principles endeavor with all his soul to bring the capitalist and the labor man together until they stand side by side, and arm in arm, and for the common good of humanity.

He is an enemy to his country who sets capital against labor or labor against capital.
