Grady told us, and told us truly, of that typical American who, in Dr. Talmage's mind's eye, was coming, but who, in Abraham Lincoln's actuality, had already come. In some recent studies into the career of that man, I had encountered startling confirmations of this judgment; and from that rugged trunk, drawing its sustenance from gnarled roots, interlocked with Cavalier sprays and Puritan branches deep beneath the soil, shall spring, is springing, a shapely tree—symmetric in all its parts—whose sheltering boughs this nation have the new birth of freedom Lincoln promised it, and mankind the refuge was sought by the forefathers when they fled from oppression. Thank God, the ax, the gibet, and the stake had their day. They have gone, let us hope, to keep company with the lost arts. It has been demonstrated that great wrongs may be redressed and great reforms be achieved without the shedding of one drop of human blood; that vengeance does not purify, but brutalizes; and that tolerance, which in private transactions is reckoned a virtue, becomes in public affairs a dogma of the most far-seeing statesmanship.

I appeal from the men in silken hose who danced to music made by slaves—and called it freedom—from the men in bell-crowned hats, who called Hester Prynne for her shame—and called religion—to that Americanism which reaches forth to its arms to smite with reason and truth, secure in the power of both. I appeal from the patriarchs of New England to the poets of New England; from Endic to Lowell; from Winthrop to Longfellow; from Norton to Holmes, and I appeal in the rights of that common citizenship—of that origin—back of the Puritan and the Cavalier—to which all of us owe being. Let the dead past, consecrated by the blood of its martyrs, not by its savage hatred—darkened alike by kingcraft and priestcraft—let the dead past bury its dead. Let the present and the future ring with the song of the singers. Blessed be the lessons they teach, the laws they make. Blessed be the eye to see, the light to reveal.
