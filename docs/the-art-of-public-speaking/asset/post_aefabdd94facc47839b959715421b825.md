"Plenty of them, plenty of them; all you have to do is just go and find them, then you have them." Al Hafed said, "I will go." So he sold his farm, collected his money at interest, left his family in charge of a neighbor, and went away in search of diamonds. He began properly, to my mind, at the Mountains of the Moon. Afterwards he went around Palestine, wandered into Europe, and at last when his money was all spent, and he was in rags, wretchedness and poverty, he stood on the shore of that bay Barcelona, Spain, when a tidal wave came rolling through the Pillars of Hercules and the poor afflicted, suffering man could not resist the awful temptation to cast himself into that incoming tide, and he sank beneath its foaming crest, never to rise in this life again.

When that old guide told me that very sad story, the camel I was riding and went back to the baggage on one of the camels, and I remember thinking to myself, "Why did he reserve that for his _particular friends_?" There seemed to be no beginning, middle or end—nothing to it. That was the first story I ever heard or read in which the hero was killed in the first chapter. I had but one chapter of that story and the hero was dead. When the guide came back and took the halter of my camel again, he went right on with the same story. He said that Al Hafed's successor led his camel into the garden and as that camel put its nose down into the clear water of the garden brook Al Hafed's successor noticed a curious flash of light from the sands of the shallow stream, and reaching in he pulled out a black stone having an eye of light that reflected all the colors of the rainbow, and he took that curious pebble into the house and left it on the mantel, then went on way and forgot all about it. A few days after that, this same old priest told Al Hafed how diamonds were made, came to visit his successor, when he saw that flash of light from the mantel. He rushed up and said, "Here is a diamond—here is a diamond! Has Al Hafed returned?" No, no; Al Hafed has not returned and that is not a diamond; that is nothing but a stone; we found it right here in our garden." "But I know a diamond when I see it," said he; "that is a diamond"

Then together they rushed to the garden and stirred up the white sands with their fingers and found others more beautiful, more valuable diamonds than the first, and thus, said the guide to me, were discovered the diamond mines of Golconda, the most magnificent diamond mines in all the history of mankind, exceeding the Kimberley in its value. The great Kohinoor diamond in England's crown jewels and the largest crown diamond on earth in Russia's crown jewels, which I had often hoped she would have to sell before they had peace with Japan, come from that mine, and when the old guide had called my attention to that wonderful discovery he took the Turkish cap off his head again and swung it around the air to call my attention to the moral. Those Arab guides have a moral to each story, though the stories are not always moral. He said, Al Hafed remained at home and dug in his own cell or in his own garden, instead of wretchedness, starvation, poverty and death in a strange land, he would have had "acres of diamonds”—for every acre, yes, every shovel of that old farm revealed the gems which since have decorated the crown of monarchs. When he had given his moral to story, I saw why had reserved this story for his "particular friends." I didn't tell him he could see it; I was not going to tell that Arab that I could see it. For it was that old Arab's way of going around a thing, like a lawyer, and saying indirectly what he did not say directly, that there was a certain young man that day traveling down the Tigris River that might better be home in America. I didn't tell him he could see it.

The best illustration that I have now of this thought was found in Pennsylvania. There was a man living in Pennsylvania who owned a farm here and what he should do if I had a farm in Pennsylvania—he sold it. But before he sold it he concluded to secure employment with his cousin in Canada. They first discovered oil there. So this farmer in Pennsylvania decided he would apply for a position with his cousin in Canada. Now, you see, this farmer was not altogether foolish. There is no more foolish than a man who leaves one job before he has obtained another. I say this old farmer did not leave one job until he had another. He wrote to his cousin, but his cousin replied that he could not engage him because he did not know anything about oil business. "Well, then," he said, "I will understand it." So he set himself at the study of the whole subject. He began at the second day of creation, he studied the subject from primitive vegetation until he knew all about it. Then he wrote to his cousin and said, "Now I understand the oil business." And his cousin replied to him, "All right, then, come on." That man, by the record of the county, sold his farm for eight hundred and thirty-three dollars—even money, I say, "no sense."

I need another illustration, and I found that in Massachusetts, and I am sorry I did, because that is my old State. This young man went out of the State to study—went down to Yale College and studied Mines and Mining. They paid him fifteen dollars a week during his last year for training students who were behind classes in mineralogy, out of hours, of course, while pursuing his own studies. But when he graduated raised his pay from fifteen dollars to forty-five dollars and offered him a professorship. Then he went straight home to his mother and said, "Mother, I won't work for forty-five dollars a week. What is forty-five dollars a week with a brain like mine! Mother, let's go to California and stake gold claims and be immensely rich." "Now," said his mother, "it is just as well to be happy as it is to be."

But as he was the only son who had his way—they always do; and they sold out in Massachusetts and went to Wisconsin, where he went into the employ of the Superior Copper Mining Company, and he was lost from sight in the employ of that company. He also had to have an interest in any mines that he should discover for company. But I do not believe that he has ever discovered a mine—I do not know anything about it, but I do not believe he has.
