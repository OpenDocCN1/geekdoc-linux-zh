The movements of the facial muscles may make a great deal more than the movements of the hand. The man who sits in a dejected heap with a look of despair on his face is expressing his thoughts and feelings just as effectively as the man who is waving his arms and shouting from the back of a dray wagon. The eye has been called the window of the soul. Through it shines the light of our thoughts and feelings.

Do Not Use Too Much Gesture As a matter of fact, in the big crises of life we do not go through many actions. When your closest friend dies you do not throw up your hands and talk about your grief. You are more likely to sit and brood in dry-eyed silence. The Hudson River does not make noise on its way to the sea—it is not so loud as the little creek in Bronx Park that a bullfrog could leap across. The barking dog never tears your trousers—at least say he doesn't. Do not fear the man who waves his arms and shouts his anger, but the man who comes quietly with eyes flaming and fire burning may knock you down. Fuss is not force. Observe these principles in nature and practice them in your driving.

The writer of this chapter once observed an instructor driving in a class of gesture. They had come to the passage from Henry VIII in the humbled Cardinal sais: "Farewell, a long farewell to all my greatness." It is one of the pathetic passages of literature. A man uttering such a sentence would be crushed, and the last thing on earth he would be to make flamboyant movements. Yet this class had an elocutionary manual before that they gave an appropriate gesture for every occasion, from paying the bill to death–bed farewells. So they were instructed to throw their arms out at full length on each side and say: "Farewell, a long farewell to all my greatness." Such a gesture might be used in an after–dinner speech at the convention of a telephone company whose lines extended from the Atlantic to the Pacific, but to think of Wolsey's using movement would suggest that his fate was just.
