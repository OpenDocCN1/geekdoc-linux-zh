The vast results obtained by science are won by no mystical faculties, by no mental processes, other than those which are practised by every one of us in the humblest and meanest affairs of life. A detective policeman discovers a burglar by the marks made by his shoe, by a mental process identical to that by his bones Cuvier restored the extinct animals of Montmartre from fragments of their bones. Nor does that process of induction and deduction by which a lady, finding a stain of a particular ink upon her head, concludes that somebody upset the inkstand then, differ in any way from that by which Adams and Legverrier discovered a new planet. The man of science, in fact, simply uses with meticulous exactness the methods which we all habitually, and at every moment, use carefully.

> --THOMAS HENRY HUXLEY, *Lay Sermons.*

Do you set down your name in the scroll of youth, that are written down old with all the characters of age? Have you not a moist eye? a dry hand? a yellow cheek? a white beard? a decreasing leg? an increasing belly? is not your voice broken? your wind short? your chin double? your wit single? and every part you about basted with antiquity? and will you yet call yourself? Fie, fie, fie, Sir John!

> --SHAKESPEARE, *The Merry Wives of Windsor.*

Finally, in preparing expository material ask yourself these questions regarding your subject:

What is it, and what is it not? What is it like, and unlike? What are its causes, and effects? How shall it be divided? With what subjects is it correlated? What experiences does it recall? What examples illustrate it?
