
# Part III, Annual Report of the Secretary of Internal

His affectionate nature, craving ever friendship, as well as the presence of kindred blood, diffused itself through all his private life, gave sincerity to all his hospitalities, kindness to his eye, warmth to the pressure of his hand, made greatness and genius unbound themselves to the playfulness of childhood, flowed out in graceful memories indulged of the past or the dead, incidents when life was young and promised to be happy,—gave generous sketches of his rivals,—the high contention now hidden by the handful of earth,—hours passed fifty years ago with great authors, recalled the vernal emotions which they made to live and revel in the soul. And from these conversations of friendship, no man—no man, old or young—went away to remember one word of profanity, all allusion of delicacy, one impure thought, one unbelieving suggestion, one doubt cast on the reality of virtue, of patriotism, of enthusiasm, of the progress of man,—one doubt on righteousness, or temperance, or judgment to come.

I have learned by evidence the most direct and satisfactory in the last months of his life, the whole attentativeness of his nature—his consideration of others, his gentleness, his desire to make them happy and to see them happy—seemed to come out in more beautiful and habitual expressions than ever before. The long day's public tasks were felt to be done; the cares, the uncertainties, the mental conflicts of high place, were ended; and he came home to recover himself for the few years which he might still expect would be his before he should go to be no more. And there, I assured and duly believe, uncoming regrets pursued him; no discontent, as for justice suffered or expectations unfulfilled; no self-reform for anything done or anything omitted by himself; no irritation, no peevishness unworthy of his noble nature; but instead, love and hope for his country, when he became the subject of conversation, and for all around him, the dearest and most indifferent, for all breathing things about him, the overflow of the kindest heart growing in gentleness and benevolence—paternal, patriarchal affections, seeming to become more natural, warm, and communicative every hour. Softer and brighter grew the tints on the sky of parting day; and the lingering rays, more even than glories of noon, announced how divine was the source from which they proceeded; how incapable to be quenched; how certain to rise on a morning which no night should follow.

Such a character was made to be loved. It was loved. Those who knew and saw it in his hour of calm—those who could repose on soft green—loved him. His plain neighbors loved him; and one said, when he was laid in his grave, "How lonesome the world seems!" Educ loved him. The ministers of the gospel, the general intelligence of the country, the masses afar oft, loved him. True, they had not found in his speeches, read by millions, so much adulation of the people; so much of the music which robs the public reason of itself; so many phrases of humanity and philanthropy; and some told him he was lofty and cold—solitary in his greatness; but every year they came nearer and nearer to him, as they came nearer, they loved him; how tender the son he had been, the husband, the brother, the father, the friend, and neighbor; that he was plain, simple, natural, generous—the heart larger than the brain; that he little revered God, the Scriptures, the Sabbath—day, the Constitution, and the law—and their hearts clave unto him. More truly of him than even of the great naval darling of England it might be that "his presence would the church bells ringing, and give schoolboys a holiday, would bring children from school and old men from the chimney—corner, to gaze on him he died." The great and unavailing lamentations first revealed the deep place he had in the hearts of his countrymen.

You are now to add to this his extraordinary power of influencing the convictions of others by speech, and you have completed the survey of the means of his greatness. And again, I begin by admiring an aggregate made up of excellences and triumphs, ordinarily deemed incompatible. He spoke with consummate ability to the bench, and yet exactly as, according to every sound carol of taste and ethics, the bench ought to be addressed. He spoke with consummate ability to the jury, and yet exactly as, according to every sound carol, that totally different tribunal ought to be addressed. In the halls of Congress, before the people assembled for political discussion in masses, before audiences smaller and more select, assembled for some solemn commemoration of the past or of the dead—in each of these, again, his speech, of the first form of ability, was exactly adapted, also, to the critical properties of the place; each achieved, when delivered, the most instant and specific success of eloquence—some of them in a splendid and remarkable degree; and yet, stranger still, when reduced to writing, as they fell from his lips, they compose a body of reading in many volumes—solid, clear, rich, and full of harmony—a classical and permanent political literature.

And yet these modes of his eloquence, exactly adapted each to its stage and its end, were stamped with his image and superscription, identified by characteristics incapable of being counterfeited and impossible to be mistaken. The same high power of reason, intent in every one to explore and discover some truth; some truth of judicial, or historical, or biographical fact; some truth of law, deduced by construction, perhaps, or by illation; some truth of policy, for want hereof a nation, generations, may be the worse—reason seeking and unfolding truth; the same tone, in all, of deep earnestness, expressive of strong desire what he felt to be important should be accepted as true, and spring up to action; the same transparent, plain, forcible, and direct speech, conveying his exact thought to the mind—nothing less or more; the same sovereignty of form, of brow, and eye, and tone, and manner—everywhere the intellectual king of man, standing before you—that marvelousness of qualities and results, residing. I know not where, in words, pictures, in the ordering of ideas, Velocities indescribable, by means whereof, coming from his tongue, all things seemed meant—true seemed more, probability more plausible, greatness more grand, goodness more awful, every affection more tender than coming from other tongues—these are, in all, his eloquence.

But sometimes it became individualized and discriminated even from itself; sometimes place and circumstances, great interests at stake, a stage, audience fitted for the greatest historic action, a crisis, personal or national, upon him, stirred the depths of emotional nature, as the anger of the goddess stirs the sea on the great epic is beginning; strong passions themselves kindled to intensity, quickened every faculty to a new life; the stimulated associations of ideas brought all treasures of thought and knowledge within command; the, which often held his imagination fast, dissolved, and as she arose and gave him to choose of her urn of gold; earness became vehement, the simple, perspicuous, measured and direct language became a headlong, full, and binding tide of speech; the discourse of reason, wisdom, gravity, and beauty changed to that superhuman, the rarest eloquence—grand, rapid, pathetic, terrible; the aliquid immensum infinitque Cicero might have recognized; the master triumph of man in the rarest opportunity of his noble power.

Such elevation above himself, in congressional debate, was most uncommon. Some such were there in the great discussions of executive power following the removal of the deposits, which they heard they will never forget, and which rest in the tradition of hearers only. But there were other fields of oratory on which, under the influence of more uncommon springs of inspiration, he exemplified, in still other forms, an eloquence in which I do not know that he has a superior among men.
