Part III, Annual Report of the Secretary of Internal

# ROBERT TOOMBS ON RESIGNING FROM THE SENATE, 1861

(Abridged)

The success of the Abolitionists and their allies, under the name of the Republican party, has produced its logical results already. They have for long years been sewing dragons' teeth and finally got a crop of armed men. The Union, sir, is dissolved. That is an accomplished fact in the path of this discussion that men may as well indeed. One of your confederates has already wisely, bravely, boldly confronted public danger, and she is only ahead of many of her sisters because of her greater facility for speedy action. The greater majority of those sister States, under like circumstances, consider her as their cause; and I charge you in your name to-day: "Touch not Saguntum."It is not only your cause, but it is a cause which receives the sympathy and will receive the support of tens and hundreds of honest patriot men in the nonslavholding States, who have hitherto maintained constitutional rights, and who respect their oaths, abide by respects, and love justice.

And while this Congress, this Senate, and this House of Representatives are debating the constitutionality and the expediency of seceding from the Union, and while the perfidious authors of this mischief are showering down denunciations upon a large portion of the patriotic men of this country, brave men are calmly voting what you call revolution—aye, sir, doing better than that: arming to defend it. They appealed to the Constitution, they appealed to justice, they appealed to fraternity, until the Constitution, justice, and fraternity were no longer listened to in the legislative halls of their country; and now you see the glittering bayonet, and you hear the tramp of armed men prepared for the arbitration of the sword; and now you see from your capitol to the Rio Grande. It is a sight that gladdens the eyes and cheers the hearts of millions ready to second them. Inasmuch, sir, as I have labored earnestly, honestly, sincerely, with these men to avert this necessity so long as I deemed it possible, and inasmuch as I heartily approve their present conduct of resistance, I deem it my duty to state their case to the Senate, to the country, and to the civilized world.
