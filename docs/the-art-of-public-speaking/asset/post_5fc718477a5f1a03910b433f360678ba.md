Did you ever notice in looking from a train window that some horses feed near the track and never even pause to look up at the thundering cars, while just ahead at the next railroad crossing a farmer's wife will be nervously trying to quiet her scared horse as the train goes by?

How would you cure a horse that is afraid of cars—graze him in a back–woods where he would never see engines or automobiles, or drive him in a pasture where he would never see them?

The only way to cure a horse that is afraid of cars is to expose him to cars. The only way to cure a person who is afraid of speaking is to expose him to speaking. A book may give you excellent suggestions on how best to conduct yourself, but sooner or later you may get wet, perhaps even strange "half scared to death." There are great many "wetless" suits worn at the seashore, but no one ever learns to swim in them. The only way to learn to swim is to plunge into the water.

Practise, practise in speaking before an audience will tend to remove all fear of audiences, just as practise in swimming will lead to confidence and facility in the water.

The Apostle Paul tells us that every man must work out his salvation. All we can do here is to offer you suggestions as how best to prepare yourself. A doctor may prescribe, but you must take the medicine.

Do not be disheartened if at first you suffer from stage–fright. Patch was more susceptible to suffering than a superannated horse would be. It never hurts a fool to appear before an audience, for his capacity is not capacity for feeling. A blow that would kill a civilized man soon heals. The higher we go in the scale of life, the greater is the capacity for suffering. For one reason or another, some master speakers never overcome stage–fright, but it will pay you to spare no pains to conquer it. Webster failed in his first appearance and had to take his seat without finishing his speech because he was nervous. Beecher was always perturbed in the beginning of an address before public.

Blacksmiths sometimes twist a rope tight around the nose, and by inflicting a little pain they distract his attention from the shoe. One way to get air out of a glass is to pour in water.

### Absorbed by Your Subject

Apply the homely principle when you are speaking: If you feel deeply about your subject you will be able to think of little else. Concentration is a process of distraction from less matters. It is too late to think about the cut of your coat when you are upon the platform; centre your interest on what you are to say—fill your mind with material, and like filling water in the glass, will drive out unsubstantial fears.

Self-consciousness is undue consciousness of self, and for the purpose of delivery, itself is secondary to your subject, not only in the opinion of the audience, but if you are wise, in your own. To hold any other view is to regard yourself as an exhibit instead of a messenger with a message worth delivering. Do you remember Hubbard's tremendous little tract, "A Message to Garcia?" So must you, by all the determination you can muster, subordinate yourself to the message. Say this to yourself sternly, and shame your self-consciousness into quiescence. If you caught fire you could rush to the stage and show directions to the audience without any self-consciousness, for the importance of what you were saying drive all fear out.

Self-consciousness through doing poorly is worse than self-consciousness through doing well. The first sign of greatness is when a man does not attempt to look and act great. Before you can call yourself a man at all, Kipling assures us, you must "not look good nor talk wise."

Nothing advertises itself so thoroughly as conceit. One may be full of self as empty. Voltaire said, "We must conceal self-love." That cannot be done. You know this to be true, for you have recognized overweening self-love in others. If you have it, others see it in you. There are things in this world bigger than self, and in working for them will be forgotten or—what is better—remembered only as to help us win toward higher thoughts.

### Have Something to Say

The trouble with many speakers is that they go before an audience with their minds blank. It is no wonder that nature, abhorring a vacuum, fills them with the nearest thing handy, generally happens to be, "I wonder if I am doing this right! How does my hair look? I know I shall fail." Their prophetic souls are to right.

It is not enough to be absorbed by your subject—to acquire confidence you must have something in which to be confident. If you go before an audience without any preparation or previous knowledge of your subject, you ought to be self-conscious—you ought to be ashamed to steal the time of your audience. Prepare yourself. Know what you are going to talk about, and, in general, how you are going to say it. The first few sentences worked out completely so that you may not be troubled in the beginning to find words. Know your subject better than your hearers know it, and you have nothing to fear.

Let your bearing modestly confident, but of all modestly confident within. Over-confidence is bad, but to tolerate premonitions of failure is worse, for a bold man may win attention by his very bearing, while a rabbit-hearted coward invites disaster.

Humility is not the personal discount that we must offer in the presence of others—against this old interpretation there has been a most modern reaction. True humility any man who thoroughly knows himself must feel; but it is not a humility that assumes a worm-like meekness; it is rather a strong, vibrant prayer for greater power for service—a prayer that Uriah Heep could never have uttered.

Washington Irving introduced Charles Dickens at a dinner given in the latter's honor. In the middle of his speech Irving hesitated, became embarrassed, and sat down awkwardly. Turning to a friend beside him remarked, "There, I told you you would fail, and I did."

If you believe you will fail, there is no hope for you. You will.

Rid yourself of this I-am-a-poor-worm-in-the-dust idea. You are a god, with infinite capabilities. "All things are ready if the mind be so." The eagle looks the cloudless sun in the face.

### Assume Mastery Over Your Audience

In public speech, in electricity, there is a positive and a negative force. Either you or your audience are going to possess the positive factor. If you assume you can you almost invariably make it. If you assume the negative you are sure to be negative. Assuming a virtue or a vice vitalizes it. Sum all your power of self-direction, and remember though your audience is infinitely more important than you, the truth is more important than both of you, because it is eternal. If your mind falters in its leadership the sword will drop from your hands.
