Stewart learned the great lesson of his mercantile life and said, "I will never buy anything until I first learn what the people wanted; then I'll make the purchase." He went around to the doors and asked them what they wanted, and when he found out what they wanted, he invested his sixty-two and a half cents and began to supply "a known demand." I care not what your profession or occupation in life may be; I care not whether you are a lawyer, a doctor, a housekeeper, teacher or whatever else, the principle is precisely the same. We must know what the world needs first and then invest ourselves to supply that need, and success is almost certain. A.T. Stewart went until he was worth forty millions. "Well," you will say, "a man can do that in New York, but cannot do it in Philadelphia." The statistics carefully gathered in New York in 1889 showed one hundred and seven millionaires in the city over ten millions apiece. It was remarkable and people think they must go there to get rich. Out of that hundred and seven millions only seven of them made their money in New York, and the others moved to New York after their fortunes were made, and sixty-seven out of the remaining made their fortunes in towns of less than six thousand people, and the richest man in the country at that time lived in one of thirty-five hundred inhabitants, and always lived there and never moved away. It is not so where you are as you are. But at the same time if the largeness of the city comes into the problem, then remember it is the smaller city that furnishes the great opportunity to make the millions of money. The best illustration that I can give is in reference to John Jacob Astor, who was a poor boy and who made all the money of the Astor family. He made more than his successors have ever earned, yet he once held a mortgage on a winery in New York, and because the people could not make enough money to pay the interest and the rent, he foreclosed the mortgage and took possession of the store and went into partnership with the man who had failed. He kept the same stock, did not give them a dollar capital, and he left them alone and went out and sat down upon a bench in the park. There out on bench in the park he had the most important, and to my mind, the pleasantest part of partnership business. He was watching the ladies as they went by; and where is the man that wouldn't get rich at that business?
