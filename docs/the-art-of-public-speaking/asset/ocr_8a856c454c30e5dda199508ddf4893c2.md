In his "Knickerbocker's" "History of New York," Irving portrays Wouter Twiller as "a robust beer—barrel, standing on skis."

Whatever forms of description you neglect, be sure to master the art of suggestion.

_Description may be by simple hint._ Lowell notes a happy instance of this sort of picturing by intimation when he says of Chaucer: "Sometimes he describes amply by the merist, as where the Friar, before setting himself down, drives away the cat. We know without of more words that he has chosen the snugest corner"

_ depict a thing by its effects._ "When the spectator's eye is dazzled, and he shades it," says Mozley in "Essays," "we form the idea of a splendid object; when his face turns pale, of a horrible one; from his quick wonder and admiration we form the ideal of great beauty; from silent awe, of great majesty.

_Brief description may be by epithet._ "Blue-eyed," "white-armed," "laughter-loving," are now conventional compounds, but they were fresh enough when Homer first joined them. The centuries have not yet improved upon "Wheels round, brazen, eight-spoked," or "Shields smooth, beautiful, brazen, well-hammered." Observe the effective use of epithet in When Will Livingston Comfort's "The Fighting Death," when he speaks of soldiers in a Philippine skirmish as being "leeched against a rock."
