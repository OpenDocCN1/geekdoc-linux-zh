Now let's go one step further in on the fly compression demonstration. How about putting a few of these steps altogether? Recall our imaging over the network with netcat. If you look at the different sizes of our compressed vs. uncompressed images, you'll see there quite a difference in size (which will, of course, depend on the compressibility of the data on the volume being imaged). Do you think it might be faster to compress data before sending over the network? Let's find out.

Going back to our simple network setup, let's do the same imaging, but this we'll pipe to gzip -c on one side of the network and gzip -cd on the other, effectively sending compressed across the wire. The resulting image is NOT compressed. We decompress before it reaches the imaging tool. You can elect to leave that if you like and simply write a compressed image.

We'll start by hashing the subject hard drive again from our boot disk. Assuming the network settings are correct, and opening our netcat listener and dc3dd process on the collection box:

```
root@bootdisk:# sha1sum /dev/sda
8fcec10ae87d6ff4590ba809ba51358679738ed /dev/sda
```

Open the listening process, redirecting the output to a file. I'm using dc3dd with hof= to collect and output hash to compare the sha1 above.

```
root@forensic:/testcomp nc -l -p 2525 | gzip -cd | dc3dd hash=sha1 hof=ncgzuncomp.raw log=ncgzuncomp.log
```

And now start the imaging on the subject box:

```
root@bootdisk:# dd if=/dev/sda | gzip -c | nc 192.168.0.1 2525
```

When the imaging is complete, we can check the resulting dc3dd log, ncgzuncomp.raw, to verify the integrity of the resulting image:

```
root@forensic:/testcomp ls -lh ncgzuncomp.raw
-rw-r--r-- 1 root 12G M 6 13:46 ncgzuncomp.raw
```

```
root@forensic:/testcomp cat ncgzuncomp.raw
```


A couple of things to notice here. First, our hashes match. We successfully read a device, compressed the data, piped it over a network, decompressed and wrote an image file. The reason we do this is to save time. Have a look at the time it took to image (from the log file) and compare it against our earlier using netcat without compression:

Without compression (from previous exercise): (dd | netcat)
12884901888 bytes ( 12 G ) copied (100%), 747.491 s, 16 M/s

With compression: (dd | gzip | netcat)
12884901888 bytes ( 12 G ) copied (100%), 522.142 s, 24 M/s

Almost four minutes savings by compressing the data before transporting it. Keep in mind that the usefulness of this is dependent on your particular bottlenecks. On a local network, by crossover cable, and writing to a USB 2.0 drive, compressing the network may have little impact. If you are imaging over an enterprise network, remotely, you may see a performance gain from compression. Your results may vary, but be aware of the technique.

# Preparing a Disk for the Suspect Image

We've already covered simple acquisitions, and media sanitization is a step that is normally performed before you conduct evidence collection. It is introduced here because it makes more sense to cover the subject of imaging when introducing imaging tools rather than introducing drive wiping before we've covered the tools we'll use.

On to wiping... We can use a special device, /dev/zero as a source of zeros. This can be used to create empty files and wipe partitions of disks. You can write zeros to an entire disk (or at least to areas accessible to the kernel and user space) using the following command (assuming /dev/sdc is the disk you want to wipe):

```
root@forens1:~# dd if=/dev/zero of=/dev/sdc bs=32k
dd: error writing '/dev/sdj': No space left on device
64945 records in
64944 records out
2128084992 bytes (2.1 GiB, 2.0 GiB) copied, 294.083s, 7.2 MB/s
```

This starts at the beginning of the drive and writes zeros (the input file) to every sector on /dev/sdc (the output file) in 32 kilobyte chunks (bs = block size). Specifying larger block sizes can speed the writing process (default is 512 bytes). Experiment with different block sizes.

# Final Words on Imaging

Anyone who has worked in the forensic field for any length of time can tell you that the acquisition process is the foundation of our business. Everything else can be cross verified and validated after the fact. But you only get one shot at a proper acquisition. You may have a limited amount of time on site, or one shot at recovering data from a disk drive. Make sure you understand how the tools work, and what the options actually do. Validating your approach prior to using it in live field is essential.

This section has introduced a number of basic tools and a rough skillset. Requirements and procedures vary from jurisdiction to jurisdiction and across organizations. Know the requirements of your particular governing body, and adhere to them.

Keep in mind also that technological advances will change much of how we do acquisitions. Solid state media and storage technologies are more than just changes to the interface that may require an adapter. The way data is physically stored and data blocks manipulated is a constant evolution. The entire approach to "obtaining an exact duplicate" changing as storage methods and technologies advance. Don't get too comfortable!
