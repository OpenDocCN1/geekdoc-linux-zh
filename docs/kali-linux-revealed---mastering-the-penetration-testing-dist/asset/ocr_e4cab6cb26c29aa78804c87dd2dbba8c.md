In 2005, WHoppIX evolved into WHAX, with a expanded and updated toolset, based on the "more modular SLAX (Slackware) live CD." Muts and a growing team of volunteers from the hacker community to realize that no matter how insightful they were, they could never anticipate the growth and fluctuation of industry and that their CD would have varied needs in the field. It was obvious that Muts and his team were actually using WHAX in the field, and they seemed dedicated to making it work for.

In 2006, Muts Moser, and his team consolidated Auditor Security Linux and WHAX into a single distribution called BackTrack. Still based on SLAX, BackTrack continued to grow, adding more tools, more frameworks, extended language support, extensive wireless support, a menu structure catering to both novice and pro users, a heavily modified kernel. BackTrack became the leading security distribution, but many still used it as a backup for their "real tools."

By early 2009, Muts and his team had extended BackTrack to BackTrack 4. Now a full-time job for Muts, BackTrack was no longer a live CD but a full-blown Ubuntu-based distribution leveraging the Ubuntu software repositories. The shift marked a serious evolution: BackTrack 4 had an update mechanism. In Muts' own words: "When syncing with our BackTrack repositories, you will regularly get security tool updates after they are released"

This was a turning point. The BackTrack team had tuned into the struggling pen testers, forensic analysts and others working in our industry. Their efforts would save countless hours and provide a firm foundation, allowing us to get back into the fight and spend more time doing the important (and fun) stuff. As a result, the community responded by flocking to the forums and wiki; by pitching in the dev team. BackTrack was truly a community effort, with Muts leading the charge.

BackTrack 4 finally became an industrial-strength platform and I, and others like me, breathed with a sigh of relief. We knew firsthand the "pain and suffering" Muts and his team were bearing, because we had been there. As a result, many of us were using BackTrack as a primary foundation for our work. We, still fiddled with tools, wrote our own code, and developed our own exploits and techniques; and we researched and experimented; but we did not spend all our time collecting, updating, validating, and organizing teams.

BackTrack 4 R1 and R2 were further revisions in 2010, leading to the ground-up rebuild of BackTrack 5 in 2011. Still based on Ubuntu, and picking up with every release, BackTrack was now a massive project that required a heroic volunteer and community effort but also funding. Muts launched Offensive Security (in 2006) not only to provide world-class training and testing services but also to provide a vehicle to keep BackTrack development rolling, and ensure that BackTrack remained open-source and free to use.

BackTrack continued to grow and improve through 2012 (with R1, R2, and R3), maintaining an Ubuntu core and adding hundreds of new tools, including physical and hardware exploitation tools, VMware support, countless wireless and hardware drivers, and a multitude of improvements and bug fixes. However, after the release of R3, BackTrack development went relatively, and somewhat mysteriously, quiet.
