We spent sharpening our skills and expertise but we wouldn't have been successful without organization and efficiency. We would have failed if we couldn't put our hands on the proper tool when needed.

That's why I spent so much time researching, documenting, testing, and cataloging tools, and at the turn of the 21st Century, it was quickly becoming an overwhelming, full-time job. Thanks to the Internet, the worldwide attack surface exploded and the variety and number of attack tools increased exponentially, by the dealt required to maintain them.

Starting in 2004, the Internet exploded not only as a foundation for business but also as a social platform. Computers were affordable, more consumer-friendly and ubiquitous. Storage technology expanded from megabytes to gigabytes. Ethernet jumped from hundreds of kilobytes to megabytes per second, and Internet connections were faster and cheaper than ever. Ecommerce was on the rise, social media sites like Facebook (2004) and Twitter (2006) came online and Google (1998) had matured to the point that anyone (including criminals) could just find anything online.

Research became critical for teams because we had to keep up with new attacks and toolsets. We responded to more computer crimes, and forensic work demanded that we treat lightly as we through potential evidence. The cost of a live CD meant that we could perform forensics on a compromised medium without compromising evidence.

Our little team had to keep up with all the latest attack and exploit methodologies; and we had to, you know, what do we paid for—penetration tests, which were in high demand. Things were spinning out of control, and before, we were spending less time in battle and much more time researching, sharpening our tools, and planning.

We were not alone in this struggle. In 2004, Mati "Muts" Aharoni, a hacker and security professional released "WhoHix" (White Hat Knoptix), a live Linux CD that was the "ultimate pen testing live CD. it included all the exploits from SecurityFocus, Packet Storm and k-otik, Metasploit Framework 2.2, and much, more."

I remember downloading WhoHoppix and thinking it was a great to have. I downloaded other live CDs, thinking that if I were in a real pinch, live CDs could save me bacon in the field. But I wasn't about to rely on WhoHoppix or any other CD for real work. I didn't trust any of them to fulfill the majority of my needs; none of them felt right for my workflow; they were not full installable distributions; the moment I downloaded they were out of date. An aged toolset is the kiss of death in our industry.

I simply added these CDs, despite their relatively massive size, to our arsenal and kept the painful process of maintaining our "real" toolkit.

But despite my personal opinions at the time, and perhaps despite Muts' expectations, WhoHix and its descendants had a seismic impact on his life, our industry, and our community.
