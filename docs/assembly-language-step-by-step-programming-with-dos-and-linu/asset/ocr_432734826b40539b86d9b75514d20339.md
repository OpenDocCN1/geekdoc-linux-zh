Understanding the ways and the limitations of CPU registers is something like following the Kosovo peace process: There are partnerships, alliances, and always a bewildering array of secret agents that each register follows. I devote most of a chapter to registers later in this book.

Most peripherals also have registers, and peripheral registers are even more limited in scope than CPU registers. Their agendas are quite explicit and in no wise secret. This does not prevent them from being confusing, as anyone who has tried programming the VGA video board at the register level will attest.

# The Assembly Line

If the CPU is the foreman, then the peripherals are the assembly-line workers, and the data bus is the assembly line itself. (Unlike most assembly lines, however, the foreman works the line as hard or harder than the rest of his crew!)

As an example: Information enters the computer through a modem peripheral, which as bits received from the telephone line into bytes of data representing characters and numbers. The modem places the assembled byte onto the bus, from which the CPU picks it up, tallies it, and then places it back on the data bus. The video board then retrieves the byte from the bus and writes it into video memory so that you can see it on your screen.

Obviously, lots is going inside the box. Continuous furious communication along the data bus between CPU, memory, and peripherals is what accomplishes the work that the computer does. The question then arises: Who tells the foreman and crew what to do? You do. How do you do that? You write a program. Where is the program? It's in memory, along with all the rest of the data stored in memory. In fact, the program is data, and that is the heart of the whole idea of programming as we know it.
