Magic? Not at all. Work it out on paper by expressing a number as a bit pattern (that is, in binary form), shifting the binary pattern one bit to the right, and then converting the binary form to decimal or hex. Like so:

```
00101001    Binary equivalent of 35H, 53 decimal
<--- by one bit yields
01101010    Binary equivalent of 6AH, 106 decimal
```

Sharp readers may have guessed that shifting to the right **divides** by powers of two—and that's also correct. Shifting right by one divides by 2; shifting right by two bits divides by 4, and so on.

The advantage of multiplying with shift instructions is that it's fast. Even on the oldest and slowest x86 CPUs, shifting a byte-sized value in a register to the left by one bit *only two machine cycles*—2... as opposed to 77 (on those same older machines) with MUL. And **MUL can do not better than 13 cycles even on the newest Pentium-class processors.

As we say, no contest.

Once the index is multiplied by two with **SHL**, the index is added to the starting address of the table, just as with **Digits**. **MOV** copies the correct segment address from the table into AX, for return to the caller.

This illustrates how you can realize enormous advantages by structuring your tables properly. Even if it means a little wasted space at the end of each element, do your best to make the length of your table elements equal to some power of two. That means making each element 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, or some larger power of two in size, but not 3, 7, 12, 20, or 25 bytes in size.
