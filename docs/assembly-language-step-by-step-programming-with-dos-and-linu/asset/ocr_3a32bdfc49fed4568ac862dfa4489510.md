Some would say that the CPU is what actually does the work, but that's
anversimplification. Plenty of real work is done in the memory sys-
tem, and especially in what are called peripherals, such as video display
bords, serial and parallel ports, and modes. So, while the CPU does
do a good deal of the work, it parcels out a bit to other compon-
 within the computer. I think its role of foreman outweighs its
assemble-line grunt.

Most of the CPU chips used in the machines lump together as a
group and all PCs come from a company called Intel, which pretty
much invented the single-chip CPU back in the early 1970s. Intel's first
bang-up success was the 8080, which helped trigger the personal com-
puter revolution by being chosen for the seminal MIT Altair 8800 com-
puter in Popular Electronics in 1974. The 808 was
an-bit- because it accessed memory 8 bits (1 byte) at a time.
8080 is long extinct, but gave birth to a pair of next-generation
CPU chips the 8086 and the 8088. These two chips are nearly
identical except that the 8088 is an 8-bit CPU, while the 8086 is a 16-bit
CPU, and accesses a word (2 bytes) at a time. IBM chose
8088 for its original 1981 IBM PC and later the PC XT, but the 8086
made it into a true IBM computer until the forgettable (and largely
forgotten) PS/2 models 25 and 30 appeared in 1987.
