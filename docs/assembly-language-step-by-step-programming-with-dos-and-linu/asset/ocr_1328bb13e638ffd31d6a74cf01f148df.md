The early 86 CPUs including the 8086 and 8088 are full of such promises. There were not enough transistors available at design time to allow all general-purpose registers to do everything, so in addition to the truly general-purpose ability to hold data, each 8086/8088 register has what I call a "hidden agenda." Each register has some ability that none of the others share. I describe each register's hidden agenda at some appropriate time in this book, and I call it out as such.

In the 20-odd years since the 8086 was created, Intel has hugely expanded the power of its x86 family of CPUs. And sure, when you get into 32-bit protected mode, most of the limitations imposed by early transistor budgets go away, and general-purpose registers become almost completely general. However, when acting in real mode (as we are speaking of here), the Pentium, 486, and 386 CPUs take on just all the characteristics of the 8086 and 8088, including this sort of limitation, which is built into the logic that decodes the instruction set for real mode.

Should you, then, learning this sort of bad-old-days limitation? I think so. What it teaches you that limitations exist and need to be remembered. Even the mighty Pentium II has limitations and restrictions. You need to develop a grasp of them, or you'll be floundering around why things don't work.

# Using Memory Data

With one or two important exceptions (the string instructions, which I cover to a degree—but not exhaustively—later on), only one of an instruction's two operands may specify a memory location. In other words, you can move an immediate value to memory, or a memory value to a register, or some other similar combination, but you cant move a memory value directly to another memory value. This is just an inherent limitation of the CPU, and we have to live with it, inconvenient as it gets at times.
