
The question that kept gnawing at me was exactly what sort of beast RAX (the computer's wonderfully appropriate name) actually was. What we had were ram-charged typewriters that RAX controlled over phone lines—that much I understood. But what was RAX itself?

I asked the instructor. In brief, the conversation went something like this:

ME: "Umm, sir, what exactly is RAX?"

HE: "Eh? Um, a computer. An electronic computer."

ME: "That's what it says on the course notes. But I want to know what RAX is made of and how it works."

HE: "Well, I'm sure RAX is all solid-state."

ME: "You mean, there's no levers and gears inside."

HE: "Oh, there may be a few. But no vacuum tubes."

ME: "I wasn't worried about tubes. I suppose it has a calculator in it somewhere. But what makes it remember that A comes before B? How does it know what FORMAT means? How does it tell time? What does it have to do to answer the phone?"

HE: "Now, come on, that's why computers are great! They put it all together so that we don't have to worry about that sort of thing! Who cares what RAX is? RAX knows FORTRAN and will execute any correct FORTRAN program. That's what matters, isn't it?"

He was starting to sweat. So was I. End of conversation.

That June I graduated with three inches of debugged and working FORTRAN punch cards in my bagbook, and still had absolutely no clue to what RAX was.

It has bothered me to this day.

## Gus to the Rescue

I was thinking about RAX six years later, while on the Devon Avenue bus heading for work, with the latest copy of Popular Electronics in my lap. The lead story involved a little thing called the COSMAC ELF, which consisted of a piece of perfboard full of integrated circuit chips, all wired together, plus some toggle switches and a pair of LED numeric displays.

It was a computer. (Said so right on the label, heh.) The article told us how to put it together, and that was about all. What did those chips do? What did the whole thing do? It was driving me mad.

As usual, my friend Gus Flassig got on the bus at Ashland Avenue and sat down beside me. I asked him what the damned thing did. He was the first human being to make the concept hang together for me:

“These are memory chips. You load numbers into the memory chips by flipping them in switch code patterns. Each number means something to the CPU chip. One number makes it add; another number makes it subtract; another makes different numbers into memory, and lots of other things. A program consists of a bunch of these instruction-numbers in a row in memory. The computer reads the first number, does what the number tells it to do, and then reads the second one, does what that number says to do, and so on until it runs out of numbers.”

If you don't find that utterly clear, don't worry. I had the advantage of being a hobbyist (so I knew what chips did) and had already written some programs in RAX. For me, God, everything suddenly hit critical mass and exploded in my ears. No matter what, I knew that it had to be something like the COSMAC ELF on a larger scale. It allowed me to understand the nature of computers at a deep level. I don't recommend that anybody wirewrap their own machines anymore, although it was common enough to do in the late seventies. ## Switches, Transistors, and Memory

Switches remember. Think about it. You flip the switch by the door, and the light in the middle of the ceiling comes on. It stays. When you leave the room, you flip the switch again, and the light goes out. It stays. The switch will remain in the position you last left until you or someone else comes back and flips it to its other position. In a sense, it remembers its last command until you change it, and “overwrite” that command with a new one. This represents a sort of rudimentary element. Light switches are more mechanical than electrical. This does not prevent them from acting as memory; in fact, the very first computer (Babbage’s nineteenth-century Difference Engine) was entirely mechanical. In fact, the larger version he designed but never finished was steam-powered. Babbage’s machine had lots of little cams that could be flipped from one position to another. Numbers were encoded and remembered as patterns of cam positions.

## One if by Land

Whether a switch is mechanical, or electrical, or something else is irrelevant. What counts is that a switch contains a pattern: on or off; up or down; flow or no flow. That pattern can be assigned memory. Paul Revere told his buddy to set up a code in the Old North Church: “One if by land, two by sea.” Once, the lamps remained lit (and thus remembered that very important code) long enough for Paul to call out the militia and warn the British. The transistor itself occupies almost no space at all. Taken together, the transistor switch and its support components are called a memory cell. I've hidden the electrical complexity of the memory cell within an appropriate black-box symbol in Figure 3.1.

A memory cell keeps current flow through it to a minimum, because electrical current flow produces heat, and heat is the enemy of electrical components. The memory cell's circuit is arranged so that if you put a tiny voltage on its input pin and a similar voltage on its select pin, a voltage will appear and remain on its output pin. That output voltage will remain in its set state until you take away the voltage from the cell as a whole, or remove the voltage from the input pin while putting a voltage on the select pin.

The "on" voltage being applied to all of these pins is kept at a consistent level. (Except, of course, when it is removed entirely). In other words, you don't put 12 volts on the input pin and then change that to 6 volts or 17 volts. The computer designers pick a voltage and stick with it. The pattern is binary in nature: You either put a voltage on the input pin, or you take the voltage away entirely. The output pin echoes that: either holds a fixed voltage or no voltage at all.

We apply a code to state of affairs: The presence of voltage indicates a binary 1, and the lack of voltage indicates a binary 0. This is arbitrary. We could as well have said that the lack of voltage indicates a binary 1 and vice versa (and computers have been built this way for obscure reasons) but the choice is up to us. Having the presence of something indicating a binary 1 is more natural, and that is the way they have evolved in computing mainstream.

A single memory cell, such as the transistor-based one we're speaking of, holds one binary digit, either 1 or 0. This is called a bit. A bit is the indivisible atom of information. There is no half-a-bit, and no bit-and-a-bit. (This has been tried. It works badly. But that didn't stop it from being tried.) Bit is a single binary digit, either 1 or 0.

## Incredible Shrinking Bit

One bit doesn't tell us much. To useful, we need to bring a lot of memory cells together. Transistors started out small (the originals from the 1950s looked like stovepipe hats for tin soldiers) and went down from there. The first transistors were created from little chips of germanium or silicon crystal about an eighth of an inch square. The size of the crystal chip hasn't changed outrageously since then, but the transistors themselves have shrunk almost incredibly.

Where, in the beginning, one chip held one transistor, in time designers crisscrossed the chip into four equal areas and made each area an independent transistor. From there it was an easy jump to adding the other minuscule components needed to turn a transistor into a computer memory cell.

The chip of silicon was a tiny and fragile thing, and was encased in an oblong molded-plastic housing, like a stick of Dentyne gum with metal legs for the electrical connections. What we had now was a sort of electrical egg carton: four little cubbyholes, each of which could contain a single binary bit. The shrinking process began.
