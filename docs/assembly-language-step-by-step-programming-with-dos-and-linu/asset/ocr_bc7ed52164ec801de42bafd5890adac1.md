This scheme is the same for both DOS and Linux. What differs is the exact nature of the EOL marker. The EOL marker for DOS is not one character but two: the carriage return character (called CR by those who know and love it) followed by the linefeed character (similarly called LF). You don't see these characters on the screen as symbols, but you see what they do: They end the line. Anywhere a line ends in an ordinary DOS text file, you'll find a mostly invisible partnership of one CR character and one LF hanging out. With Linux being different: a single LF, without a partner CR.

Why do two characters want to end a line in a DOS text file? Long ago, there was (and still is, at hamfests) an incredible mechanical nightmare called Teletype machine. These were invented during World War II as robot typewriters that could send written messages over long distances through electrical signals that could pass over wires. As was a separate mechanical operation to return the typing carriage to the left margin of the paper (carriage return) and another to feed the paper up one to expose the next clean line of paper to the typing carriage (line feed). A separate electrical signal was required to do each of these operations, and while I don't know why that was necessary, it has carried over into the dawn of the twenty-first century in the form of those two characters, CR and LF. Not only is this a case of the tail wagging the dog, it's a case of the tail walking around 30 years after the poor dog rolled over and died.

Figure 4.2 shows how CR and LF divide what might otherwise be a single string of characters into a structured sequence of lines. It's important to understand the structure of a text file because structure dictates how some important software tools operate, as I explain a little later.

The CR is actually character 13 in the ASCII character set summarized in Appendix D. The LF character is character 10. They are two of a set of invisible characters called whitespace, indicating their role in positioning text characters ('', '*', etc.) within the white space of a text page. The other whitespace characters include the space character itself (32), the tab character (character 9), and the form feed character (character 12), which can optionally divide a text further into pages.
