The JZ instruction has been replaced with a JNZ instruction. That makes much more sense, to close the loop we have to jump, and we only close the loop while the counter is greater than 0. The jump back to label WorkLoop will happen only while the counter is greater than 0.

Once the loop decrements to 0, the loop is finished. JNZ falls through, and the code that follows the loop (which I don't show here) executes. The next instruction could be a JMP to label AllDone, as shown earlier, or it could be the next bit of work that the language program has to do. The point is if you can position the program's next task immediately after the JNZ instruction, you don't need to use the JMP instruction at all. Instruction execution will just flow naturally into the next task that needs performing. The program will have a more natural and less-tangled top-to-bottom flow and will be easier to read and understand.

# Flags

In Chapter 6, I explained the Flags register and briefly described the purposes of all the flags it contains. Most flags are not terribly useful, especially when you're starting out as a programmer. The Carry flag (CF) and the Zero flag (ZF) will be 90 percent of your involvement in flags as a beginner, with the Direction flag (DF), Sign flag (SF), and Overflow flag (OF) together up an additional 9.998 percent. It might be a good idea to reread that of Chapter 6 now, just in case your grasp of flag etiquette has gotten a little rusty.

As explained earlier, JZ jumps when ZF is 1, whereas JNZ jumps when ZF is 0. Instructions that perform some operation on an operand (such as AND, OR, XOR, INC, DEC, and all arithmetic instructions) set ZF according to the results of the operation. On the other hand, instructions that simply move data around (such as MOV, XCHG, PUSH, and POP) do not affect ZF or any of the other flags. (Obviously, POPF affects the flags by popping the top-of-stack value into them.) One irritating exception is the NOT instruction, which performs a logical operation on its operand but does not set any flags—even when it causes its operand to become 0. Before you write code that depends on flags, check your instruction reference to make sure that you have the flag etiquette down correctly.
