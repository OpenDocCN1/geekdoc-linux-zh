The word exploit in cybersecurity can be used as both a noun and a verb. As a noun, an exploit is a procedure for abusing a particular vulnerability. As a verb, to exploit a vulnerability is to perform the procedure that랴 abuses it.

Let's wrap this up by discussing attack surfaces and vectors. An attack surface³² describes all the points of contact on our system or network that could be vulnerable to exploitation. An attack vector³³ is a specific vulnerability and exploitation combination that can further create a threat actor's objectives. Defenders attempt to reduce their surfaces as much as possible, while attackers try to probe a given attack surface to locate promising vectors.

# 3.2.3 Threat Actor Classifications

The previous section introduced threats and attack vectors. Cybersecurity professionals are chiefly interested in threat actors since, particularly, that our systems, networks, and enterprises are vulnerable to them. Key attributes of cybercrime include anonymity, the ability to execute attacks at a distance, and (typically) a lack of physical danger and monetary cost.

There are a wide variety of threat actors. Different people and groups have varying levels of technical sophistication, different resources, personal motivations, and a variety of legal and moral systems guiding their behavior. While we cannot list every kind of threat actor, there are several high-level classifications to keep in mind:

Individual Malicious Actors: On the most superficial level, anyone attempting to do something that they are not supposed to do fits into this category. In cybersecurity, malicious actors explore digital tactics that are unintended by developers, such as authenticating to restricted services, stealing credentials, and defeating websites.

The case of Paige Thompson³⁴ is an example of an individual attacker can cause extreme amounts of damage and loss. In July 2019, Thompson was arrested for exploiting a router which had unnecessarily high privileges to download the information of 100 million people from Capital One. This attack led to the loss of personal information including SSNs, account numbers, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, etc.

This attack³⁵ was facilitated by a configured Web Application Firewall (WAF) that had excessive permissions to list and read files. The attack could have been prevented³⁶ by applying the principle of least privilege and verifying correct configuration of the WAF. Since the attacker posted about their actions on social media, another mitigation could have been social monitoring.
