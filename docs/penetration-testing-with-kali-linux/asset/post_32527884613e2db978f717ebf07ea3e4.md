## 4.7 Narrowing our Focus

Now that we know to study time on our calendar, let's consider how to organize our physical space. For some time, educational scientists have been trying to determine the ideal study space. One of the reasons is no consensus is because research¹⁸⁵ seems to suggest that changing environments occasionally is good for us.

Maybe our study space is at the dining room one week and in a quiet corner of the library next. It's okay to move around a bit, but there are a few things we will need from this space, regardless of where it is and how often we return to.

To end, we need to address a significant problem: multitasking. Study after study has shown the negative impact of multitasking on learning, job performance, and even brain health in general. Sometimes we feel like we can accomplish more by doing more things at once, but this simply isn't true.

Creating a productive study space is not just about doing one thing at a time, but also about minimizing noise. Even listening to music may not seem like much of a distraction at the time, processing this background noise still takes up a finite amount of mental focus. Studies show that, in listening to music — especially fast music with lyrics — there is some additional research that suggests certain types of music (slow, instrumental music, in particular), may be actually helpful to some.¹⁹¹.

In cases where it is actually helpful, it is entirely possible that music is blocking out other, even more distracting sounds (a learner in a coffee shop may find it easier to focus with headphones that cancel out the surrounding conversations, for example). It's also reasonable to do what we can to make the study space we feel comfortable in and that one we are enjoying spending time in.

More to the point, interruptions caused by phone alerts, text messages, emails, and individuals who might need our attention are another form of multitasking. Small habits, putting your phone in airplane mode or choosing a relatively isolated place for study, can sharpen focus.

### 4.7.5 Find a Community

Let's consider the benefits of learning with others. While studying alone has its advantages, interacting with peers can provide new perspectives and motivation. Many students find that joining a study group or participating in online forums helps them stay on track and overcome difficult concepts.

There are many ways to connect with others, including:
- Study groups: Small groups of students who meet regularly to discuss course material, and tackle complex problems together.
- Online forums: Digital platforms where students can ask questions and receive answers from a global community.
- Discord servers: Real-time chat platforms where students can interact, share resources, and support each other.
- Mentorship programs: Connecting with more experienced students or professionals who can provide guidance and advice.

Joining a community can make the learning process feel less like a solitary journey and more like a shared experience where growth is encouraged by everyone.

### 4.7.6 Study Your Own

Let's wrap up this Module by examining our own strategy. Since many of the details are constructed up to you are responsible for assessing and improving. While this might sound like a lot, let's review an easy and effective approach: at the end of a study session, take just 10 seconds to think about how it went. It's a very small thing, but it can make a huge difference.

To understand how, we'll look at the two obvious and extreme outcomes of a study session. If the study session was particularly difficult, much of self-reflection might lead you to think about what the content made it difficult. Generally speaking, we want to ask why it was difficult. The easy answer here might be "SQL Injection is just tough," but the difficulty of the material is at least out of our hands (though this might indicate a need to spend the next study session reviewing foundational materials).

We're specifically interested in the things that, as learners, have some control. Here is a list of potential questions to ask the study session:

- 1. What time did I start the study session?
- 2. How long was the study session?
- 3. Did I get interrupted (if, so, how did that happen)?
- 4. What did I do before I started studying?
- 5. What did I eat or drink before I studying?
- 6. What was my study location like? Was it quiet or busy?
- 7. What did I do during the study session specifically?

This is not a complete list of possible questions. The answer to each of these things might lead us to locate a more specific point of frustration.
