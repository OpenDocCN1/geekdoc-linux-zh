Like other cybersecurity laws, GLBA requires that financial institutions ensure the confidentiality and integrity of customer financial information by anticipating threats to security and taking steps to protect against unauthorized access. GDPR: The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)⁹² is a law adopted by the European Union⁹³ in 2016 that regulates data privacy and security. It applies to the private sector and most public sector entities that provides rights over data including the well-known "right to be forgotten" and other rights related to notifications of data breaches and portability of data between providers.

GDPR outlines a strict baseline for processing. For example, personal data may be processed only if the data subject has consent, to comply with legal obligations, to perform tasks in the public interest, or for other "legitimate interests". For businesses that process data on a large scale or where data processing is a core operation, a data protection officer - who is responsible for overseeing data protection - must be appointed.

GDPR also establishes an independent supervisory authority to audit and enforce compliance with these regulations and administer punishment for non-compliance. The fines for violating these regulations are high: a maximum of 20 million Euro or 4% of revenue (whichever is higher), plus any damages that individuals may seek.

One unique aspect of GDPR is that it applies to any entity collecting or processing data related to people in the Europe, regardless of their location. At the time of its adoption, it was considered the most strict data privacy law in the world and has become a model for how laws and regulations around the globe.

Key disclosure laws⁹⁴ are laws that compel the disclosure of cryptographic keys or passwords under specific conditions. A suspected crime of part of a criminal investigation may have adopted disclosure laws under varying conditions. For instance, Part III of the United Kingdom's Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act (RIPA)⁹⁵ grants the power to disclose decryption keys or decrypt data. Failure to comply is punishable by a maximum of two years in prison or if there is a matter of national security or deceit is involved.

CCPA: The California Privacy Act of 2018 (CCPA)⁹⁶ is a California law granting residents of the state privacy rights concerning personal information held by for-profit businesses. One of these rights is the "right to know", which requires businesses to disclose to consumers, upon request, what personal information has been collected, used, and sold about them, and why. "right to opt out" allows consumers to request that their personal information not be sold, something that must, with exceptions, be approved. Another right is the "right to delete", which allows consumers to request that businesses delete collected personal information. In this case, there are a number of exceptions that allow business to decline these requests.

## 3.5.2 Standards and Frameworks

PCI DSS. The Payment Card Data Security Standard (PCI DSS)⁹⁷ is an information security standard, first published in 2004, for organizations handling customer payment data for major credit cards. It is managed by the Payment Card Industry Standards Council. It's purpose is to ensure that payment data is properly secured in order to reduce the risk of credit fraud with other frameworks, PCI DSS consists of a number of requirements, compliance with which must be assessed annually. Most of these requirements resemble other best practices regulating network and security system, access control, vulnerability management, monitoring. For example, Requirement 2 prohibits the use of vendor-supplied defaults for system passwords and other security-related parameters. Other requirements are credit-card formulations of other familiar best practices. For example, Requirement 3 outlines types of credit card data that should be stored and must be protected.

CIS Top 18. The Center for Internet Security (CIS) Critical Security Controls, also known as CIS Controls⁸ are a set of 18 recommended controls to increase an organization’s security posture. While not as laws or regulations, these controls pertain to areas that are concerned with, including data protection, access control, continuous vulnerability management, malware detection, and more. These controls are divided into a number of safeguards (previously known as sub-controls), which, in turn, are grouped into implementation groups⁹. IG consists of controls that are considered the minimum standard for information security to protect against the most common attacks and should be implemented by every organization with limited expertise. IG consists of additional safeguards that are meant to help more complex organizations, typically with multiple departments and staff. IG, which consists of all safeguards, is typically implemented by dedicated experts.

NIST Cybersecurity Framework. The National Institute for Standards and Technology (NIST) Cybersecurity Framework¹⁰ is a collection of standards and practices designed to help organizations understand and reduce cybersecurity risk. It was originally developed to help protect critical infrastructure; however, it has been updated by a wide array of organizations. The Framework Core is a set of cybersecurity activities and outcomes. It is divided into five high-level framework¹¹. For example, Asset Management and Risk Assessment. These categories, in turn, include subcategories that consist of statements describing the outcome of improved security and are aligned with References. These references go into detail about possible technical implementations. For example, Subcategory ID.BE-1 (Function: Identify, Category: Business Environment) states "The organization's role in the supply chain is identified and communicated."

The Framework Implementation Tiers specify the degree to which practices satisfy the outcome described by the subcategories of the Framework Core. There are four such Tiers: partial (the least degree), risk informed, repeatable, and adaptive. Framework Profiles refer to the relationship between the present implementation of an organization's cybersecurity capabilities (Current Profile) and their desired outcome (Target Profile), which is determined by the organization's business objectives, requirements and risk appetite. The comparison of these profiles can help the organization perform a gap analysis, as well as understand and prioritize the work required to fill it.

ATT&CK and D3DEF. The MITRE¹⁰⁹ has tabulated and organized a framework for cataloging how groups of actors work to infiltrate systems and achieve their goals. This framework, called MITRE ATT&CK⁴, is constantly updated to reflect the latest TTPs used by malicious groups across the globe. More details about the ATT&CK framework and how adversaries can be classified in OffSec's SOC-200 course.

More recently, MITRE released a mirrored framework from the defensive perspective. While ATT&CK is meant to catalog and categorize the various ways that actors operate in the world, D3DEF⁵ portrays a set of best practices, actions, and methods employed by defenders to prevent, detect, mitigate, and react to attacks.

Cyber Kill Chain. The Cyber Kill Chain¹ is a methodology developed by Lockheed Martin to help defenders defend against cyber attacks. It outlines seven stages of attack lifecycle: reconnaissance, weaponization, delivery, exploitation, installation, command and control, and actions on objectives.

In the reconnaissance, an attacker identifies a target and enumerates potential weaknesses through which it may be exploited. Weaponization is the process by which an attack method to exploit this weakness is identified. This attack is launched in the delivery phase and, in the exploitation phase, the payload is executed on the target system. This leads to the installation stage in which malware is installed on the system. This malware is used to execute further commands in command and control phase. In the actions on objectives, the attacker performs the actions required to achieve their goals, which may be data theft, modification, destruction, etc.
