## 4.4.4 Analyzing this Approach

If much of the preceding example was new to you, congratulations! You've survived your first bit of cyber security training. Remember, the actual solutions and commands aren't as important as understanding (for now) how this was taught.

Although we chose an admittedly simple section from our written training, let's take a moment to examine how we taught this material. We'll highlight a few things in particular:

- 1. Using the demonstration method
- 2. Learning by doing
- 3. The skill, not the tool
- 4. Interleaving
- 5. Expecting the unexpected

Let's quickly explore each of these.

The demonstration method is used specifically in the tone and voice of the example covered, also in the series of actions that we follow. We don't skip steps, including verifying that our solutions worked.

Notably, we encounter a "problem" (not being able to execute our script) almost immediately, which represents the real-world, day-to-day experience of students after the course has ended. Research also supports problem solving as a very effective learning strategy for both engagement and retention.¹⁶³.

This problem-solving approach is used throughout Modules intentionally. One learners can take advantage of this by trying to predict outcomes. We might, for example, to guess what the next step will be in solving a problem. If we are surprised that the course material goes in another direction, and if we're curious, we can always find our solution!

This is a great way to follow the material, but let's consider something more direct and practical. Learning by doing is in an area where students can take learning into their own hands and accelerate their growth. The best way to do this is to follow along.

We can acknowledge that the case presented in this Module, it would have been difficult to follow along normally. Normally, a Module would include at least one virtual machine that is specifically set up to allow learners to follow the accompanying text. In this case, we would have a Linux machine with find_employee_names.py script on it.

Let's discuss where and how to follow along focusing on the content presented in the Module. A keen student might have noticed that all of the code chunks uses a similar style of formatting. Review one quickly.

The "kali@kali:~$" is what will appear on the screen for a user who is following along. Everything that appears in blue text (in this case, "ls chmod") is a command that you can type into the terminal. The text that follows is the output.

It's also important to understand where the focus is, which brings us to the skill, not the tool.

If you are already familiar with chmod, you have noticed that we chose one of many different methods to use this tool. We chose, for example, not to explore how the permissions for our script (before we were able to execute) could have been represented with the numerical expression 644, which we fixed by running chmod 755.

Of course, it's almost impossible to remember every specific command and syntax, and piling on too much information increases cognitive load, making it more difficult to remember the material later. Even the most experienced security researchers find themselves looking up now and again, and we encourage learners to focus on why a command is being run versus what command is being.

Sometimes new ideas are introduced or when there is an opportunity to learn more outside the text, we might introduce a footnote. Getting used to "leaving" the immediate problem in order to do a bit of research is a critical skill. There have a number of footnotes in this module already, and they appear superscript in the text.

Interleaving is enviable with this type of hands-training. As a quick reminder, in the context of education, interleaving is mixing of multiple subjects. In this case, we reviewed the tool, cat, and ls, although although weren't directly related to the things we were trying to study. They were, of course.

## 4.5 Tactics and Common Methods

Next, we need to think about strategy and tactics. Consider the following quote from Sun Tzu:

> Strategy without tactics is the slowest route to victory. Tactics without strategy is the noise before defeat. — Sun Tzu

In the most basic sense, we can think of strategy as being a long-term vision, while tactics are the short-term, immediate actions that we take. Strategy is the map, and tactics are the steps.

For learners in a formal school structure, the strategy and tactics of study are often built into the school. A student's schedule and the topics of study, and even how they will approach learning, are all dictated by the school district or the instructor.

In the absence of that rigid school structure, a common mistake of adult learners is to approach their studies without thinking about either tactics or strategy. We might know, for example, that it's important to take “notes”, but what exactly should we be writing? And what should we do with these notes?

This Learning Unit will present a series of specific tactics for learners to choose from. The Learning Unit that will discuss a few strategies we can use to approach our studies.

The tactics follow are not intended as a complete or prescriptive list. What works for one may not work for others. Students should take ideas and determine for themselves what might work for them.

This Learning Unit covers the following Learning Objectives:

- 1. Understand one potential note taking method called Cornell
- 2. Learn about Retrieval Practice
- 3. Understand Spaced Practice
- 4. Explore SQ3R and PQ4R Method
- 5. Examine the Feynman Technique
- 6. Understand the Leitner System

While each student will learn at their own pace, this Learning Unit should take about ten minutes to complete.

Since this Learning Unit is intended as a reference list of tactics, we will not provide challenge questions at the end as we have with any other Learning Units in this Module.
