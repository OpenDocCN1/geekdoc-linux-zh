DevSecOps¹² (an abbreviation for Development, Security and Operations) is an approach to software development that integrates security into all stages of the software development lifecycle, rather than postponing it to the end. A DevSecOps Engineer¹³ is responsible for automating security and other security-related processes. This role might be a good fit for someone who has an understanding of Continuous Integration / Continuous Development (CI/CD) pipeline tools, an interest in security testing automation, and the ability to work in a fast-paced environment.

The OffSec Library contains a considerable number of resources that can help learners with software development, understanding the different attack vectors to automate testing and for [learning] types of automation tools available. This information can be found in the WEB and PEN courses at 200 and 300 level. CLD-100 also provides details about Docker and Kubernetes: essential tools for DevSecOps.

Site Reliability Engineer: A Site Reliability Engineer¹⁴ is responsible for ensuring and improving the availability and performance of software systems. A person may wish to be a Site Reliability Engineer if they have software development experience and are interested in using automation to monitor for, alert, and respond to reliability-related issues. learners can learn about and and Kubernetes, some of the key technologies used to support SRE, by following CLD-100 in the OffSec library.

System Hardener (System Administrator). A System Hardener¹⁵ is responsible for configuring systems to reduce their security risk. This involves changing insecure configurations, removing unused programs, ensuring firewalls are appropriately restrictive, etc. A person may seek out this course if they have experience with system administration, are familiar with techniques, and enjoy making systems and the data they store more secure. Many of the skills required for this position are covered in the PEN track at 100, 200 and 300 level. PEN-100, for instance, explores some of the basics of networking and system administration. PEN-200 describes some of the common techniques that attackers use. PEN-300 teaches advanced techniques that attackers use to bypass defenses.

### 4.1 Learning Theory

Let's begin with a very basic discussion of Learning Theory. We'll make some general observations about this field and examine the current state of how students learn. In general, this Learning Unit and the next will illuminate some of the problems and difficulties individuals face when learning new subjects. This Learning Unit covers the following Objectives:

- Understand the general state of our understanding about education
- Understand the basics of memory mechanics and dual encoding
- Recognize some of the problems faced by learners, including "The Curve of Forgetting" and cognitive load

While each student will learn at their own pace, this Learning Unit should take about 15 minutes.

### 4.1.1 We Know and We Don't

Although humans have always taught, we have only recently (within the past 100 years) begun researching learning theory. Some of this research focuses on the structure and purpose of schools themselves. For example, a great deal of research ponders the ideal classroom size, whether or not activities in gym class can help a student in science class, etc. Although these studies may initially seem relevant to our focus on cybersecurity, a few aspects of this research are worth mentioning.

First, learning is not entirely dependent on the student. The teacher, education format, and a variety of factors affect success more than a student's raw capability. In fact, a student's performance is a poor predictor of future success and external events and circumstances can drastically affect a student's performance.

Second, as new educational studies are constantly released, it's clear there is still much to be discovered about the mechanics of our memory. This includes research suggesting that the notion of learning (or learning styles) is more of a myth than previously thought.

With in mind, OffSec designs our courses around current, established academic research regarding learning theory, and (partially we aim to be perpetual learners) we are constantly seeking to improve our methods. As instructors, our goal is to create a highly-effective learning environment that equips students to excel in the ever-changing field of information security, regardless of past experience or performance in the field.

However, if we can discuss more practical strategies, let's select some of the current research in the field of learning theory to understand how it's best applied.

### 4.1.2 Memory Mechanisms and Dual Coding

It can be a bit overwhelming to think of education as a whole, so let's try to understand it in simple terms. One of the ways we can demonstrate that we've 'learned' something is if we are able to create and retrieve a memory.

For example, we might learn a specific command to rename a file in Linux, `oldfilename.txt newfilename.txt`. Later, we might find ourselves at a computer, needing to rename a file. We hope in that situation, away from our textbook and any instructional material, we will remember this.
