4. Proving Grounds

The training materials themselves will always trend focusing on scenarios that we can follow along with. There are times when we need to discuss a bit of theory that we have enough background to go deeper, but in general, if the material can demonstrate working through a problem, the expectation is that the student should be able to follow along. A virtual machine (VM) is specifically built to accommodate this.

The Module Exercises themselves will involve working with a VM as well. This is the approach as often as possible, but with some Modules (one, example) that are more theoretical, exercises are practised in a more standard question-answer format.

The OffSec Library also contains Challenge Labs, which take the exercises one step further. A Challenge Lab is essentially, an environment of additional practice exercises specifically created to help learners prepare for an exam (which, as expected, is hands-on). We highly recommend that students take advantage of this additional opportunity.

Finally, we leverage assessments and exams. These are exercises and networked lab environments specifically for proving the skills we've learned. A real world environment will not give us a clear indication for which vulnerabilities might be present on a system, we don't create a 1:1 link between a course module and an assessment (for example, we don't advertise whether or not a machine is vulnerable to privilege escalation).

With in mind, the skills and methods students will learn in the courses are directly applicable in the assessment and exam environments.

# 4.3.3 Facing Difficulty

There is a common exception that "practice makes perfect". That be true, but it begs the question, what makes for ideal practice?

Let's consider the experiment that was performed in 1978.¹152 A group of 8-year-old children were divided into two groups to practice a simple task: toss a small bean bag into a target hole. After being introduced to the target at a distance of three feet (about 90 cm), the groups spent the next three months practising. One group kept with the same distance. The other aimed at a pair of targets - practising with distances of two feet (60 cm) and four feet (120 cm).

In the final test, the task was to toss the bean bags to a target three feet away. The group who had spent all of their practising at that exact distance was in fact beaten by the group who had practised at two and four feet.

This and other studies demonstrate that struggle is not only important to the learning experience, but it's actually more important than mere repetition for creating the neural pathways that help us learn new skills.
