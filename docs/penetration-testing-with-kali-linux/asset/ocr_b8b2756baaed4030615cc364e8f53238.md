# 21.1.1 Enumeration - Defining our Goals

Before we begin, let's discuss the scenario and define our goals.

In this scenario, we'll enumerate the corp.com domain. We have obtained target domain credentials through a successful phishing attack. Alternatively, the target organization may have provided us with user credentials so that we can perform penetration testing based on an assumed breach. This would speed up the process for us and give the organization insight into how easily attackers can move within their environment once they have gained initial access.

The user we have access to is steanie who has remote desktop permissions on a Windows 11 machine that is part of a domain. This user is not a local administrator on the machine, which is something we might need to take into consideration as we move forward.

During a real-world assessment, the organization may define the scope and goals of the penetration test. In our case however, we are restricted to the corp.com domain with the PWK labs. Our goal will be to enumerate the full domain, finding possible ways to achieve the highest privilege possible (domain administrator in this case).

In this Module, we will perform the enumeration from one client machine with the low privileged steanie domain. However, once we starting attacks and we are able to gain access to additional users and computers, we may have to repeat parts of the enumeration from the new standpoint. This perspective shift (pivot) is critical during the enumeration process considering the complexity of permissions across the domain. Each pivot may give us an opportunity to advance our attack.

For example, if we gain access to another low-privileged account that seems to have the same access as steanie, we shouldn't simply dismiss it. Instead, we should always repeat our enumeration with that new account since administrators often grant individuals increased permissions based on their role in the organization. This persistent "rinse and repeat" process is the key to successful enumeration and works extremely well, especially in large organizations.
