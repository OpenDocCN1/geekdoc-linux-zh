The word exploit in cybersecurity can be used as both a noun and a verb. As a noun, an exploit is a procedure for abusing a particular vulnerability. As a verb, to exploit a vulnerability is to perform the procedure that abuses it.

Let's wrap this up by discussing attack surfaces and vectors. An attack surface³² describes all the points of contact on our system or network that could be vulnerable to exploitation. An attack vector³³ is a specific vulnerability and exploitation combination that can further create a threat actor's objectives. Defenders attempt to reduce their surfaces as much as possible, while attackers try to probe a given attack surface to locate promising vectors.

## 3.2.3 Threat Actor Classifications

The previous section introduced threats and attack vectors. Cybersecurity professionals are chiefly interested in threat actors since, particularly, that our systems, networks, and enterprises are vulnerable to them. Key attributes of cybercrime include anonymity, the ability to execute attacks at a distance, and (typically) a lack of physical danger and monetary cost.

There are a wide variety of threat actors. Different people and groups have varying levels of technical sophistication, different resources, personal motivations, and a variety of legal and moral systems guiding their behavior. While we cannot list every kind of threat actor, there are several high-level classifications to keep in mind:

- Individual Malicious Actors: On the most superficial level, anyone attempting to do something that they are not supposed to do fits into this category. In cybersecurity, malicious actors explore digital tactics that are unintended by developers, such as authenticating to restricted services, stealing credentials, and defeating websites.

The case of Paige Thompson³⁴ is an example of an individual attacker can cause extreme amounts of damage and loss. In July 2019, Thompson was arrested for exploiting a router which had unnecessarily high privileges to download the information of 100 million people from Capital One. This attack led to the loss of personal information including SSNs, account numbers, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, etc.

This attack³⁵ was facilitated by a configured Web Application Firewall (WAF) that had excessive permissions to list and read files. The attack could have been prevented³⁶ by applying the principle of least privilege and verifying correct configuration of the WAF. Since the attacker posted about their actions on social media, another mitigation could have been social monitoring.

- Malicious Groups. When individuals band together to form groups, they often become stronger than their individual group members. This can be even more true online because the ability to communicate instantly and at vast distances enables people to achieve goals that would have been impossible without such powerful communication tools. For example, the ability to quickly coordinate who-does-what over instant messaging services is just as valuable to malicious cyber groups as it is to modern businesses. Malicious groups can have any number of goals, that are usually more purposeful, organized, and resourceful than individuals. They, are often considered to be one of the most dangerous threat actors.

Let's examine an example of a group-led attack. Over the span of a number of months, the “Lapsus$ performed a number of attacks on a wide range of companies, stealing proprietary information and engaging in extortion. These attacks resulted in loss of corporate data - including proprietary data such as source code, schematics, and other documentation. The attacks further resulted in the public exposure of data, and financial losses for companies submitted to extortion.

The variety and sophistication of techniques used by the group show how this kind of malicious actor can be so dangerous. In particular, individuals within a group can bring their own specialties to the table that people alone wouldn't be able to leverage. In addition, they can launch many different types of attacks at targets at a volume and velocity that an individual wouldn't be able to. It's a common indeed in the cybersecurity industry that the attacker needs to succeed once, while the defender must succeed every time.

- Insider Threats: Perhaps one of the most dangerous types of threat actor, an insider threat is anyone who has privileged access to a system and can abuse their privileges to gain further access. Often, insider threats are individuals or groups of ex-employees of an enterprise who became motivated to have it. Insider threats can be so treacherous because they are usually assumed to have a level of trust. At the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic, Christopher Dobbins, who had been hired as Vice President of a medical packaging company, used a fake account that he created during his employment to access company systems and delete data that was critical to the company's distribution. This attack resulted in the delayed delivery of medical supplies and the disruption of the company's broader operations. The danger of an insider threat is showcased here. The attack was created by a fake account which could have been prevented by applying the principle of least privilege, which we'll explore in a later section. Since the attack was enabled by a fake account, it could have been prevented by rigorously auditing accounts. Lastly, this activity was performed after the VP's termination, better monitoring of anomalous activity may have prevented or mitigated the attack. Palo Alto Networks suggests focusing on security practices such as MFA, access control, and network segmentation.

- Nation States. Although international politics, cyber war, and digital intelligence are vast subjects and significantly beyond the scope of this Module, we recognize that some of the most proficient, resourceful, and well-funded operators of cyber attacks at the nation-state level within many countries across the globe. Since 2009, North Korean actors, usually grouped under the name Lazarus,¹¹ have a number of different attacks ranging from data theft (Sony, 2014), to ransomware (WannaCry, 2017) to financial targeting banks (Bangladesh Bank, 2016) and cryptrencies - notably, the 2022 attack. These attacks have resulted in loss and leak of corporate data, including proprietary data (Sony) and financial losses for companies that paid a ransom. An insurance assurance firm NCC Group² suggests the following to prevent and mitigate attacks from the Lazarus group: network segmentation, patching and updating internet-facing resources, ensuring the correct implementation of MFA, monitoring for anomalous user behavior (example: multiple, concurrent sessions from different locations), ensuring sufficient logging, and log analysis.
