We can do this by splitting content into an appropriate structure of sections and subsections. The number of audiences we have for a particular engagement depends heavily on our relationship with the client, their size, budget, and maturity. For the sake of this Module, we consider an engagement for which we have only two audiences. The first, and arguably the more important, is the management level. This is often the level at which many external engagement contracts are signed and where the value of investing in the testing needs to be highlighted. Depending on the business, this may be C-level functions (CISO, CSO, CFO, etc), or department heads of IT or security.

However, most executives and upper-level directors will not necessarily have the technical ability to follow a detailed technical explanation. We should provide them with a section that highlights the outcome and impact of the engagement in a way that accurately reports on the vulnerabilities found without being overloaded with technical details.

The second audience we consider made up of the technical staff who have the technical knowledge to understand the report and implement the remediations for the vulnerabilities we have identified. This audience must be provided with enough technical detail to enable them to understand what is wrong, the impact of each finding is, and how they can be fixed. In addition, this audience greatly benefits when we can provide advice on how to prevent similar types of issues from occurring in the future.

# 5.2.3 Executive Summary

The first section of the report should be an Executive Summary. This enables senior management to understand the scope and outcomes of the testing at a sufficient level to understand the value of the test, and to approve remediation. We start with the bite-sized pieces of information that provide the big picture, and follow up with the full Executive Summary.

The Executive Summary should start by outlining the scope of the engagement. Having a clear scope agreed in advance of the testing defines the bounds of what will be covered. We want to be very clear about what exactly was tested and whether anything was dropped from the scope. Timing issues, as insufficient time due to finding many vulnerabilities make the statement for any subsequent test appropriate. Including the scope in the report protects the penetration test from any suggestion of not having completed the required testing. This also gives the client more accurate model of what is practical given the budget and time constraints that were initially set.
