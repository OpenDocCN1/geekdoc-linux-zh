## 25.1.1 STOP! Do This First

If you are reading this Module and haven't completed ALL the PWK Capstone exercises, I highly recommend going back and finishing them before proceeding. The Capstone exercises provide you with an opportunity to hack machines with specific constraints on what needs to be enumerated. By compromising simple machines and smaller networks via the Capstone exercises, you will set yourself up for a more successful, effective, and pleasant experience with the Challenge Labs.

Once you have completed the Capstone exercises, make sure to read and follow along with the Assembling Pieces Module before developing a methodological framework for attacking larger networks. We recommend starting with the Challenge Labs only once the Capstone exercises and the Assembling Pieces Module are complete.

## 25.1.2 Challenge Labs 1-3

Much of the below information is included in the Introduction to PWK Module. We are repeating the information here because it has been a while since you read the introduction. Please review carefully before starting the Challenge Labs.

There are two types of Challenge Labs. The first are called scenarios. Each scenario consists of a set of networked machines and a short background story that puts those machines in context. Your goal is to obtain a Domain Administrator on an Active Directory domain, and compromise as many machines on the network as possible.

In the same way that Capstone Exercises test the learner on the material of multiple Learning Units, so to do these scenarios test the learner on the material of multiple Learning Modules. Your level of uncertainty about the network here is high and similar to real-world penetration tests, because you will not know which machines are vulnerable to what types of attacks. In addition,

All machines contain either a local.txt file, a proof.txt file, or both. The contents of these files are randomized hashes that can be submitted to the OLP to log each compromise. Just like the Module exercise the contents of these files will change on every revert of the machine.

The following scenarios provide a high level overview of each scenario:

**Challenge Lab 1: MEDTECH.** You have been tasked to conduct a penetration test for MEDTECH, a recently formed IoT startup. Your objective is to find all vulnerabilities and misconfigurations in order to increase their Active Directory security posture and reduce the attack surface.

**Challenge Lab 2: RELIA.** You are tasked with a penetration test of RELIA, an international company building systems for the industry. The target caught a few weeks ago and now want to get an assessment of their IT security. Their goal is to find out if an attacker can breach the perimeter and gain Domain privileges in the internal network.

**Challenge Lab 3: SKYLARK.** Skylark Industries is a aerospace corporation that performs research & development on cutting-edge aviation technologies. One of their branch offices has been targeted by an Advanced Persistent Threat (APT) actor ransomware attack. For this reason, the company CISO now wishes to further shield Skylark's attack surface. You have been tasked to conduct a preemptive penetration test towards their HQ infrastructure and find any vulnerability that could potentially jeopardize the company's trade secrets.

> Please note that Challenge 3 is significantly more difficult than Challenges 1 & 2. It requires a substantial amount of pivoting, tunneling, looking for information on multiple targets and paying close attention to post-exploitation. It is beyond the scope of the OSCP exam. If preparing for the exam is your main objective, you may wish to work through Challenges 4, 5 & 6 before returning Challenge 3.

## 25.1.3 Challenge Labs 4-6

The second type of Challenge Labs consists of an OSCP-like experience. They are composed of six OSCP machines. The intention of these Challenges is to provide a mock-exam experience that closely reflects a similar level of difficulty to that of the actual OSCP exam.

Each challenge contains three machines that are connected via Active Directory, and three standalone machines that do not have any dependencies or intranet connections. The standalone machines have a local.txt and a proof.txt.

While the Challenge Labs have no point values, on the exam the standalone machines would be worth 20 points each for a total of 60 points. The Active Directory set is worth 40 points together, and the entire domain must be compromised to achieve any points for it.

All the intended attack vectors for these machines are taught in PEN-200 Modules, or are leveraged in the first three Labs. However, the specific requirements to trigger the vulnerabilities may differ from the exact scenarios. You are expected to be able to take the demonstrated techniques and modify them for the specific environment.

When completing Challenges 4-6, we recommend a different approach compared to Challenges 1-3. In particular, the purpose of these challenges is to provide you with direct, intuitive insight into the types of machines that are likely to be on the exam. These Challenges are not designed to be all-encompassing; the exam may contain vectors that are not part of these Challenges. Again, anything taught within PWK is fine.

In order to mimic the exam as closely as possible, we suggest that you avoid discussing these machines with peers and the Student Mentors. In addition, you may want to experiment with setting a timer to complete these challenges. However, we dont necessarily recommend trying to do within 24 hours, simply because it is more effective to spread out your learning over a longer period. For each of the challenges, you might want spending an initial 24 hours in total to see how far you can get within the time limit. Then, step back and explore your progress.

## 25.2.1 Client-Side Simulations

The internal VPN lab contains a number of simulated clients that can be exploited using client-side attacks. These clients are programmed to simulate common corporate user activity. Subtle hints throughout the lab can help you locate these simulated clients. Thorough-exploitation information gathering may also reveal communication between client machines.
