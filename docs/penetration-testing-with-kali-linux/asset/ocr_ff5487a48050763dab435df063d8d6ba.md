Another extremely valuable mindset the aptly-security mindset. Proposed by security researcher Bruce Schneider,¹19 this mindset encourages a constant questioning of how one can attack (or defend) a system. If we begin to ask this question automatically when encountering a novel idea, machine, system, network, or object, we can start noticing a wide array of recurring patterns.

At OffSec, we encourage learners to adopt the Try Harder²⁰ mindset. To better understand this mindset, let's quickly consider two potential perspectives in a moment of “failure.”

+   1. If my attack or defend fails, it represents a truth about notice as it is a truth about the system skills/processes/configurations/approach as much as

+   2. If my attack or defense fails, this allows me to learn something new, change my approach, and doing something differently.

These two perspectives provide someone with the mental fortitude to make mistakes and learn from them, which is absolutely essential in any cybersecurity sub-field. More information about how to learn and the Try Harder mindset can be found in the “Effective Learning Strategies” Module that is part of this Introductory Learning Path.

# 3.1.3 Emulating the Minds of Opponents

It's worth to consider the particular attention that we will give to the offensive²¹ side of security, even in many of our defensive courses and Modules. One might wonder why a cybersecurity professional primary interest and goal is defending a network, organization, or government should also learn offense.

Let's take the analogy of a medieval monarch building a castle. If the monarch learns that their enemy has capable of hurling large boulders, they might design their castle with thicker walls. Similarly, if their enemy is equipped with ladders, the monarch might give their troops to push the ladders off the walls.

The more this monarch knows about their-attack and the more they can think like an attacker, the better defense they can build. The monarch might engage in “offensive” types of activities or audits to understand the gaps in their own defenses. For example, they could conduct ‘war-games’ where they direct their soldiers to mock-battle each other, helping them understand the capabilities and destructive potential of a real attacker.

In cybersecurity, enterprises might hire an individual or a firm to perform a penetration test - also known as a Pent penetration tester takes the role of an attacker to better understand the system’s vulnerabilities and exposed weaknesses. Leveraging the skill-sets and minds of an attacker allows us to answer questions like “How might an attacker gain access?”, “What can they do with that access?”, and “What are the worst possible outcomes from an attack?

While hacking skills is (of course) essential for aspiring testers, we also believe that defenders, system administrators, and developers will greatly benefit from a least courtesy education in effective techniques and technologies as well.
