## 32.6 MANAGEMENT

The role of IT management is to define the IT group's overall strategy and to oversee the organization. Many responsibilities fall on the managers' shoulders:

- Leading the group, bringing vision, and providing necessary resources
- Hiring, staff assessment, and skill development
- Assigning tasks to the staff and tracking progress
- Ensuring compliance with SLAs
- Negotiating changes and updates to SLAs
- Communicating with the managers of the overall organization
- Handling problems: staff conflicts, rogue users, hardware, etc.
- Acting as a "higher authority" to whom users can escalate problems
- Overseeing the development of infrastructure
- Planning for disasters and emergencies
- Extracting documentation from sysadmins
- Facilitating security through policy development (both for users and administrators)

It might seem that the task of interfacing with customers is missing from this list. However, we believe that this role is actually best filled by technical staff. Managers frequently do not have the technical background to evaluate the difficulty and feasibility of customers' requests.

## Leadership

Leadership is hard to describe. When lacking or poorly executed, its absence is all too apparent. In a way, leadership is the "system administration" of organizations: it sets the direction, makes sure components work together, and keeps the whole system running with few errors as possible.

Unfortunately, technical prowess that makes someone great administrator doesn't necessarily translate to the leadership role, which requires a more people-centered skill set. People are a lot harder to master than Perl. For managers with strong technical backgrounds, it can be particularly hard to focus on the job of management and avoid the temptation to do engineering work. It’s sometimes more comfortable and fun to dive into solving a technical problem than to have a long conversation with a “difficult” staff member. But which is more valuable to the organization?

A simple (and eye-opening) check on your level of leadership is: Make a list of the tasks your organization is working on. Use one color to mark the areas in which you are steering the boat, and a different color to mark the areas in which you are rowing or pulling the boat. What color is dominant?

## Personnel management

Personnel management can be particularly challenging. As part of your oversight function you deal with both technical and personal sides. Technically brilliant sysadmins can sometimes be poor communicators. As your manager, you need to keep them growing in both dimensions. Technical growth is relatively easy to promote and quantify, but personal growth is just as important. Below are some important questions to ask when assessing an employee:

- Is this person's behavior suit our work environment?
- How does person interact with authorities, customers?
- Does this person get along with other members of the team?
- Does this person have leadership skills that should be developed?
- How does person respond to criticism and technical disputes?
- Does this person actively address gaps in her knowledge?
- How are person's communication skills?
- Can this person plan, implement, and demonstrate ownership of tasks?
- Does this person tend to find solutions to roadblocks?

## Hiring

It’s important to make these assessments for potential hires as well as for existing employees. The personal qualities of applicants are often overlooked or undervalued. Don’t take shortcuts in this area—you’ll regret it later! There are two approaches to building a system staff:

- Hire experienced people.
- Grow your own.

Experienced people may come up to speed faster, but you sometimes want them to learn certain things or change old habits. Conversely, inexperienced administrators may require extensive training. Regardless of which option you choose, it’s helpful to have documented policies and procedures so existing staff have clear direction and understand your organization's way of doing things.

Some of the qualities of a good system administrator are contradictory. An admin must be brave enough to try innovative solutions when problems arise but must also be careful enough not to try anything destructive. Interpersonal skills and problem-solving skills are both important, but they often seem to lie on axes. While all of your sysadmins need to be stellar communicators, a few go a long way toward customer satisfaction. When hiring admins, you will have to decide which characteristics are most important for a particular role. For example, if you are hiring an administrator who will be focused on back-end systems and have little interaction with customers, you might rank communication skills lower than technical skills. But since this person will be part of a larger team, you cannot ignore interpersonal skills. To assess technical expertise, you might try drafting a set of pertinent questions you pose to interviewees. You might even try sticking a bogus question in to measure the applicant's answers. We believe that in-person interviews are important. You will learn more about an applicant in the first 15 minutes of an in-person interview than in a longer conversation. We also believe strongly in checking references. During the reference check, we like to ask open-ended questions that give the respondent a chance to send subtle messages about the applicant. Listen carefully! People generally not like to say negative things during reference check, but they may give you hints if you are paying attention. Some of the questions to ask will emerge from your interview with the candidate. For example, if the interview raises concern about whether applicant pays close attention to details, you might ask something like “Would you consider the applicant more of a detail-oriented person or a big-picture thinker?”

## Firing

If you make a hiring mistake, fire early. You may miss a few late blooms, but keeping people who are not putting their own weight can alienate your other staff members as they take up the slack and clean up the messes. Your customers will also realize that certain individuals don't get things done and start questioning particular administrators for their jobs. You don't want your customers interfering with management decisions in your daily business. In many organizations it is very hard to fire someone, especially after the evaluation period is over. Make sure that initial evaluations are taken seriously. Later, you may have to collect data showing incompetence, give formal warnings, set performance goals, and so on.

## Mechanics of personnel management

There is more to integrating a new employee into your infrastructure than just writing an offer letter. You must be aware of and honor your organization's rules regarding advertising for positions, trial periods, reviews, and so on. Another set of chores defines the mechanics of getting a new person settled with desk, computer, keys, accounts, and sudo access. Your processes should ensure that a system administrator helped administer a particular set of servers is not given carte blanche to administer any system in the company. Just as important are the policies and procedures to follow when sysadmin leaves the organization. You need a checklist so you don't forget anything. Your checklist should include things as:

- Removing the user's domain account (LDAP or Active Directory)
- Removing the user's UNIX and Linux accounts
- Removing the user's all from the site's sudoers files
- Collecting keys and access cards (document all keys and cards collected)
- Collecting a company cell phone

Some organizations can print a definitive list of access rights and hardware that have been given to each employee. This is a great way to make sure you haven't forgotten anything. In the United States, it's common for employees to give two weeks' notice before quitting. Some sites forget the two-week period and walk the employee to the door, immediately revoking all physical and network access. Smart!
