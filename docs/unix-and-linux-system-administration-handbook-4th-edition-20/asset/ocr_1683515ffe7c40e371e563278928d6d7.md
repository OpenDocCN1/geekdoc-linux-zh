# Control = liability

ISPs typically have an appropriate use policy (AUP) dictated by their upstream providers and required of their downstream customers. This "flow down" of liability assigns responsibility for users' actions to themselves, not to the ISP or the ISP's upstream provider. Such policies have been used to control spam and protect ISPs in cases where customers have stored illegal or copyrighted material in their accounts. Check the laws in your area; your mileage may vary.

Your policies should explicitly state that users are not to use company resources for illegal activities. However, that is not really enough—you also need to discipline users if you find out they are doing nasty things. Organizations that know about nasty things but do not act on them are complicit and can be prosecuted. Unenforced or inconsistent policies are worse than none, from both a practical and legal point of view.

Because of the risk of being found complicit in user misbehavior, size limits the data that you log, the length of time for which logs are kept, and the amount of log file history kept on backup tapes. Software packages help with the implementation of this policy by including levels of logging that help the sysadmin debug problems that do not violate users' privacy. However, always be aware of what kind of logging might be required by local laws or by any regulatory standards you apply to.

# Software licenses

Many sites have for K copies of a software package have N copies in use, where K < N. Getting caught in this situation could be damaging to the company, probably more damaging than the cost of N minus-K other licenses. Other sites have received a demo copy of an expensive software package and hacked it (reset the date on the machine, found the license key), to make it continue working after the expiration of the demo period. How do you sysadmin deal with unwanted license agreements and make copies of software unlicensed? What do you do when you find that machines you are running pirated software?

It's a very tough call. Management will often not back you up in your requests that unlicensed copies of software be either removed or paid. Often, it is a sysadmin who signs the agreement to remove the demo copies after a certain date, but a manager who makes the decision not to remove them.
