## 32.10 LEGAL ISSUES

The U.S. federal government and several states have laws regulating computer crime. At the federal level, there are two pieces of legislation from the early 1990s and two more recent ones:

- * The Federal Communications Privacy Act
- The Computer Fraud and Abuse Act
- The No Electronic Theft Act
- The Digital Millennium Copyright Act

Some issues in the legal area are the liability of sysadmins, network operators, and web hosting sites; peer-to-peer file-sharing networks; copyright issues; and privacy issues. The topics in this section comment on these issues and a variety of other legal debates related to system administration.

### Privacy

Privacy has always been difficult to safeguard, with the rise of the Internet, it is in more than ever. Medical records have been repeatedly disclosed by poorly protected systems, stolen laptops, and misplaced backup tapes. Databases full of credit card numbers have been compromised. Web sites offering free antivirus software actually install spyware when used. Fake email arrives daily, appearing to be from your bank and alleging problems with your account require you to verify your account data. A close inspection of the email reveals that the data would go to a hacker in eastern Europe or Asia and not to your bank. This type of attack is called “phishing.”

### Policy enforcement

Technical measures can never protect against these attacks because they target your site's most vulnerable weakness: its users. Your best defense is a well-educated user base. To a first approximation, no legitimate email or web site will

- Suggest that you have won a prize
- Request that "verify" account information or passwords
- Ask you to force a piece of email
- Ask you to install software you have not explicitly searched for
- Inform you of a virus or other security problem

Users who have a basic understanding of these dangers are more likely to make sensible choices when a pop-up window claims they have won a free Macbook.

Log files may prove to beyond a shadow of a person X did bad thing Y, but to a court it is just hearsay evidence. Protect yourself with written policies. Log files sometimes include time stamps, which are useful but not necessarily admissible unless your computer is running the Network Time Protocol (NTP) to keep your clock synced to a reference standard.

You may need a security policy in order to prosecute someone for misuse. It should include a statement such as: "Unauthorized use of computing systems may involve not only transgression of organizational policy but also a violation of state and federal laws. Unauthorized use is a crime and may involve criminal and civil penalties; it will be prosecuted to the full extent of the law.”

We advise you to display a splash screen that advises users of your snooping. You might say something like: "Activity may be monitored in the event of a real or suspected security incident."

You may want to ensure that users see the notification at least once by including it in the startup files you give to users. If you use the SSH to log in (and you should), you can configure `/etc/ssh/sshd_config` so that SSH allows the splash screen.

Be sure to specify that by the act of using their accounts, users acknowledge your written policy. Explain where users get copies of policy documents and post keys on an appropriate web page. Also include specific penalty for noncompliance. It is more important that you demonstrate good faith effort than the cost of unlicensed software.

In addition to the splash screen approach, a good idea is to have users sign an agreement before they are given access. This agreement should be crafted in conjunction with your legal department. Make signing the agreement part of the induction process for new hires.

### Control = liability

ISPs typically have an appropriate use policy (AUP) dictated by their upstream providers and required of their downstream customers. This "flow down" of liability assigns responsibility for users' actions to themselves, not to the ISP or the ISP's upstream provider. Such policies have been used to control spam and protect ISPs in cases where customers have stored illegal material. Check the laws in your area; your mileage may vary.

Your policies should explicitly state that users are not to use company resources for illegal activities. However, that is not really enough—you also need to discipline users if you find out they are doing it. Organizations that know about nasty things but do not act on them are complicit and can be prosecuted. Unenforced or inconsistent policies are worse than none, from both a practical and legal point of view.

Because of the risk of being found complicit in user misbehavior, limit the data that you log, the length of time for which logs are kept, and the amount of log file history kept on backup tapes. Software packages help with the implementation of this policy by including levels of logging that help the sysadmin debug problems that do not violate users' privacy. However, always be aware of what kind of logging might be required by local laws or by any regulatory standards you apply to.

### Software licenses

Many sites have K copies of a software package have N copies in use, where K < N. Getting caught in this situation could be damaging to the company, probably more damaging than the cost of N minus K other licenses. Other sites have received a demo copy of an expensive software package and hacked it (reset the date on the machine, found the license key), to make it continue working after the expiration of the demo period. How do you sysadmin deal with unwanted license agreements and make copies of software? What do you do when you find that machines are running pirated software?

It's a very tough call. Management will often not back you up in your requests that unlicensed copies of software be either removed or paid. Often, it is a sysadmin who signs the agreement to remove the demo copies after a certain date, but a manager who makes the decision not to remove them.

We are aware of several cases in which a sysadmin's immediate manager would not deal with the situation and told the sysadmin not to rock the boat.
