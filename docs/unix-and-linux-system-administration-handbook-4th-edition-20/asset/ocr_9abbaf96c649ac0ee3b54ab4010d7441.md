# DKIM: DomainKeys Identifed Mail

DKIM is the new hope for positively identifying a sender's organization. If widely deployed, it would curb spammers and fishers' ability to forge the sender's domain. Mail from your bank would really be from your bank—or at the very least, would not be categorized as phishing or spam.

DKIM replaces an earlier system called DomainKeys. DKIM uses public cryptography (with keys stored in DNS) to let receivers verify both the origin and integrity of a message. These guarantees are becoming essential in our connected world, where much business is done electronically. DKIM also prevents a sender from denying that he sent a message, a feature known as nonrepudiation.

A DKIM implementation has two halves: the part that signs outbound email as it leaves your server, and the part that verifies inbound email as it arrives. The first operation should be performed by your outgoing mail hub before the mail leaves your server (after any internal rewriting and content scanning has been done). The second part, verification, should be done as soon as a message is received, after other scanning tools add to change its headers.

sendmail.org has a couple of handy tools that are generic and can be used with any software that implements DKIM. The first generates the ADSP TXT records you must add to DNS to implement DKIM.^25. The second tool is a verifier that you can use to check your setup after it's all ready to go.

The details of DKIM and ADSP resource records are covered on page 591. They are currently TXT records but may get their own DNS resource type records in the future. Both OpenSSL and amavis include code to generate DKIM keys. Various DKIM milter packages also contain scripts that generate keys.

Once you have configured your MTA to generate DKIM signatures, you can send a message to sa-test@sendmail.net to verify that everything is working correctly. The server will email you back to tell you what features it received.

If you have a Gmail account, another way is to send yourself a message. Clicking on the "show details" link should reveal a signed-by field. You can also ask Gmail to "show original" in the drop-down menu—this command will show you the raw message with all its headers, including the DKIM signature.
