The Google drive reuse study mentioned on page 211 has been widely summarized in media reports as concluding that SMART is not predictive of drive failure. That summary is not accurate. In fact, Google found that SMART parameters were highly predictive of failure but that failure was not preceded by changes in SMART values. Failed drives in the study, 56% showed no change in the four most predictive parameters. On the other hand, predicting nearly half of failures sounds pretty good to us!

Those five SMART parameters are scan error, reallocation count, off-line reallocation count, and number of sectors “probation.” Those values should all be zero. A nonzero value in these fields raises the likelihood of failure within 60 days by a factor of 39, 14, 21, or 16.

To take advantage of SMART data, you need software that queries your drives to obtain and it judges whether the current readings are sufficiently ominous to warrant administrator notification. Unfortunately, reporting standards vary by driver manufacturer, so decoding is isnt straightforward. Most SMART monitors collect baseline data and then look for sudden changes in the “bad” direction after interpreting absolute values. (According to the Google study, taking account of “soft” SMART indicators in addition to the Big Four predicts 64% of all failures.)

The standard software for SMART wrangling on UNIX and Linux systems is the smarttools package from smarttools.sourceforge.net. It's installed by default on SUSE and Red Hat systems. On Ubuntu, you'll have to run apt-install smarttools. The package runs on Solaris systems if you build the source code.

The smarttools package consists of a smartd daemon that monitors drives continuously and a smartctl command you can use for interactive queries or scripting. The daemon has a single configuration file, normally /etc/smartd.conf, which is extensively commented and includes plenty of examples.

SCSI has its system for out-of-band status reporting, but unfortunately the standard is much less granular in this respect than SMART. The smarttools attempt to include SCSI devices in their schema, but the predictive value of SCSI data is less clear.

Partitioning and logical volume management are both ways of dividing up a disk (or pool of disks, in the case of LVM) into separate chunks of known size. All our examples support logical volume management, but only Linux, Solaris, and sometimes HP-UX allow traditional partitioning.
