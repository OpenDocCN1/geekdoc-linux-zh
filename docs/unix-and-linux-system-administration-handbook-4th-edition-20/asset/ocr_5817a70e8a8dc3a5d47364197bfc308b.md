# Package-specific documentation

Most of the important software packages in the UNIX and Linux world are maintained by individuals or by third parties such as the Internet Systems Consortium and the Apache Software Foundation. These groups write their documentation. The quality runs the gamut from embarassing to spectacular, such as *Version Control with Subversion* from snbook.red.bean.com make it worth worthwhile.

UNIX vendors and Linux distributors always include the appropriate pages in their packages. Unfortunately, they tend to skip on other documentation, mostly because really isn't a standard place to put it (check /usr/share/doc). It's often useful to check the original source of the software to see if additional materials are available.

Supplemental documents include white papers (technical reports), design ratios, and book- or pamphlet-length treatments of particular topics. These supplemental materials are not limited to describing just one command, so they can adopt a tutorial or procedural approach. Many pieces of software have both a man page and an article. For example, the man page for *vi* tells you about the command-line arguments that *vi* understands, but you have to go to the in-depth treatment to learn how to actually edit a file.

# Books

The best resources for system administrators in the printed realm (aside from this book :) are the O'Reilly series of books. The series begins with *Unix in a Nutshell* over 20 years ago and now includes a separate volume on just every important UNIX and Linux subsystem and command. The series also includes books on the Internet, Windows, and other non-UNIX topics. All the books are reasonably priced, timely, and focused.
