If you need a PPD file and your printer vendor doesn't supply one—say, because the printer doesn't have a PostScript interpreter and the vendor doesn't care about anything but Windows—go to linuxprinting.org and hunt through the Foomatic database for more information. Your printer may also be supported by the Gutenberg printer project (gutenprint.sourceforge.net). If you have a choice of PPDs from these sources and your users want last drop of quality, the ones marked “foomatic+gutenprint” are quite good. However, you'll still have to experiment with the printer configuration to find out what options give the best output.

If a PPD file is nowhere to be found:

+   • You have consulted linuxprinting.org before you acquired the printer. If you got the printer out of a dumpster, “free” doesn't always mean “inexpensive"

+   • There may be a PPD file that will let print something, even if it doesn't take advantage of all your printer's features. For example, we've had luck using generic HP drivers on non-HP printers.

Though CU depends on PPDs, older printing systems make no use of them. For the BSD or System V printing systems, you can either manage your Post-Script or live with what you get by default.

# 26.10 PAPER SIZES

Users want on physical sheets of paper. Paper comes in sizes and shapes. To make your users happy, you should know the basic facts about paper sizes.

In the United States and Canada, the most common paper size is called letter and is 8.5 × 11 inches. Some Linux distributions (e.g., Knoppix and SUSE) are produced in Europe, where they don't even know what inches are, or in England, where they do know but don't use them to measure paper. In these places, and the common paper type is called A4, and printers all use A4 trays. Some distributions' printing produce A4 pages by default.

A4 paper makes sense because it's irrational—mathematically speaking, the ratio of width to height of paper is √. If you slice a piece of paper in half horizontally, you get two half-size sheets of paper that have the same length-to-width ratio: √. This paper size is called A5. Cut in half and you get two sheets of A6. In the other direction, A3 is twice the area of A4, but the same shape, and so on.

In other words, you can manufacture A0 paper, which has an area of square meter, and use a paper cutter to create the other sizes you need. The only common U.S. paper size you can play this kind of game with is ledger (11 × 17 inches, also known as tabloid), which you can slice in half to get two sheets of letter.
