
## Chapter 8 Storage

SATA smooths many of PATA's sharp edges. It improves transfer rates (potentially to 70 MB/s with the upcoming 6 Gb/s SATA) and includes superior error checking. The standard supports hot-swapping, native command queuing, and sundry performance enhancements. SATA eliminates the need for master and slave designations because only a single device can be connected to each channel.

SATA overcomes the 18-inch cable limitation of PATA and introduces new data and power cable standards of 7 and 15 conductors, respectively. These cables are infinitely more flexible and easier to work with than ribbon cables—no curving or twisting to fit drives on the same cable. They do seem to be a bit more-sensitive than the old PATA ribbon cables, however. We have seen several of the cheap pack-in SATA cables that come with motherboards fail in actual use.

SATA cables slide easily onto their mating connectors, but can just easily slide off. Cables with locking connectors are available, but they're a messy blending. Onboards with six or eight SATA ports connected together, it can be hard to disengage the locking connectors without a pair of needle-nosed pins.

SATA also introduces an external cable standard called eSATA. The cables are electrically identical to SATA, but the connectors are slightly different. You can add an eSATA port to a system that has only internal SATA connectors by installing an inexpensive converter bracket.

Be really of extreme multrive enclosures that have only a single eSATA port—some of these smart (RAID) enclosures that require a proprietary driver (the drivers support UNIX or Linux). Other enclosures have a SATA port multiplier built in. These are potentially usable on UNIX systems, since not all SATA host adapters support port expanders, pay close attention to the compatibility information. Enclosures with multiple eSATA ports—one per drive—are always safe.

## Parallel SCSI

SCSI, the Small Computer System Interface, defines a generic data pipe that can be used by all kinds of peripherals. In the past it was used for disks, tape drives, scanners, and printers, but these most peripherals have abandoned SCSI in favor of USB.

Many factors of SCSI interface have changed since 1986, when SCSI-1 was first adopted as an ANSI standard. Traditional SCSI uses parallel cabling with 8 or 16 conductors.

Unfortunately, there is no real reason or shame to the naming conventions for parallel SCSI. The terms "fast," "wide," and "ultra" were introduced at various times to mark significant developments, but as those features became standard, the descriptors vanished from the names. The nimble-sensing Ultra SCSI is in fact a 20 MB/s standard that no one would dream of today, so it has to give way to Ultra2, Ultra3, Ultra-320, and Ultra-640 SCSI. For the curious, the following regular expression matches all the various flavors of parallel SCSI:

(Fast-Wide)?Ultra(( Wide)?[2(Wide)?[3]-320-[640])? SCSI]-[SCSI-[1-3])

Many different connectors have been used well. They vary depending on the version of SCSI, the type of connection (internal or external), and the number of data bits sent per cycle. The only one of these connectors still being manufactured today is the SCA-2, which is an 80-pin connector that includes both power and connections.

Each end of a parallel SCSI bus must have a terminating resistor (“terminator”). These resistors absorb signals as they reach the end of the bus and prevent noise from reflecting back onto the bus. Terminators take several forms, from small external plugs that you snap onto a regular port to tiny resistor packs that install onto a device’s circuit boards. Most modern devices are authentic.

If you experience seemingly hardware problems on your SCSI bus, check that ends of the bus are properly terminated. Improper termination is one of the most common SCSI configuration mistakes on old SCSI systems, and the errors it produces can be obscure and intermittent.
