# Free cooling

Nothing is more disturbing on a cold winter's day than walk outside a data center and see the compressor pad whirling at full speed. It's 10 degrees outside, but the HVAC engineer apparently designed a system that uses mechanical cooling (and an amazing amount of energy) to pull heat out of the data center regardless of the ambient temperature.

Many organizations are obsessed with availability (aka uptime). What often aren't considered are the additional energy and resources used to ensure a particular level of availability.

# Equipment life extension

Internal customers are accustomed to thinking of services as being either up or down. Consider offering degraded service as an additional choice for fault management, and ask whether that might meet the customers' availability needs.

For example, instead of running a fully redundant set of equipment for production environment, you could use server virtualization to deploy applications to a single chassis in the event of an outage. This configuration might supply all the standard functionality, but at slower speed than normal. In some cases, this tradeoff can reduce the organization's capital costs by 50% or more.

# Equipment life extension

Electronics manufacturing consumes energy and generates toxic waste, so purchases of new equipment entail an environmental cost that isn't necessarily reflected in the price tag. Unfortunately, the technology industry has become accustomed to rapid innovation and product development that manufacturers of not continue support for equipment after just a few years.

If your current equipment meets your business needs and is energy efficient, you may want to consider a life extension strategy. Such a scheme typically involves scouring ebay and other sources of salvage equipment for similar systems you can acquire cheaply and bring to your site a source of vintage spare parts. This approach typically extends system life by two to three years, though in at least one case we have kept a system running eight extra years this way.

If older equipment is not meeting performance requirements or cannot be supplemented by on-site stores, another option is to buy new equipment for the production environment and reassign the current equipment to a development environment, where performance and reliability are not important. This approach doesn't avoid new purchases entirely, but it may delay purchases for the development for a year or two.

If equipment simply must be retired, make sure that you turn it over to a legitimate computer recycler who will break it down into component pieces and recycle each piece properly. Make sure the recycler has a certified data destruction program so that your data doesn't later up in someone else's hands.

Computers contain a surprising amount of toxic waste. Whatever you do, just throw out old equipment into the dumpster—that typically goes to a landfill not designed to handle electronics.

Some regions have organizations that provide computer recycling services for free. In the Portland, Oregon, area, freegeek.org is a model recycling program.

# Warmer temperature in the data center

Approximately one-third of the energy consumed in a traditional data center goes to support cooling. Historically, data centers have maintained temperatures in the range of 68–77 degrees Fahrenheit. These values are now seen as conservative.

In 2009, the American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-Conditioning Engineers (ASHRAE) issued guidance that an expanded range of 64.4–80.6 degrees Fahrenheit is acceptable for data centers. Raising the data center temperature by three degrees typically saves estimated 12% in cooling costs.

See Chapter 27, Data Center Basics, for additional cooling tips.

# Low-power equipment

When procuring equipment, take the time to select products that have mini-environmental impact.

The IEEE has standardized the criteria for environmental assessment of electronics in IEEE publication 1680. The Electronic Products Environmental Assessment Tool (EPEAT), considers a wide range of potential impacts that might be involved in a product’s manufacture. It can help you procure products uniformly. The system currently covers desktop and laptop computers, for clients, workstations, and computer monitors. It is required for U.S. federal government purchases. Visit EPEAT at epeat.net.

Note that EPEAT compliance conforms to Energy Star standards (in version 5.0 as of July 1, 2009) for energy consumption during use.

Some manufacturers (including Dell, Sun, IBM, and HP) offer environmentally focused product families. But even eco-friendly servers offer environmental impact and consume power. The existence of these product lines should not be viewed as a license to add equipment in the name of being green. Focus first on reducing the number of servers that you need, then pick the most eco-friendly option for meeting that need.

- Monitors – replace CRTs with LCD monitors. They use significantly less power and contain fewer toxic elements.
- Workstation idle – centrally configure workstations to “sleep” or power-off for a given period (e.g., 30 minutes).
- Workstation count – limit desktops to one per user. Users who claim need more than one workstation should be encouraged to use a desktop environment.
- Task-based sizing – don’t “one size fits all” workstations. Have three or four tiers of workstation specifications so that users have appropriate configuration for their task.
- Personal heaters – this is not really an IT topic it’s a bit of ours, and the IT department is usually the one to notice. Do not allow the use of personal heaters in users' offices or cubicles. Explain to users that such heaters feed a vicious cycle in which office HVAC and the heater fight in an effort to enforce different temperature targets. Escalate the issue if the user’s area is truly the wrong temperature, with the appropriate HVAC support team. (Maybe you can offer them some VIP support in exchange for their assistance.)
- Print duplexing – configure printers to double-sided, two-up printing. This works fine for most routine printing, and users can always select something other than the default for special cases.
- Document campaign – launch a campaign or contest within your organization to find ways to eliminate the use of printed documents.
- Office temperature – since office computing equipment is designed to work at much higher temperatures than humans are, raise that office cooling setting to 78°F or higher.
