The power allocations for the upper tiers in Table 27.2 may seem generous, but they are not so hard to reach, even with today's equipment. APC measured the power consumption of a chassis containing 14 IBM Blade HS20s at 4,050 watts. Six of those classes in a rack consume 24.3 kW. Without cooling, it's enough power to reduce 50 pounds of steel, aluminum, or silicon to a liquid puddle within 15 minutes. Needless to say, you'll need special cooling arrangements and multiple power supplies for these configurations.

Once you've defined your power tiers, estimate your need for racks in each tier. On the floor plan, put racks from the same tier together. Zoning concentrates the high-power racks and lets you plan cooling resources accordingly.

# kVA vs. kW

One of the many disconnects between IT folks, facilities, and UPS engineers is each of these groups uses different units for power. The amount of a UPS is typically labeled in kVA (kilovolt-amperes). By computer engineers and the electrical engineers who support your data center usually express power in watts (W) or kilowatts (kW). You might remember from fourth grade science class that watts = volts × amps. Unfortunately, your fourth grade teacher failed them that watts is a vector value, which for AC power includes a "power factor" (pf) in addition to volts and amps.

If you are designing a bottle-filling line at a brewery that involves large motors and other heavy equipment, ignore this section and hire a qualified engineer to determine the correct power factor to use in your calculations. For modern-day computer equipment, you can cheat and use a constant. The equations you use for a "probably good enough" conversion between kVA and kW are

```
kVA / .85
kW = kVA * .85
```
