One person who received a tape from Ken was Professor Robert Fabry of the University of California at Berkeley. By January 1974, the seed of UNIX had been planted.

Other computer scientists around the world also took an interest in UNIX. In 1976, John Lions (on the faculty of the University of New South Wales in Australia) published a detailed commentary on a version of the kernel called V6. This effort became the first serious documentation of the UNIX system and helped others to understand and expand upon Ken and Dennis's work.

Students at Berkeley enhanced the version of UNIX they had to meet their needs. The first Berkeley tape (1BSD, for 1st Berkeley Software Distribution) included a Pascal system and for the PDP-11. The student behind the release was a grad student named Bill Joy. 2BSD caused the next year, and 3BSD, the first Berkeley release for the DEC VAX, was distributed in 1979.

In 1980, Professor Fabry struck a deal with the Defense Advanced Research Project Agency (DARPA) to continue the development of UNIX. This arrangement led to the formation of the Computer Systems Research Group (CSRG) at Berkeley. The next year, 4BSD was released. It became quite popular, largely because it was the only version of UNIX that ran on the DEC VAX 11/70, the computing platform of the time. Another big advantage of 4BSD was the introduction of TCP/IP sockets, the generalized networking abstraction that spawned the Internet and is now used by most modern operating systems. By the mid-1980s, most universities and research institutions were running at least one UNIX system.

In 1982, Bill Joy took the 4.2BSD tape with him to start Microsystems (now of Oracle America) and the SunOS operating system. In 1983, the court-ordered divestiture of AT&T began. An unexpected effect of the divestiture was that AT&T was now free to begin selling UNIX as a product. They released AT&T UNIX V, a well-recognized albeit somewhat awkward commercial implementation of UNIX.

Now that Berkeley, AT&T, Sun, and other UNIX systems were available to a wide variety of organizations, the foundation was laid for a general computing infrastructure built on UNIX technology. The same system that used by the astronomy department to calculate star distances could be used by the applied math department to calculate Mandelbrot sets. And that system was simultaneously providing email to the entire university.

# THE RISE OF SYSTEM ADMINISTRATORS

The management of general-purpose computing systems demanded a different set of skills than those required just two decades earlier. Gone were the days of the system operator who focused on getting a single computer system to perform a specialized task. # A Brief History of System Administration

System administrators came into their own in the early 1980s as people who ran UNIX systems to meet the needs of a broad array of applications and users.

Because UNIX was popular at universities, and because those environments included lots of students who were eager to learn the latest technology, universities were early leaders in the development of organized system administration. Universities such as Purdue, the University of Utah, the University of Colorado, the University of Maryland, and the State University of New York (SUNY) Buffalo became bothers of system administration.

System administrators also developed an array of their own processes, standards, best practices, and tools (such as sudo). Most of these were built out of necessity; until, unstable systems and unhappy users were the result.

Evi Nemeth became known as the "mother of system administration" by undergraduates to work as system administrators to support the Engineering College at the University of Colorado. Her close ties with Berkeley, the University of Utah, and SUNY Buffalo created a system community that shared tips and how. Her, often called the "munchkins" or "Evi slaves" used USENIX and other conferences and worked as on-site in exchange for the opportunity to absorb information at the conference.

It clear out that system administrators had to be jacks of all trades. A system administrator might start a typical day in the 1980s by using a wirewrap tool to fix an interrupt jumper on a VAX backplane. Mid-morning tasks might include sucking pulsed toner out of a malfunctioning first-generation printer. Lunch hour could be spent helping a grad student debug a new kernel driver, the afternoon might consist of writing backup tapes and harassing users to clean up their home directories to make space in the filesystem. A system administrator was, a-everything, take-no-prisons guardian angel.

The 1980s were a time of unreliable hardware. Rather than living on a single chip, the CPUs of the 1980s were made of several hundred chips, all of them prone to failure. It was the system administrator’s job to isolate failed-hardware and get it replaced, quickly. Unfortunately, these were also the days before it was common to FedEx parts on a whim, so finding the right part from a local source was often a challenge.

In one case, our beloved VAX 11/780 was down, leaving the entire campus without email. We knew there was a business down the street that packaged VAXes to be shipped to the (then cold-war) Soviet Union for "research purposes." Desperate, we showed up at their warehouse with a huge wad of cash in our pocket, and after an hour of negotiation, we escaped with the necessary board. At that time, someone remarked that it felt more comfortable to buy drugs than VAX parts in Boulder.

# SYSTEM ADMINISTRATION DOCUMENTATION AND TRAINING

As more individuals begin to identify themselves as system administrators—and as it became clear that one might make a decent living as a sysadmin—requests for documentation and training became more common. In response, folks like Tim O'Reilly and his team (then O'Reilly and Associates, now O'Reilly Media) to publish UNIX documentation that was based on hands-on experience and written in a straightforward way.

As a vehicle for in-person interaction, USENIX Association had its first conference on system administration in 1987. This LISA held mostly to a west coast crowd. Three years later, the SANS (SysAdmin, Audit, Security) Institute was established to meet the needs of the east coast. Today, both are still going strong.

In 1989, we published the first edition of this book, titled UNIX Administration Handbook. It was quickly embraced by the community, perhaps because of the lack of alternatives. At the time, UNIX was unfamiliar to our publisher whose production department replaced all instances of the string "etc" with "and so on." We took advantage of the situation to seize total control of the bits from cover to cover, but the publisher is admittedly much more UNIX savvy today. We will omit a few other stories out of fear of souring our amicable relationship.

# UNIX HUGGED TO NEAR DEATH, LINUX IS BORN (1991–1995)

By 1990, it seemed that UNIX was well on its way to world dominance. It was unquestionablyably the option of choice for research and scientific computing, and it had been adopted by mainstream businesses as Taco Bell and McDonald's. Berkeley's CSRG, consisting of Kirk McKusik, Mike Krels, Keith Bosch, and others, had just released an earlier 4.3 release that added support for the CCI Power 6/32 (code named "Tahoe" processor).

Commercial releases of UNIX such as SunOS were thriving, their success driven by the power of the Internet and the first fighters of e-commerce. PC hardware had become a commodity. It was reasonably reliable, inexpensive, and relatively high-performance. Although versions of UNIX that ran on PCs did exist, all the good options were commercial and closed source. The fight was ripe for an open source PC UNIX.
