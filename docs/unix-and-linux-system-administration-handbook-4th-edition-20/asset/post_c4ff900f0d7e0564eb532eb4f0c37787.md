## AIX recipe

Run `lsdev -C disk` to see a list of the disks the system is aware of, then `lspv` to see which disks are already set up for volume management. The device that appears in the first list but not the second is your new disk. Let's say it's hdisk1.

Next, run the following command sequence, selecting appropriate names for the volume group (vgname), logical volume (volume), and mount point. Examples of reasonable choices: vgname, home, and /home.

```
$ sudo mkvg -y vgname hdisk1      # Create volume group
$ lsvg vg                    # Note freespace value
```
MAX LVs: 256      FREE PPS: 325 (freespace megabytes)

```
$ sudo crfs -jfs vgname -m mountpoint -a size=freespaceM
$ sudo mkdir mountpoint
$ sudo mount mountpoint
```

## 8.2 STORAGE HARDWARE

Even in today's Post-Internet world, there are only a few basic ways to store data: hard disks, flash memory, magnetic tapes, and optical media. The last technologies have significant limitations that disqualify them from use as a system's primary filesystem. However, they're still used for backups and for “near-line” storage—cases in instant access and rewritability are of primary concern.

See page 301 for a summary of current tape technologies.

After 40 years of hard disk technology, system builders are finally getting a practical alternative in the form of solid state disks (SSDs). These flash-memory-based devices offer a different set of tradeoffs against a standard disk, and they're sure to exert a strong influence over the architectures of databases, filesystems, and operating systems in the years to come.

At the same time, traditional hard disks are continuing their exponential increases in capacity. Twenty years ago, a 60MB hard disk cost $1,000. Today, a variety of 1TB drive runs $80 or so. That's 20,000 times more storage for the money, or double the MB/s every 1.15 years—nearly the rate predicted by Moore's Law. During the same period, the sequential throughput of market-drive drives has increased from 5 KB/s to 100 MB/s, a comparatively powerful factor of 200. And random-access seek times have hardly changed. The more things change, the more they stay the same.

A third—hybrid—category, hard disks with large flash-memory buffers, were touted a few years ago but never actually materialized in the marketplace. It's not clear to us whether the drives were delayed by technical, manufacturing, or marketing concerns. They may appear on the scene, but the implications for system administrators remain unclear.

Disk sizes are specified in gigabytes that are billions of bytes, as opposed to memory, which is specified in gigabytes (gibibytes, really) of $2^{30}$ (1,037,741,824) bytes. The difference is about 7%. Be sure to check your units when estimating capacities and capacities.

See page 14 for more information on IEC units (gibibytes, etc.).

Hard disks and SSDs are enough alike that they can be drop-in replacements for each other, at least at the hardware level. They use the same hardware interfaces and protocols. And they have different strengths, as summarized in Table 8.1. Performance and cost values are of mid-2010.

### Table 8.1 Comparison of hard disk and SSD technology

| Characteristic | HD | SSD |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Terabytes | Gigabytes |
| Random access time | 8ms | 0.25ms |
| Sequential read | 100 MB/s | 250 MB/s |
| Random read | 2 MB/s | 250 MB/s |
| Cost | $0.10/GB | $3/GB |
| Reliability | Moderate | Unknown |
| Limited writes | No | Yes |

## Hard disks

A typical hard drive contains several rotating platters coated with magnetic film. They are read and written by tiny heads that are mounted on a metal arm that swings back and forth to position them. The heads float close to the surface of the platters but do not actually touch.

Reading from a platter is quick; it is the mechanical maneuvering needed to address a particular sector that drives down random-access throughput. There are two main sequences of delay.

First, the head arm must swing into position over the appropriate track. This part is called seek delay. Then, the system must wait for the next sector to pass underneath the head as the platter rotates. That part is rotational latency. Disks can stream data at tens of MB/s if reads are optimally sequenced, but random reads are fortunate to achieve more than a few MB/s.

A set of tracks on different platters that are all the same distance from the spindle is called a cylinder. The cylinder's data can be read without any additional movement of the arm. Although heads move amazingly fast, they still move much slower than the disks spin around. Therefore, any disk access that does not require the heads to seek to a new position will be faster.

Rotational speeds have increased over time. Currently, 7,200 RPM is the standard for market-oriented drives, 10,000 RPM and 15,000 RPM are popular. Higher rotational speeds decrease latency and increase the bandwidth. But random reads are fortunate to achieve more than a few MB/s. ## Solid state disks

SSDs use flash memory to store data. Unlike hard disks, they have no moving parts, which makes them faster, quieter, and more durable. However, they are also more expensive per gigabyte of storage.

The main components of an SSD are the controller, which manages the data, and the NAND flash memory cells. The controller is responsible for tasks such as wear leveling, error correction, and data mapping.

One limitation of flash memory is the finite number of times each cell can be written and erased. However, for most users, this is not a major concern in normal use. To extend the life of an SSD, controllers use wear leveling algorithms to ensure that all cells are used evenly.

Another advantage of SSDs is their low power consumption. Because they have no moving parts, they consume much less power than hard disks. This makes them ideal for laptops and other mobile devices.

In conclusion, SSDs are rapidly replacing hard disks in many applications due to their performance and reliability. As technology continues to improve and prices fall, SSDs will likely become the standard for all storage.
