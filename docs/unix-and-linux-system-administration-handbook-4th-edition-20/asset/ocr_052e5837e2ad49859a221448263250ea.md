When you replace a cartridge, save the box and baggie the new cartridge came in use when recycling the spent one. Find a company to take the old cartridge off your hands.

Keyed consumables spurred the growth of companies ("punch and pours") that refill cartridges for a fraction of the new-cartridge price. Cartridge recyclers are usually punch-and-pours, so you can recycle your old cartridges and get replacements at the same time.

Opinions on the quality and life span of recycled cartridges vary. One punch-and-pour we won't know refill toner cartridges or sell manufactured ones because they believe the savings are less than the increased maintenance costs for the printers that use them.

Pay attention to the cost per page

Most expensive printers are sold at close to their manufacturing cost. The manufacturers make their money on the consumables, which are disproportionately expensive. As of this writing, a quick check reveals that Amazon is selling for $80 laser printer that takes toner costly $65. You can buy a cheap printer for less than $50 at Wal-Mart, but it won't be long before you need to buy a set of replacement inkjet cartridges that cost more than the printer. You can feign outrage over this, but printer companies have to make their money on something. Cheaper cartridges would mean cheaper printers.

A good rule of thumb is that inkjet printers last as long as you don't print with them. Laser printers have a higher initial cost, but the consumables are cheaper and last. A full-color page from an inkjet printer costs 20–50 times as much as an analog print from a laser printer. It also requires special paper and prints more slowly. Ink cartridges dry quickly and frequently plug up or go bad. The inkjet runs when wet, so don't use inkjet to print a recipe book for use in the kitchen. On the other hand, you can now get photo prints from an inkjet that looks as good as prints from a photo lab. Color laser No, by comparison.

All printers have failure-prone mechanical parts. Cheap printers break faster. In other words, its all tradeoffs. For low-volume, personal use—printing a web page or two a day, or printing a couple of rolls of film per month—a low-cost, general purpose inkjet is an excellent choice.

Next time you go printer shopping, estimate how long you want to keep your printer, how much printing you do, and what kind of printing you need before you buy. Assess quantitatively the long-term cost per page for each candidate printer. Ask your local punch-and-pour where they remanufacture cartridges for the printer, and at what price.
