# DLV: domain lookaside validation

you must publish your DNSKEY record in your own zone before your parent installs the corresponding DS record.

If your parent zone isn't signed, you must provide some other way for the outside world to verify that the public key published in your DNS is really yours. There are three different ways to do this:

+   - Use one of the trusted anchor repositories (TAR) to publish your public key; for example, the one from SecSpider. To use TAR keys on your servers, get the key lists from SecSpider, from the IANA (contains only TLDs), or from the RIPE-NCC TAR (contains only their zones, mostly European TLDs and reverse zones). Put these in a trusted-clause in your name server config file.
- Use a domain lookaside validation server as provided by ISC, the Internet Systems Consortium. This service essentially makes isc your adaptive DNS parent. It's easy and free; see isc.org/ops/dlv. Other DLV servers exist, but you can use only one, and ISC is well established to run.
- Use Variages (vantage-points.org), a daemon that partners with copies of itself that belongs to you trust, forming a social network of daemons that can obtain keys independently from different sites on the Internet and compare their results to decide if the keys are authentic.

We describe the DLV solution in the next section. But all three of these options are interim solutions designed to help with incremental DNSSEC deployment. If your parent zone is already signed, don't even consider these options. Just give your parent the DS record.

# DLV: domain lookaside validation

A DNSSEC-aware caching server that receives a signed query first verifies that the record signatures match the domain's public key as specified by its DNSKEY record. The client then attempts to validate the key by looking in the parent zone for a DS record. If no DS record is available, the client looks for a DLV record in the original domain; or record redirects to dlv.isc.org or whichever ever DLV server is acting as the zone's foster parent. Once the client gets hold of the DLV record from dlv.isc.org, it can verify the chain of trust. Setting up DLV service for your zone is therefore a matter of generating the appropriate DLV records and putting them in the right places.

A DLV record is really just a DS record in disguise. The record type is different, but the body of the record is the same. The record's name field is modified to place the record into the DLV provider's zone. For example, example.com might become example.com.dlv.isc.org.

In BIND, dnssec-signzone -1 (lowercase letter L) generates the DLV record:

```
$ dnssec-signzone -1 dlv.isc.org example.com
```
