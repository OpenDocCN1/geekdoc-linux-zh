
# Scope and descriptions of services

The requirements for happy customers and happy IT staff have some factors in common. However, a few things seem to be orthogonal or even in direct conflict. The boss must make sure that these differing expectations can be made compatible and attainable.

An SLA helps align users and support staff. A well-written SLA addresses each of the issues discussed in the following sections.

# Scope and descriptions of services

This section is the foundation of the SLA because it describes what the organization can expect from IT. It should be written in terms that can be understood by nontechnical staff. Some examples might be

- Email
- Internet and web access
- File servers
- Business applications
- Printing

Here is a list of issues to consider when documenting your standards:

- Response time
- Service (and response times) during weekends and off-hours
- House calls (support for machines at home)
- Weird (unique or proprietary) hardware
- Upgrade policy (ancient hardware, software, etc.)
- Supported operating systems
- Standard configurations
- Expiration of backup tapes
- Special-purpose software
- Janitorial chores (cleaning screens and keyboards, vacuuming grills)

When considering service standards, keep in mind that many users will want to customize their environments (or even their systems) if the software is not locked down to prevent this. The stereotypical IT response is to respond to all modifications, but this policy makes things easier for IT, it isn't necessarily the best policy for the organization.

Address this issue in your SLAs, and try to standardize on a few specific configurations. Otherwise, your goals of easy maintenance and scaling to grow with the organization will meet some serious impediments. Encourage your creative, hacking employees to suggest modifications that they need for their work, and be diligent and generous in incorporating these suggestions into your standard configurations. If you don't, your users will work hard to thwart your rules.

# Queue management policies

In addition to knowing what services are provided, users must also know about the priority scheme used to manage the work queue. Priority schemes always have wiggle room, but try to design one that covers most situations with few or no exceptions. Priority-related variables are listed below:

- The importance of the service to the overall organization
- The security impact of the situation (has there been a breach?)
- The service level the customer has paid or contracted for
- The number of users affected
- The importance of any relevant deadline
- The loudness of the affected users (sneaky wheels)
- The importance of the affected users (this is a tricky one, but let's honest: some people in your organization have more pull than others)

Although all these factors will influence your rankings, we recommend a simple set of rules with some common sense to deal with the exceptions. Basically, we use the following priorities:

- Many people cannot work
- One person cannot work
- Requests for improvements

If two or more requests have top priority and the requests cannot be worked on in parallel, we base our decision regarding which problem to tackle on the severity of the issues (e.g., email not working makes everybody unhappy, the temporary unavailability of a web service might hinder only a few people). Queues at lower priorities are usually handled in a FIFO manager.

Users generally assume that all their data is stored on backup tapes will be archived forever. Backup media don't last indefinitely; magnetic media in particular have a finite lifetime after which reading data becomes difficult. (You might periodically rewrite your data, possibly to notify, if you need it for a long time.) Backup tapes can also be subpoenaed, so your organization may not want data to be available forever. It's best to work with the people in charge of such decisions to draw up a written agreement that specifies how long backups must be kept, whether multiple copies are to be made (required? permissible? never?), and where those copies must be stored.

These decisions should be made in the context of an organization-wide data retention policy. This type of policy is covered later in this chapter, but you need to classify your data and develop a retention schedule for each class.

Make your backup and retention policies available to users. This measure promotes realistic expectations regarding both backups and recoveries. It also puts users on notice that they must take precautions of their own if they need better data protection than is provided for in SLA.

In particular, users should understand whether or not the files on their local workstations will be backed up. Many organizations back up their centralized servers do not back up individual workstations. Usually, the workstations are cloned from system images and are considered disposable. Users need to know this so they can store critical information appropriately.

# Roles and responsibilities

You must document who is responsible for what. Organizations that do not divide duties become inefficient and ineffective. Problems fall through the cracks because it's not who owns problem domains. Problems can also fall prey to groupthink, where it takes two or three administrators to take care of a single task. Some examples of defined roles are the:

- Backup administrator
- Storage area network (SAN) and file service maven
- Application wrangler
- Patching and security czar
- Guy nobody is quite sure what he does

Or, you might map out roles and responsibilities according to the descriptions of services you have already defined. This-approach may imply that you have to delineate responsibilities from an administration perspective rather than the user's point of view.

Don't forget to include "understudy" responsibilities in your taxonomy. Staff members won't be in the office every day, and you need to know who to go to when a domain's primary administrator is away.

# Conformance measurements

An SLA needs to define how the organization will measure your success through the terms of the agreement. Targets and goals allow the staff to work towards a common outcome and can lay the groundwork for cooperation throughout the organization. Of course, you must make sure you have venues in place to measure the agreed-upon metrics.

At a minimum, you should track the following metrics for your infrastructure:

- Percentage of projects completed on time and on budget
- Percentage of SLA elements fulfilled
- Uptime by system (e.g., 99.9% available through Q1)
- Percentage of tickets that were satisfactorily resolved
- Average time to ticket resolution
- Percentage of security incidents handled according to the incident handling process

## 32.2 THE STRUCTURE OF AN IT ORGANIZATION

Now that we have addressed the overall function of an IT organization, we can peek inside its structure. As a support organization grows, it becomes clear that not everybody in the group or know everything. Instead, you must find a balance between efficiency and separation of duties.

Role separation adds a layer of checks and balances to the IT organization. Over time, this feature is becoming more and more important as standards and regulations creep into even the smallest of organizations.

Take, for example, a 20-person U.S. company that has developed a hosted application for medical facilities. If this application stores any protected health information (PHI), then the organization's systems must all comply with the dreaded Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA). Among other things, this legislation requires you to define roles to protect sensitive data. For example, the tasks of determining what a user should have and of actually provisioning that access must be executed by two different people.
