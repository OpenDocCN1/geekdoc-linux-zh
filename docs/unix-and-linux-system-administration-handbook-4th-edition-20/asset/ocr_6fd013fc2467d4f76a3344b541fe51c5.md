Since most logs are text files, they can be viewed or parsed with standard tools such as cat, grep, tail, and Perl. Most modern systems also include log management tools that rotate, compress, and monitor log files on a daily or weekly basis.

Log files managed by syslog usually contain events from multiple sources. For example, complaints from the kernel and from a network daemon may appear adjacent to each other. At sites that have set up a centralized logging server, events from multiple hosts may be aggregated and processed.

The importance of having a well-defined, site-wide logging strategy has grown along with the adoption of formal IT standards such as COBIT and ISO 27002 as well as the maturing of industry regulations. These external standards may require you to maintain a centralized, hardened, enterprise-wide repository for log activity, with time stamps provided through NTP and a strict schedule. We discuss some specific strategies in this chapter.

# 11.1 FINDING LOG FILES

UNIX is criticized for being inconsistent, and it is just. Just take a look at a directory of log files and you're sure to find some with names mailog, like ftp.log, and maybe even some like ipNet, lpd-err, or console.log. In addition to having random names, log files are scattered across different directories and filesystems. By default, most of these are found in /var/adm or /var/log.

Linux systems are generally a bit more sane, although each distribution has its own way of naming and dividing the log files. For the most part, Linux packages are sending their logging information to files in the /var/log directory.
