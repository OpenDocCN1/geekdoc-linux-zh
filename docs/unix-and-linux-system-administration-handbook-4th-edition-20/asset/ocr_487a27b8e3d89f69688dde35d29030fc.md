# Solid state disks

SSDs read and writes across banks of flash memory cells, which are individually rather slower in comparison to modern hard disks. But because of parallelism, the SSD as a whole meets or exceeds the bandwidth of a traditional disk. The strength of SSDs is that they continue to perform well when data is read or written at random, an access pattern that's predominant in real-world use.

Storage device manufacturers like to quote sequential transfer rates for their products because the numbers are impressively high. But for traditional hard disks, these sequential numbers have no relationship to the throughput observed with random reads and writes. For example, Western Digital's high-performance Velociraptor can achieve nearly 120 MB/s in sequential transfers, but their random read results are on the order of 2 MB/s. By contrast, Intel's currentgeneration SSDs stay above 30 MB/s for all access patterns.

This performance comes at a cost, however. Not all SSDs are more expensive per gigabyte of storage that are hard disks, but they also introduce several wrinkles and uncertainties into the storage equation.

Each page of memory in an SSD (typically 4KiB on current products) can be rewritten only a limited number of times (usually around 100,000, depending on the underlying technology). To limit the wear on any given page, the SSD firm maintains a mapping table and distributes writes across all the drive's drives. This remapping is invisible to the operating system, which sees the drive as a series of blocks. Think of it as virtual memory for storage.

A further complication is that flash memory must be erased before it can be rewritten. Erasing is a separate operation that is slower than writing. It's impossible to erase individual pages—clusters of adjacent pages (typically 128 pages or 512KiB) must be erased together. The write performance of an SSD can drop substantially when the pool of pre-erased pages is exhausted and the drive must recover pages on the fly to service ongoing writes.

Rebuilding a buffer of erased pages is harder than it might seem because filesystems do not mark or erase data blocks that are no longer using. A storage device doesn't know the filesystem now considers a given block to be free; it only knows that someone gave it data to store there. For order an SSD to maintain its cache of pre-erased pages (and thus, its write performance), the filesystem has to be capable of informing the SSD that certain pages are no longer needed. As of this writing, ext4 and Windows 7 NTFS are the common filesystems that offer this feature. But given the enormous interest in SSDs, other filesystems are sure to become more SSD-aware in the near future.
