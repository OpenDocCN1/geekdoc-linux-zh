# Virtual private networks

Many organizations that have offices in several locations would like to have all those locations connected to one big private network. Such organizations can use the Internet as if it were a private network by establishing a series of secure, encrypted 'tunnels' among their various locations. A network that includes such tunnels is known as a virtual private network or VPN.

VPN facilities are also needed when employees must connect to their private network from their homes or from the field. A VPN system doesn't eliminate every possible security issue relating to ad hoc connections, but it's enough for many purposes.

See page 943 for more information about IPsec.

Some VPN systems use the IPsec protocol, which was standardized by the IETF in 1998 as relatively low-level adjustment to IP. Others, such as OpenVPN, implement VPN security on top of TCP using Transport Layer Security (TLS), formerly known as Secure Sockets Layer (SSL). TLS is also on the IETF's track, although it hasn't yet fully adopted.

A variety of proprietary VPN implementations are also available. These systems generally don't operate with each other or with the standards-based VPN systems, but that's not necessarily a major drawback if all the endpoints are under your control.

The TLS-based VPN solutions seem to be the marketplace winners at this point. They are as secure as IPsec and considerably less complicated. Having a free implementation in the form of OpenVPN doesn't hurt. (Unfortunately, it doesn't run on HP-UX or AIX.)

To support home and portable users, a common paradigm is for users to download a small Java or ActiveX component through their web browser. This component provides VPN connectivity back to the enterprise network. The mechanism is convenient for users, but be aware that the browser-based systems differ widely in their implementations: some provide VPN service through a pseudo-network-interface, others forward only specific ports. Still are little more than glorified web proxies.

Be sure you understand the underlying technology of the solutions you're considering, and don't expect the impossible.
