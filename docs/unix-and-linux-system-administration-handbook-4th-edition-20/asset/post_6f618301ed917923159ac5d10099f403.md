We suggest the following order of operations for sanity-checking a message:

- Checking RFC compliance of the sender's SMTP implementation
- Verifying the existence of local recipients

Many spam robots do not follow the SMTP protocol correctly; they typically start before the EHLO response. A slight delay by your server can expose their "early talker" behavior. You can use this information to reject the connection or to increase the spam score of the received messages.

Checking recipients to verify that they are valid local users is good unless your later checks might transform recipients' addresses. Early checking minimizes the work your mail server has to do for mail that will eventually turn out to be undeliverable. It also eliminates lot of "shotgun" spam. However, it does let the sender probe your user address space.

The order of the other checks is primarily driven by their cost. Quick check and easy things before time-consuming things, that, on average, bad messages are refused as soon as possible.

Once you have identified a message as bad, what do you do with it? Refuse, drop, quarantine it, archive it? We recommend you quarantine and archive while you are testing your setup. When you are satisfied that the system is doing what you want, refuse or drop all viruses and refuse or archive spam, according to users' preferences. Delete archived spam that is more than a month old; while, users can move false positives from the spam box to their regular mailbox.

# Spam

Spam is the jargon for junk mail, also known as unsolicited commercial email or UCE. It becomes a serious problem because although the response per dollar is low (A list of 30 million email addresses costs $40). If it didn't work for spammers, it wouldn't be such a problem. Surveys show that 95%–98% of all mail is spam. Refer to spammological sites like spamhaus.net for the latest numbers.

Our main recommendation regarding spam is that you use the preventive measures and publicly maintained blacklists that are available to you. A good one is zen.spamhaus.org. Advise your users to delete the spam they receive. Many spam messages contain instructions on how recipients can be removed from the mailing list. If you follow those instructions, the spammers may remove you from the current list, but they immediately add you to several other lists with the annotation "reaches a real human who reads the message". Your email address is then worth more.

Folks that sell email addresses to spammers use a form of dictionary attack to harvest addresses. Starting with a list of common names, the scanning software adds first initials in hopes of hitting on a valid email address. To check the addresses, the software connects to the mail servers at, say, 50 large ISPs and does a VRFY, EXPN, or RCPT on each of trillions of addresses. MTAs can block the SMTP commands VRFY and EXPN, but not RCPT. Such actions may delay your mail server and interfere with it being able to accept and deliver email promptly. To protect themselves from this sort of abuse, MTAs can rate-limit the RCPTs from a single source.

# Forgeries

Forging is trivial; many user agents let you fill in the sender's address anything you want. MTAs can use SMTP authentication between local servers, but that doesn't scale to Internet sizes. Some MTAs add warning headers to outgoing local messages that they think be forged.

Any user can be impersonated in mail messages. Be careful if your organization's authorization vehicle for things like door keys, access cards, and money. You should warn administrative users of this fact and suggest that if they see suspicious mail that appears to come from a person in authority, they should verify the validity of the message. Caution is doubly appropriate if the message asks for unreasonable privileges be given to an unusual person.

# Message privacy

Message privacy essentially does not exist unless you use an external encryption package such as Good Privacy (PGP), the GNU Privacy Guard (GPG), or S/MIME. By default, all mail is unencrypted. End-to-end encryption requires support from mail agents.

Both S/MIME and PGP are documented in the RFC series, with S/MIME on the track. However, we prefer PGP and GPG; they are more widely available. PGP was designed by an excellent cryptographer, Phil Zimmermann, whom we trust.

# Spam filtering

The spam problem has led to an arms race between abatement and spammers, with more sophisticated techniques being deployed on both sides. Some of the current control measures are:

- Greylisting: temporary deferrals (a form of RFC checking)
- SpamAssassin, a heuristic pattern-matching tool
- Blacklists: lists of known bad guys in the spam world, often DNS-based
- Whitelists: lists of known good guys, DNS-based, avoid false positives
- Mail filters ("milters") that scan the headers and body of a message
- SPF and DKIM/ADC records to identify senders' domains and policies
- amavis-new and MailScanner: antivirus/antispam systems

We cover each of these in more detail later.

# When to filter

When to filter is a fundamental question, and one with no perfect answer. The main question is whether to filter "inline" with the SMTP transaction or after the mail has been accepted. There are advantages and disadvantages to both schemes. The advantages of inline (pre-queue) filtering include:

- You can reject the mail and thus not take responsibility for delivery (This may even be required for legal reasons in some countries!).
- The sender is notified reliably why the mail could not be delivered. You don't need to trust the sender of the mail; you just state the reason for rejecting the mail and let the originating sender deal with it. Much cleaner and more reliable than accepting the mail then bouncing it.

However, there are advantages to post-queue filtering too:

- The performance of your Internet-facing mail server is not dragged down by expensive checking. This is especially valuable when bursts of mail arrive at the same time.
- Filtering after a message has been queued is simpler and more robust.

At first glance you might think post-queue filtering is best. It doesn't impact your mail server and is easier on your sysadmins. However, the bounce messages generated by post-queue filtering become their own type of spam when the sender's address is forged—as it usually is.

# Greylisting/DCC

Greylisting is a scheme in which you configure your mail server to defer all connections from unrecognized IP addresses for 15 minutes to an hour. The server rejects the mail with a “try again” message. Real MTAs sending users’ email will wait and then try again; spammers will move on their lists and won't retry.

Greylisting has been implemented for hot MTAs; it is especially effective as a spam-fighting tool DCC (the Distributed Checksum Clearinghouses; from rhyholite.com/dcc) that detects the “bulkiness” of a message by a fuzzy checksum and seeing how other mail servers have seen that same checksum. It is not real spam detector per se, but a bulk detector. If you whitelist the bulk email you expect to receive (such as mailing lists you belong to), then the remaining detections of unsolicited bulk email, which is pretty much the definition of spam.

DCC can do greylisting as well; it is used as a filter and greylist or reject inline during an SMTP session. Because DCC does not do pattern matching as SpamAssassin-type tools do, it is not fooled by spammers who add randomness to their messages in an attempt to foil the pattern matchers.

The effectiveness of greylisting has declined (from more than 97% to below 90%) as spammers have begun to take it seriously and spruced up their SMTP implementations. However, it is still effective when used with blacklists because the automated maintainers often manage to spam sites onto the blacklist before the retry period has elapsed.

# SpamAssassin

SpamAssassin (spamassassin.apache.org) is an open source Perl module written by Habeeb Dihu and maintained by Ian Lustman. It is a good product of identifying spam. It can be invoked through a filter and is used in lots of antispam products.

SpamAssassin uses a variety of ad hoc rules to identify spam. The rules used to be updated frequently, but they seem to be less actively maintained these days.
