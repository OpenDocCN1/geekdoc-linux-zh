## Blacklists

See Chapter 17 for more information about DNS.

Several organizations (e.g., spamhaus.org) compile lists of spammers and publish in the DNS. The MTAs can be configured to check these blacklists (also known as Realtime Black Lists or RBLs) and reject mail that comes from listed sites.

There are also lists of open relays, that is mail servers that are willing to forward a message from the Internet to a user outside their local site without authenticating the sending server. Spammers use open relays to obfuscate the origin of their messages and fight the cost of working their huge volumes of email onto other sites.

## Whitelists

Whitelists are DNS-based reputation lists that are essentially the opposite of the blacklists described above. They are used to reduce the number of false positives generated by spam filters. One whitelist, dnswl.org, rates domains as follows:

- High – never sends spam
- Medium – rarely sends spam, fixes spam problems when they occur
- Low – occasionally sends spam, slower to correct it
- None – legitimate mail server might send spam

They recommend that you omit of your usual mail scanning for every domain with a rating.

- Skip blacklisting and greylisting for every domain with a rating.
- Skip spam filtering for domains with ratings of high or medium.
- Never skip virus scanning.

The web site includes details for using the whitelist with each of the MTAs we describe in this book. Lookups are done through DNS, as with blacklists. For example, if you want to know the rating of IP address 1.2.3.4, do you a DNS query for the pseudo-host 4.3.2.1.dnswl.org. The return value is an IP address of 127.0.x.y, where x is a number that identifies the domain's general category of business (e.g., financial services or email marketing) and y is the site’s rating from 0–3 (0 = none, 3 = high).

To speed whitelist evaluations, you can download the data for the entire whitelist and **rsync** it to keep current; don't choose the even hour or half hour for your **cron** job.

## Miltering: mail filtering

The developers of sendmail created an API that lets third-party programs filter the headers and content of mail messages as they are being processed by the MTA. These "milters" are used for spam fighting, virus detection, statistical analysis, encryption, and host of other purposes. Milters are fully supported by the sendmail and Postfix MTAs; Exim uses filters and ACLs instead. For a catalog of available milters, complete with user ratings, license information, and statistics on downloads and updates.

MTAs invoke milters on incoming messages while they are still connected to the sending site. The MTA can recognize the profile of a virus or spam message and report back to the MTA, discard the message, create log entries, or take whatever other action you feel is appropriate. Milters have access to metadata and message content.

Miltering is potentially a powerful tool for fighting spam and viruses for violating users' privacy. A tough situation evolves when managers want to know exactly what proprietary information is leaving the organization by email, while employees feel that their email should be private. Make sure employee agreements are explicit about any kind of scanning you intend to do.

## SPF and Sender ID

The best way to fight spam is to stop it at its source. This sounds simple and easy, but in reality it is an impossible challenge. The structure of the Internet makes it difficult to track the real source of a message and to verify its authenticity. The community needs a sure-fire way to verify that the entity sending an email is really who or what it claims to be.

Many proposals have addressed this problem, but SPF and Sender ID have achieved the most traction. SPF, or Sender Policy Framework, by the IETF in RFC4408 defines a set of DNS records (see page 588) through which an organization can identify its official outbound mail servers. MTAs can then refuse email purporting to be from organization’s domain if the email does not originate from one of these.
