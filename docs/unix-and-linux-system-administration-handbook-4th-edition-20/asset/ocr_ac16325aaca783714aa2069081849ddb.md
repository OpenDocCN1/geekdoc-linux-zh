Rotational speeds have increased over time. Currently, 7,200 RPM is the mass-market standard for performance-oriented drives, 10,000 RPM and 15,000 RPM drives are popular at the high end. Higher rotational speeds decrease latency and increase the bandwidth of data transfers, but the drives tend to run hot.

Hard disks fail frequently. A 2007 Google Labs study of 100,000 drives the tech world with the news that hard disks more than two years old had an annual failure rate (AFR) of more than 6%, much higher than the failure rates predicted based on their extrapolation of short-term testing. The overall pattern was a few months of infant mortality, a two-year honeymoon then a jump to the 6%-8% AFR range, overall disks in the Google study had less than a 75% chance of surviving a five-year tour of duty.

Interestingly, Google found no correlation between failure rate and two environmental factors that were formerly thought to be important: operating temperature and drive activity. The complete paper can be found at tynurl.com/fail-pdf.

Disk failures tend to involve either platter surfaces (bad blocks) or mechanical components. The firmware and hardware interface usually remain operable after a failure, so you can query the disk for details (see page 230).

Drive reliability is often quoted by manufacturers in terms of mean between failures (MTBF), denoted in hours. A typical value for an enterprise drive is around 1.2 million hours. However, MTBF is a statistical measure and should not read to imply that an individual drive will run for 14 years before failing.

MTBF is the inverse of AFR in the drive's steady-state-period—that is, in bit before wear-out. A manufacturer's MTBF of 1.2 million hours corresponds to an AFR of 0.7% per year. This value is almost, but not quite, concordant with the AFR range observed by Google (1%–2%) during the first two years of sample drives.

Manufacturers' MTBF values are probably accurate, but they are cherry-picked from the most reliable phase of each drive's life. MTBF values should be regarded as an upper bound on reliability; they do not predict your expected failure rate over the long time. Based on the limited data quoted above, you might consider dividing' MTBFs by a factor of 7.5 or so to arrive at a more realistic estimate of five-year failure rates.

Hard disks are commodity products, and one manufacturer's model is much like another, given similar specifications for spindle speed, hardware interface, and reliability. These days, you will need a dedicated qualification laboratory to make distinctions among competing drives.
