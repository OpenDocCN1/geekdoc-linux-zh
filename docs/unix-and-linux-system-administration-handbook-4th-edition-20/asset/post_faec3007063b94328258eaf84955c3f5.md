Why were there competing printing systems, and was there any important difference between them? Stand up in the middle of a users' group meeting and yell, "Anyone who vi instead of emacs is an idiot!" Then come us again.

Network printing added another universe of complexity. Early printing systems were idiosyncratic and used an assortment of protocols for printer-to-spooler communication, client-to-spooler communication, and network traffic negotiation.

HP's JetDirect printers often accepted raw data on port 9100, as did printers from other manufacturers that adopted HP's convention. Printers with internal lpd daemons (implementations of the BSD protocol) expected jobs on port 515.

Gritting its teeth, the IETF' Printer Working Group created the Internet Printing Protocol (IPP), which built on top of HTTP. This chose structured interactions in terms of simple GET and POST requests and let take advantage of standard technologies for authentication, access control, and encryption.

Michael Sweet and Andrew Sent of Easy Software Products (ESP) brought IPP to UNIX in the form of the CUPS implementation. Apple adopted CUPS for Mac OS X (and, in 2007, bought the source code), and CUPS became the most complete implementation of IPP on the planet. CUPS is an open source project, fixes many older systems' problems, and is freely redistributable.

## Printer diversity

In addition to diversity in print systems, administrators face diversity in the printers themselves.

Because printers plug into computers, users tend to lump them in peripherals as much as mice and monitors. They're more complicated than that. They're really more like smartphones or routers, but with moving parts.

At one time, the most powerful computer made was the Apple LaserWriter. Today, your desktop machine is probably more powerful than your printer, but the printer is still a computer. It has a CPU, memory, an operating system, and perhaps even a disk.

If it's a network printer, it has its own network stack and IP address. If you have a network printer around, enter its address (or DNS name) into your web browser. Chances are, the printer will serve up web pages that let you administer the printer hardware: the printer is running its own web server.

(Since system administrators are secured, you may already be thinking, "Does that mean a printer could be compromised or hit by a denial of service attack?" You bet. See the section on security that starts on page 1081.)

## Common printing software

What operating system is your printer running? What?! You don't know? Not surprising. You probably can't find out, either, without some digging—and perhaps not even then. The operating system varies from vendor to vendor and sometimes even from model to model. Mid-range and higher-end printers may run some derivative of UNIX or Linux.

Your printer may handle a variety of network protocols and accept jobs in any of several different printer-specific page-description and document-description languages. You may even understand and print bitmap formats such as GIF, JPG, and TIFF.

Your printer may only print in black and white, or it may print in color. Your printer prints pages at resolutions that range from 150 through 2400 dots per inch (dpi), or even at asymmetric resolutions such as 1200 × 1200dpi in one direction and 600 in the other.

If you're administering a larger facility, you may need to support several models of printers from several different manufacturers, each of which has different capabilities. This state of affairs means that the printing software on your computers must be prepared to communicate with diverse (and some unknown) hardware-through an array of protocols.

## 26.7 COMMON PRINTING SOFTWARE

There's more to printing than spooling and printing jobs. Even on a Ubuntu system (which uses CUPS), the command

```
$ man -k | egrep 'ghostscript|cupsprint(erlji)|jobqueuefilter'
```

lists well over a hundred printing-related pages—and that's just a quick and dirty search. (Not everything will be printing-related. apd is a daemon that talks to Universal Power Supplies by APC, and even the print command has nothing to do with printing.) Several of these commands and tools are worth knowing about and across all three of the printing systems covered in this book.

Both the BSD and System V systems lack many of the translation facilities that are needed to drive modern printers. So, most vendors who use these systems have at least one set of tools on top of their printing system to provide additional features. These tools are sometimes included in the OS, but more often they are extra add-ons. Third-party and freely distributed packages are also used in wide use.

lpr is one of the oldest printing tools. It reformats text for the printed page. It breaks its input into lines, adds headers and footers, and can do space-saving. (Why? Because that's how many lines fit on an old, black-and-white printer.)

Adobe’s enscript command performs similar conversions with quite a few bells and whistles. GNUscript is an open source version of this command that is backward compatible with Adobe’s; however, it offers a wealth of new features, including highlighting for various paper sizes, font downloading, and user-defined headers. One of encscript’s main claims to fame was its implementation of page-independent printing. If you’re not using CUPS, this feature can still be useful. At the end of the complexity spectrum is Ghostscript, originally written by Peter Deutsch so that he could produce PostScript documents on inexpensive printers. Today, CUPS uses it as a filter, but Ghostscript can also create page images for the screen, either on its own or with help from front ends such as Evince, GNOME, or KDE’s GhostView. Linux distributions come with a free version of Ghostscript. If you need to install and build Ghostscript yourself, see ghostscript.com. A commercial version of Ghostscript with support is available from Artifex Software.

## 26.8 PRINTER LANGUAGES

A print job is really a computer program written in a specialized programming language. These programming languages are known collectively as page description languages or PDLs. Language interpreters for these PDLs often run inside the printers. Pages encoded in PDL can be much smaller and faster to transmit than the equivalent images. PD descriptions can also be device and resolution independent. You may encounter PostScript, PCL5, PCL6 (also called PCL/XL), and PDF. Many printers can accept input in more than one language. Printers have to interpret jobs in these languages and transform them into some form of bitmap representation that makes sense to the actual hardware. Just as with C or Java, these languages exist in multiple versions, and the versions make a difference. Most PostScript printers understand PostScript Level 3, but if you send a Level 3 program to a printer that only understands Level 2, the printer may be confused. Rasterizing a PDL (or anything else, such as an image file) into a bitmap page is called “raster image processing,” and a program that performs such rasterization is called a RIP. “To rip” is sometimes used informally as a verb—this has nothing to do with CDs, DVDs, BitTorrent, or DMCA.

## PCL

It's possible to rip print jobs in your computer and view the images on your display. We discuss host-based interpreters that do this, such as Ghostscript, on page 107. You could in theory use your computer to rip jobs for printing and ship the completed (and much larger) bitmaps off to a not-very-smart device. In fact, this is the way that many Windows "GDI" printers work. The level of support for this mode of operation varies widely among systems.

## PostScript

Post is still the most common PDL found on Unix and Linux systems. It was invented at Adobe Systems, and many PostScript printers use an interpreter from Adobe. Almost all page layouts can generate PostScript, and some work with PostScript exclusively. PostScript is a full-fledged programming language. You can read PostScript programs with a text editor or with less. The programs are full of parentheses, curly braces, slashes, and often start with the characters %PS. Although these starting characters are not required by the language itself, PostScript interpreters and other printing software often look for them when trying to recognize and classify print jobs.

## PCL

PCL is Hewlett-Packard's Printer Control Language. It's understood by HP printers and many other brands; some printers speak only PCL. Unlike PostScript, which is a Turing-complete, generalized programming language, PCL just tells how to print pages. PCL jobs are binary, not human readable, and much shorter than the equivalent PostScript. Applications seldom generate PCL directly, but filters can convert PostScript to PCL.

PCL also varies more than PostScript. The differences are minor but annoying. PCL jobs that print correctly on a LaserJet 5si can print slightly on a LaserJet 5500, and vice versa. It's not just a matter of models; every printer has a custom PCL dialect with commands to exploit that printer's features. For example, if you tell your computer you have a LaserJet 4500 when you actually have a LaserJet 4550, it may generate some PCL commands that the printer misinterprets. If you have a stored PCL print job—say, a blank purchase request form—and replace the printer for which it was generated with something new, you may have to regenerate the job.
