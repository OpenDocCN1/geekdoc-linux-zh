
Here is some information to gather as you start your green IT assessment:

- **Equipment survey** – everything, including servers, laptops, workstations, monitors, printers, storage devices, network gear, backup devices, UPSs, and cooling units. Capture the location, model number, "size" (in units appropriate to the specific equipment), and each age of item.

It's helpful to have power consumption data for each item well. Rated power consumption can be misleading—better to measure a device's actual energy use with a Kill A Watt meter, which costs around $20. For devices that have both active and sleep states (e.g., printers), you may want to record average energy use over a one-day or one-week period.

- **Accounting for consumables** – power, toner, storage media

- **Organizational metrics** – including gross revenue, number of employees, number of physical locations, total facility energy consumption, IT equipment consumption (in data centers), data center energy consumption, total IT capital cost, total IT operations cost, and total facilities for data centers.

Once you've collected this baseline data, identify three targets for optimization. These targets should be tied to your organization's overall strategy for success and growth, and if achieved, they should also demonstrate progress toward becoming a green IT shop. We can't tell you what targets will work best for your environment, but here are some examples:

- Data center energy consumption per dollar of gross revenue
- Number of employees per physical server
- Sheets of paper used per employee per month
- Average energy consumption of an employee's workspace equipment
- Average life of a laptop computer
- Data center energy use as a proportion of total facility use

Reducing direct consumption should always be your first-choice strategy; less is more. If you can achieve your mission with less effort and fewer resources, that eliminates both capital and operational costs.

## Application consolidation

Over time, organizations and IT departments tend to accumulate applications. New applications come onboard to support specific business initiatives and the CEO's projects, but old applications rarely die. More commonly, they linger "on the road to retirement" for a decade with no one willing to take the risk of pulling the plug. Whatever the reason, the number one opportunity for progress in an established organization is to consolidate applications to the minimum set that meets current business needs.

Let's consider an example that has three applications: EmployeeLinq, AccountAwesome, and ElectricClockster. Although this is a simplified example, it's loosely based on real-world applications used by one organization that we examined. Each of the applications had a back-end database server, an application server, and a web front-end server. That's a total of nine servers to support these three applications.

## Server consolidation

Most organizations have at least few "single purpose" servers that operate at 10% utilization or for example, we've seen many organizations that have dedicated NTP (network time protocol) servers. NTP is a low-overhead protocol that requires very little effort. Reserving a server for NTP is like flying a Boeing 767 cross-country with only one passenger.

Server consolidation is a close cousin of application consolidation and is equally effective. Instead of bundling multiple functions into one application, you bundle multiple services onto one server.

Unlike Windows, UNIX and Linux excel at preemptive multitasking. A good solution in the NTP case is to run the NTP daemon on the same servers that provide common infrastructural services such as DNS and Kerberos.

Another opportunity for server consolidation is presented by database servers that are dedicated to a single application. If you have competent systems and DBAs (as good monitoring), a single database server should be able to host the databases for many applications. Again, this consolidation reduces license fees, capital costs, and energy consumption.
