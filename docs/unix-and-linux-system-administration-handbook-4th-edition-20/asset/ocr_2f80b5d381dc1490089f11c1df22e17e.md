Because UNIX was popular at universities, and because those environments included lots of students who were eager to learn the latest technology, universities were early leaders in the development of organized system administration. Universities such as Purdue, the University of Utah, the University of Colorado, the University of Maryland, and the State University of New York (SUNY) Buffalo became bothers of system administration.

System administrators also developed an array of their own processes, standards, best practices, and tools (such as sudo). Most of these were built out of necessity; until, unstable systems and unhappy users were the result.

Evi Nemeth became known as the "mother of system administration" by undergraduates to work as system administrators to support the Engineering College at the University of Colorado. Her close ties with Berkeley, the University of Utah, and SUNY Buffalo created a system community that shared tips and how. Her, often called the "munchkins" or "Evi slaves" used USENIX and other conferences and worked as on-site in exchange for the opportunity to absorb information at the conference.

It clear out that system administrators had to be jacks of all trades. A system administrator might start a typical day in the 1980s by using a wirewrap tool to fix an interrupt jumper on a VAX backplane. Mid-morning tasks might include sucking pulsed toner out of a malfunctioning first-generation printer. Lunch hour could be spent helping a grad student debug a new kernel driver, the afternoon might consist of writing backup tapes and harassing users to clean up their home directories to make space in the filesystem. A system administrator was, a-everything, take-no-prisons guardian angel.

The 1980s were a time of unreliable hardware. Rather than living on a single chip, the CPUs of the 1980s were made of several hundred chips, all of them prone to failure. It was the system administrator’s job to isolate failed-hardware and get it replaced, quickly. Unfortunately, these were also the days before it was common to FedEx parts on a whim, so finding the right part from a local source was often a challenge.

In one case, our beloved VAX 11/780 was down, leaving the entire campus without email. We knew there was a business down the street that packaged VAXes to be shipped to the (then cold-war) Soviet Union for "research purposes." Desperate, we showed up at their warehouse with a huge wad of cash in our pocket, and after an hour of negotiation, we escaped with the necessary board. At that time, someone remarked that it felt more comfortable to buy drugs than VAX parts in Boulder.
