The two golden rules of autonegotiation are:

- You must use autonegotiation on all interfaces capable of 1 Gb/s or above. It is required by the standard.
- On interfaces limited to 100 Mb/s or below, you must configure both ends of a link in autonegotiation mode, or you must manually configure the speed and duplex (half vs. full) on both sides. If you configure only one side in autonegotiation mode, it will not (in most cases) “learn” how the other side has been configured. The result will be a configuration mismatch and poor performance.

## Power over Ethernet

Power over Ethernet (PoE) is an extension of UTP Ethernet (standardized as IEEE 802.3af) that transmits power to devices over the same cable that carries the Ethernet signal. It's especially handy for Voice over IP (VoIP) telephones or wireless access points (to name just two examples) that need a relatively small amount of power and a network connection wherever they lie.

The power supply capacity of PoE systems has been stratified into four classes that range from 3.84 to 12.95 watts. Never satisfied, the industry is currently working on a higher power standard (802.3at) that may provide more than 60 watts. It is convenient to operate an Easy-Bake over the network port in the conference room⁵

PoE has two ramifications that are significant for sysadmins:

- Need to be aware of PoE devices in your infrastructure so that you can plan the availability of PoE-capable switch ports. They are more expensive than non-PoE ports.
- The power budget for data closets that hold PoE switches must include the wattage of the PoE devices. Note that you don't have to budget the same amount of extra cooling for the closet because the heat generated by the consumption of PoE power will be dissipated outside the closet (usually, in an office).

## Jumbo frames

Ethernet is standardized for a typical packet size of 1,500 bytes (1,518 with framing), a value chosen long ago when networks were slow and memory for buffers was scarce. Today, 1,500-byte packets look pretty shrimpy in the context of a gigabit Ethernet. Because every packet consumes overhead and introduces latency, network throughput can be higher if larger packet sizes are allowed.

Unfortunately, the IEEE standards for the various types of Ethernet forbid large packets because of interoperability concerns. But just as highway traffic often mysteriously flows faster than the speed limit, illicit kind-size Ethernet packets are a common sight on today's networks. Egged by impatient customers, manufacturers of network equipment have flouted the IEEE and built support for large frames into their gigabit products.

To use these-called jumbo frames, all you need to do is bump up your network interfaces' MTUs. Throughput gains vary with traffic patterns, but large transfers over TCP (e.g., NFS4 or CIFS file service) benefit the most. Expect a modest but measurable improvement on the order of 10%.

Be aware of these points, though:

- All network equipment on a subnet must support and use jumbo frames, including switches and routers. You cannot mix and match.
- Because jumbo frames are nonstandard, you usually have to enable them explicitly. Devices may accept jumbo frames by default, but they probably will not generate them.
- Since jumbo frames are a form of outlawry, there’s no universal consensus on exactly how large a jumbo frame can or should be. The most common value is 9000 bytes, but larger is generally better. We endorse the use of jumbo frames on gigabit Ethnets, but don’t be scared by the extreme-sounding name.

## 16.2 Wireless: Ethernet for Nomads

A wireless network consists of Access Points (WAPs, or simply APs) which are usually dedicated appliances that consist of one or more radios and some form of embedded operating system. The common wireless standards used are 802.11g and 802.11n.

802.11n delivers up to 600 Mbps of bandwidth and can use the 5 GHz frequency band (though 2.4 GHz is recommended for deployment). Typical operating range is approximately double of 802.11g. Today, 802.11g (and its grandfather, 802.11b) are commonplace. The transceivers are inexpensive and built into most laptops. Add-in cards are widely available for desktop PCs.

You can configure a Linux box to act as an access point if you have the right hardware and driver. Since most PC-based wireless cards are still designed for Microsoft Windows, they may not come from the factory with Linux drivers.

An alone 802.11b/g base station for the home or small office is Apple’s AirPort Express, a wall-like device that is expensive ($99) and highly functional.¹ Another option is to consider running a stripped version of Linux (such as OpenWRT) on a commercial router. Openwrt.org for information and a list of compatible hardware.

Literally dozens of vendors are having wireless access points. You can buy them at Home Depot and even at the grocery store. Predictably, the adage that "you pay for it" applies. Cheap access points (in the $50 range) are likely to perform poorly when handling large file transfers or more than one active client.

Debugging a wireless network is something of a black art. You must consider a range of variables when dealing with problems. If you are deploying a wireless network at an enterprise scale, you'll probably need to invest in a wireless network analyzer. We highly recommend the analysis products made by AirMagnet.
