## Pick a reasonable backup interval

The more often backups are, the less data is lost in a crash. However, backups system system resources and an operator’s time. You must provide adequate data integrity at a reasonable cost of time and materials. In general, costs increase as you move toward more granular restoration capabilities.

On busy systems, it is generally appropriate to back up directories every workday. On systems that are used less heavily or on which the data is volatile, you might decide that performing backups several times a week is sufficient. A small system with only one user, performing backups once a week is probably adequate. How much data are your users willing to lose?

## Choose filesystems carefully

Filesystems that are rarely modified do not need to be backed up as frequently as users’ home directories. If only a few files change on an otherwise static filesystem (such as /etc/passwd in the root filesystem), you can copy these files every day to another partition that is backed up regularly.

If /tmp is a separate filesystem, it should not be backed up. The /tmp directory should not contain anything essential, so there is no reason to preserve it. This seems obvious, unless you are in better shape than many sites you’ve visited.

## Make daily dumps on one piece of media

In a perfect world, you could daily dumps of all your important filesystems onto a single tape. High-density media such as DLT, AIT, and LTO make this goal practical for some sites. However, as our work habits change and commuting becomes more popular, the range of “good” times to backups is shrinking. More and more network services must be available around the clock, and large backups take time.

## Protect your backups

Another major problem is the rapid expansion of disk space that has resulted from the ever-lower price of hard disks. You can no longer purchase a stock-top machine with less than 250GB of disk space. Why clean up your disks and enforce quotas when you can just throw a little money to the problem and more disk space? Unfortunately, it's all too easy for the on-line storage to outstrip your ability to back it up.

Backup units are perfectly capable of dumping filesystems to multiple pieces of media. However, if a dumps multiple tapes, an operator or tape library robot must be present to change the media, and the media must be carefully labeled to allow restores to be performed easily. Unless you have a good reason to create a early large systems, doesn't do it.

If you can't fit your daily backups on one tape, you have several options:

- Buy a higher-capacity backup device.
- Buy a stacker or library and feed multiple pieces of media to one device.
- Change your dump sequence.
- Use multiple backup devices.

## Keep media off-site

First, baby steps: you should always have an off-line copy of your data. That is, a protected copy that is not stored on a hard disk on the machine of origin. shots and RAID arrays are not substitutes for backups!

Most organizations also keep backups off-site so that a disaster such as a fire can destroy both the original data and the backups. "Off-site" can be anything from a safe deposit box at a bank to the President's or CEO's home. In this context, snapshots are primarily a tool for creating "real" backups of a filesystem. They are not a replacement for off-line backups. Snapshots also help facilitate backup backups, since the database only needs to be paused for a second while the snapshot completes. Later, the relatively slow tape backup can be performed against the snapshot as the live database goes happily on its way serving up queries.

## Limit activity during backups

In practice, it is next to impossible to find a disk that doesn't always have at least a little. Users want 24/7 access to data, services around the clock, and databases require special backup procedures. Most databases will be temporarily stopped or in a partially degraded mode so that backups accurately capture data at a single point in time. Sites with lot of data may not be able to tolerate the downtime to perform traditional backup of their database. These days the only way to do a backup with no disk activity is to create a snapshot.

Most SAN controllers, and all our operating systems, provide some way to create a snapshot of a filesystem. This feature lets you relatively safe backups of an active filesystem, even on which files are currently open. On Linux, snapshots are implemented through the volume manager (see page 249), and on our other systems they are created through the filesystem.

Snapshots can be created almost instantaneously thanks to a clever copy-on-write scheme. No data is actually copied or moved at the time the snapshot is created. Once the snapshot exists, changes to the filesystem are written to new locations on disk. In this, two (or more) images can be maintained with minimal use of additional storage. Snapshots are similar in concept to incremental backups, except that they operate at the block level rather than the filesystem level.
