Because administrative operations are mediated through software, AIX does not need to store information in text files. Instead, it's squirrelled in a variety of binary formats and logs, most notably the Object Data Manager. Sysadmins can use generic ODM commands to inspect and modify this data, but that's-something of a black art, and it's generally discouraged. Overall, AIX is a dark and a mysterious continent with many backwaters, much like the Windows registry.

If one persists in cutting away at AIX's carapace, one eventually discovers a sad UNIX homunculus lying contorted therein. But it's not a healthy creature; wrinkled with age, its skin pale from lack of exposure to the outside world and to the last few decades of UNIX advancements. Clearly, IBM considers the real action to be somewhere other than the UNIX mainframe.

# Dan Foster'S CASE FOR THE DEFENSE

Ouch! You don't paint a charitable picture. However, it's fair to characterize many of your objections as 'not invented here' syndrome; in other words, as resistance to anything that doesn't toe the standard UNIX line.

There's some validity to your general point that AIX aspires to be more than just UNIX clone. That's not necessarily bad. AIX is not for cows. It's designed to facilitate reliability, consistency, and ease of administration. It has differs from other UNIX systems, so it looks and feels a bit different, too.

AIX draws a variety of useful tools from IBM's mainframe and AS/400 systems. For example, it uses a centralized logging facility that applications can hook into through an API. That system facilitates error reporting, administrator notification, and problem diagnosis. Syslog tries to implement some of these features for UNIX generally, but as this book's many sections on logging show, it's not consistently done.(Years later, Sun adopted a similar approach with the fault management daemon, fmd, in Solaris 10.)

Another case in point is hardware management. AIX uses generic tools for just about any supported device. It easily repairs actions (which, in some cases, can disable fault LEDs and issue-resolution resolutions), providing an audit trail. The system is easy to extend through callback hooks, but it doesn't leave you on your own as most versions of UNIX.

To address one of your specific complaints, task-specific commands are a feature, not a bug! They benefit administrators in several ways.
