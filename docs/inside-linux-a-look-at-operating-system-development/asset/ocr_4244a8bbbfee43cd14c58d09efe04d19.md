# ## Why you need Linux

Until recently, Linux has been regarded as a hobbyist's system. It started out as a project by a student, Linus Torvalds, to see how the memory management hardware of the Intel 386 really worked. That is, Linus wanted to identify what detailed steps were required to build a practical virtual memory system with that hardware. Since he shared his code on the Internet, there was opportunity for others to make their own contributions to the system. This development process has now been in operation for over five years. Appendix D provides a timeline of the development of Linux.

Although Linux clearly started out as a student project, it is now regarded as an appropriate foundation for many commercial products. Linux has become the operating system of choice for hundreds of thousands of users worldwide¹.

Over the past decade we have seen the establishment of three categories of computer systems: the large scale systems supporting large commercial, engineering or scientific operations; the workstation supporting a single engineer or business worker; and the personal computer used at home. The large scale systems run expensive operating systems requiring a support staff for system management. The workstations run some flavor of Unix and are administered by a small staff or an experienced user. One might suggest the personal computer is run without management—at the whim and abilities of the individual.
