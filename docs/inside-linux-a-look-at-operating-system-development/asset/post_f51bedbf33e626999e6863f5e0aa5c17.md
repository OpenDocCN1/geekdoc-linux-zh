## Chapter 0

### Preface

This book provides an informal introduction to a number of operating system issues by looking at the history of operating systems, by looking at how they are used, and by looking at the details of one operating system. The contents are a conscious effort to braid discussion of history, theory and practice so that the reader can see what goes on inside the system.

Because of recurring references to the Linux system, this book doesn’t presume to cover “operating systems” in any formal way. Other books do that quite well[46, 34]. Nor is this book intended to be just a series of tutorials on how to use some features of Linux or Unix. There is a growing list of such books on the market as well[53, 47]. In spite of these limits, this book will give the computer professional a deeper understanding of how Unix systems in general (and Linux in particular) operate. Because the Linux kernel code source files are readily available, the reader is encouraged to explore the details by examining or modifying the code.

Traditionally the system kernel and run-time library have been shown as layers of software which surround the hardware and insulate the hardware from the user programs (Figure 0.1). This book focuses on the shaded areas in Figure 0.1, encompassing the system kernel, some parts of the run-time library, some parts of the hardware, some of the software development tools, and some user interfaces.

### 0.1 Motivation

I'm a hacker who knows better.

My first programming experience was in high school, when my algebra teacher wrangled some machine time from some of his former co-workers so that we could try our hand at FORTRAN II ($\dagger$) programming. In college I studied physics and mathematics—computer science degrees were only being offered at a few universities at the time.

In spite of my lack of formal training—the college offered no classes in programming as such—I got a job at the college's computer center and actually got paid for writing a program for one of my professors.

That led to a ten year career in programming during which I learned a bit of computer science by watching and doing. It was only in the late 1970s that I held a position where we discussed what we were doing and how others might do it. During these discussions I realized how little I knew about my chosen field.

About that time, I moved to a city that possessed a research university with a good policy for continuing education. One class led to another and I easily fell into a program of study that eventually led to graduate degrees in computer science.

Unlike many of my fellow students, I had significant practical experience in compiler modification, networking, task schedulers for embedded applications, and system management. Because of this background, the material I was studying fell into four categories:

- I know that—$I\ use\ it\ every\ day$
- I knew that—but I had forgotten
- I didn’t know that—this is new
- You can do that?—this is amazing

At the same time I was matriculated, I was hired by the department to install and manage their Unix systems. The Berkeley Software Distribution (BSD) contained all the sources, so when something broke or when something wasn’t clearly documented, I could dive in and find out what was really going on. I was able to examine the implementation of the theories being discussed in my courses. Later, I taught classes in programming languages, operating systems and software engineering. This course of work, study and preparation showed me the errors of my earlier ways—my bad habits and limited understanding were exposed. Although I now know how much more I don’t know, I also know more than I did when I started and I’d like to share this knowledge. There is a meme\(^1\) out in the world that “examination destroys appreciation.” I think this idea is wrong—one cannot appreciate craftsmanship without examination.

I like to tell the story of my wife’s clearest demonstration of computer literacy. At the time she was a reluctant user of computers—except for games, she used them only for word processing. With the aid of \textbf{vi} and \textbf{nroff} she struggled through over twenty revisions of her thesis. I often watched over her shoulder and suggested new formatting commands or new methods of moving text around.

She had gained enough sophistication to be horrified when she discovered the department secretary had been physically cutting and pasting output from a word processing system. The reason: “I’ve deleted and re-entered this paragraph several times, but it always comes out doubled.” She advised the secretary (correctly, it turned out) there had to be something embedded in the file that said “duplicate the following.”

The key was that even though she had no experience with that specific system, she had learned what word processing systems could do in general and she could identify likely behavior.

> ^1 This term was coined by Richard Dawkins to name a unit of replication in the ideosphere, e.g., an idea, catch-phrase, tune, or technique. Memes propagate by being passed from one person to another.

In a similar vein, I hope that this book will show you what is possible on a personal computer. Then, as an enlightened consumer, you'll demand Linux for your system and you might even be tempted to make your own contribution. All this will help popularize Linux, my ultimate goal.

### 0.2 Style and Structure

The careful reader will notice some unusual things in the book. Some of them can be explained.

#### 0.2.1 Quotation Marks

I acknowledge that the American typesetting convention of including punctuation within quotation marks is generally more attractive than placing punctuation outside quotation marks. Unfortunately, this convention is ambiguous with respect to what exactly is being quoted. Such a distinction is especially important when dealing with computer commands being quoted. Therefore, I use quotation marks to delimit exactly what is being quoted.

#### 0.2.2 Vocabulary

As you'll discover, "state" refers to two things: $state_{1}$ refers to what a process is doing, e.g., running, waiting, ready-to-run, and $state_{2}$ refers to everything about a process, including the contents of all its memory and registers, as well as what it is doing ($state_{1}$). It's unfortunate that these two meanings are so closely related. The reader will have to be careful to distinguish them as there are no adequate substitutes which could be used to avoid confusion.

The terms "process" and "task" are entirely too similar in meaning. If I were obliged to distinguish them, I would define "process" as a program in execution and "task" as something lighter than a process. ("Lighter" means having a smaller state.) I've taken the easy course and almost entirely avoided using "task" in my writing. I use "job" to refer to a set of procedures submitted to a batch stream.
