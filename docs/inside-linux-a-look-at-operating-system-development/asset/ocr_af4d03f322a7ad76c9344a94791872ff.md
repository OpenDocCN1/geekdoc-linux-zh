Independent If the new thread is not coupled to the parent, it normally can only access global data, traditionally held in heap storage. The new thread will not normally access (and cannot easily access) data held on other threads' stacks.

Co-ordination and rendezvous The parent thread and all its children are said to be independent, but they are typically working in co-operation towards some goal. This means that they need some mechanisms for co-ordination. The two mechanisms in common use are signals and semaphores.

In the Unix implementations, signals are one-shot events (software interrupts) which do not accumulate and which cause a waiting process to proceed. They convey a modest amount of information— the signal number. Signals can be lost should another signal arrive before the first is handled by the receiving process, i.e., signals aren't counted. Some Unix implementations of signals have subtle race conditions wherein a signal handler can drop a signal or fail to prepare to catch the next signal. The POSIX standards have addressed this latter shortcoming, but there is still the basic problem that signals aren't counted. Semaphores have a much more formal use, described in Section 5.2.4. They can be used among threads as well as among processes to coordinate execution.
