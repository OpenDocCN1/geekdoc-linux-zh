Phil Zimmerman developed PGP as a Public Key Infrastructure (PKI), featuring a convenient interface, ease of use and management, and the security of digital certificates. One characteristic set PGP apart from the majority of cryptystems available: PGP functions without certification authorities (CAs). Until the introduction of PGP, PKI implementations built around the concept of CAs and centralized key management controls.

Both PGP and GnuPG rely on the notion of a web of trust:if you trust someone else, the person you trust can provide introduction to the third party. When you trust someone, you perform an operation called key signing. By signing someone's key, you verify that the person's public key is authentic and for you to send email. When you sign a key, you are asked whether this person to introduce other keys to you. It is common practice to assign this trust based on several criteria, including your knowledge of the person's character or a lasting professional relationship with them. Best practice is to sign someone's key only after you have met face to avert any chance of a man-in-the-middle scenario. The disadvantage of this scheme is the lack of a central registry for associating with people you do not know.

PGP is available without cost for personal use but its deployment in a commercial environment requiring the purchase of a license. This was not always the case: Soon after its introduction, PGP was available on many bulletin board systems, and users could implement it in any manner they chose. PGP rapidly gained popularity in the networking community, capitalized on its encryption and key management capabilities for secure transmission of email.

After a time, attention turned to RSA and IDEA, the two robust cryptographic algorithms that form an integral part of PGP's code. These algorithms are privately owned. The wide distribution and growing user base for PGP sparked battles over patent violation and licenses, resulting in the eventual restriction of PGP's use.

Enter GnuPG, which supports most of the features and implementations available by PGP and complies with the OpenPGP Message standard. Because GnuPG does not use the IDEA algorithm but rather relies on BUGS (Big and Useful Great Security; www.gnu.org/directory/bugs.html), you can use it without restriction; it is released under the GNU GPL (refer to “The Code Free” on page 5).
