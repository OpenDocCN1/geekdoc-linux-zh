# THE HERITAGE OF LINUX: UNIX

The UNIX system was developed by researchers who needed a set of modern tools to help them with their projects. The system allowed a group of people working together on a project to share selected data and programs while keeping other information private.

Universities and colleges played a major role in furthering the popularity of the UNIX operating system through the "four-year effect." When the UNIX operating system became widely available in 1975, Bell Labs offered it to educational institutions at nominal cost. The schools, in turn, used it in their computer science programs, ensuring that computer students became familiar with it; Because UNIX was an advanced development system, the students became acclimated to a sophisticated programming environment. As these students graduated and entered industry, they expected to work in a similarly advanced environment. As more of them worked their way up the ladder in the commercial world, the UNIX operating system found its way into industry.

In addition to introducing students to the UNIX operating system, the Computer Systems Research Group (CSRG) at the University of California at Berkeley made significant additions and changes to it. In fact, it made so many popular changes that one version of the system is called the Berkeley Software Distribution (BSD) of the UNIX system (or just Berkeley UNIX). The other major version is UNIX System V (SVR4), which descended from versions developed and maintained by AT&T and System Laboratories.

# FADE TO 1983

Richard Stallman (www.stallman.org) announced¹ the GNU Project for creating an operating system, both kernel and system programs, and presented the GNU Manifesto,² which begins as follows:

GNU, which stands for GNU's Not Unix, is the name for the complete UNIX-compatible software system I am writing so that I can give it free to everyone who can use.

Some years later, Stallman added a footnote to the preceding sentence when he realized it was creating confusion:

The wording here was careless. The intention was that nobody would have to pay for *permission* to use the GNU system. The words do not make this clear, and people often interpret them as saying that GNU should be distributed at little or no charge. That was never the intent; later, the manifesto mentions the possibility of companies providing the service of distribution for a profit. Subsequently I have learned to distinguish carefully between “free” in the sense of freedom and “free” in the sense of price. Free software is software that users the freedom to distribute and change. Some users may obtain copies at no charge, while others pay to obtain copies—and if the funds help supporting the software, so make it better. The important thing is that everyone who has a copy has the freedom to cooperate with others in using.

In the manifesto, after explaining a little about the project and what was accomplished so far, Stallman continues:

# Why I Must Write GNU

I consider that the golden rule requires that if I like a program I must share it with other people like it. Software sellers want to divide the users and conquer them, making each user agree not to share with others. I refuse to break solidarity with other users in this way. I cannot in good conscience sign a nondisclosure agreement or a software license agreement. For years I worked within the Artificial Intelligence Lab to resist such tendencies and other inhomogeneities, but eventually they had gone too far: I could not remain in an institution where such things are done for my will.

So that I can continue to use computers without dishonor, I have decided to put together a sufficient body of free software so that I will be able to get along without any software that is not free. I have resigned from the AI Lab to deny MIT any legal excuse to prevent me from giving GNU away.

# THE CODE IS FREE

The tradition of free software dates back to the days when UNIX was commercialized, making it unavailable. The manufacturers turned it proprietary. The GPL and GNU software are distributed under the terms of the GNU Public License (GPL, www.gnu.org/licenses.html). The GPL says you have the right to copy, modify, and redistribute the code under the agreement. When you redistribute the code, however, you must distribute the same license with it, making the code and the license inseparable. If you get source code for the Internet under an accounting program that is under the GPL and then modify the modified code and redistribute an executable version of the program, you must also distribute the modified code and the GPL agreement with it. Because this arrangement is the reverse of a normal copyright copy (it gives rights instead of limiting them), it has been termed copyleft. (This paragraph is not a legal interpretation of the GPL; it is intended to merely give you an idea of how it works. Refer to the GPL itself when you want to make use of it.)
