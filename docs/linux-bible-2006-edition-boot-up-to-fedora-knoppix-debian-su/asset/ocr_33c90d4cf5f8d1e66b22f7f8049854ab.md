Microsoft's attempts to U.S. courts to protect the Windows trademark were not initially successful, but in countries including Finland, Sweden, Belgium, Netherlands and Luxembourg, Microsoft was able to get injunctions against Linows. Because of those rulings, and related threats of massive fines being imposed, Linows stated that it could not remedy the situation without changing its name.

The rulings supported Microsoft's contentions that Lindows infringed on the Microsoft trademark, and that Lindows could not allow citizens of those countries to access the Lindows Web site or access products with the Lindows name. Because keeping any country's residents off of a particular Web site would have proved very difficult, Lindows had to make some drastic changes, most notable the alteration of its name.

Microsoft was still pursuing an injunction or summary judgment and would soon see its Windows challenged in court. To avoid a costly battle, Microsoft offered a $280 million and other items including some technology licensing to settle the case. Linde accepted, and that was the end of the dispute.
