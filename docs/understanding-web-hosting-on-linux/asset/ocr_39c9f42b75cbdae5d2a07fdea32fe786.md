# Linux Origins

Linux is considered a new operating system. Because it is UNIX-based, however, much of the technology behind it is over 30 years old. In 1969, Ken Thompson and Ritchie, software engineers at AT&T Bell Labs who had previously worked on the Multics (Multiplexed Information and Computing Service) operating system, began developing a portable operating system. They called this operating system "Unics" as on Multics (The later mutated to an x.)

To make their operating system portable, Thompson and Ritchie had to develop a language that was not bound to a specific platform, and the C language was born. The C source for every UNIX operating system could be copied and then recompiled on various platforms. Because AT&T Bell Labs made the C source code for UNIX freely, several UNIX variants emerged: BSD (Berkeley Systems Division) UNIX, Sun Micros' SunOS, and AT&T's System V. After the federal breakup of AT&T, Bell Labs UNIX was generally considered to be a commercial asset of AT&T. As a result, companies that wanted to use UNIX to formally license it.

The vendors who to license UNIX rights from AT&T ended up tweaking their own flavors of UNIX so that they could lock their customers into their variant. Despite these "UNIX wars," as they were called, UNIX was still successful, and most technical colleges had UNIX in their curriculum.

To circumvent UNIX licensing, Andrew Tanenbaum, the author of highly successful books on operating systems and compilers, created his own flavor of UNIX, MINIX, for the sole purpose of teaching UNIX to college students. Much later, the University of California at Berkeley also created a UNIX derivation called FreeBSD, that, like MINIX, was enabled to be freely modifiable and distributable. Today, however, the most known and famous version of UNIX is Linux, created by Linus Torvalds.

In the early 90s, Torvalds, a graduate student at the University of Helsinki, became tired of the limitations of MINIX. He began to develop Linux as his version of UNIX for the Intel platform. Through Internet newsgroups and forums, Torvalds invited other computer scientists to assist with his project. On October 5, 1991, the initial version of Linux, 0.02, was released. Version 1.0, the first business-quality version, was made available in March 1994.

Linux has received so much positive press than any other UNIX variation, including IBM's, AT&T's, and Sun Micros'. Interestingly, FreeBSD (www.freebsd.org), is comparable to Linux in features and quality, has nowhere near the acceptance rate, mostly because of bad timing. While Linux was the darling of the press, the BSD was in litigation with AT&T to determine whether BSD contained property Bell Labs material. By the time BSD was from the litigation, Linux had gained the industry momentum.
