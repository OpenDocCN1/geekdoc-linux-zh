Once the mail arrives at its final destination, you use your email client to retrieve it. If you haven't already viewed the message you sent to yourself earlier, find it in the /var/spool/mail directory of your system, in a file that has your user name. One method to view the message is to simply issue the command `cat /var/spool/mail/username` (substitute the appropriate username).

"But wait! you proclaim. "What is all other stuff at the top of the message?"

That "stuff" is heading information added by sendmail, for RFC 1869, which describes SMTP. Each SMTP server, from the SMTP server you connected to the last SMTP server to handle the message, adds its own heading information, but you can always see the route your message took. Most email clients hide that information from you, but you can configure them to show headers, if you wish.

Using the cat command to view your message isn't very practical, since each incoming message is appended to this file, so you'd be seeing the same messages over and over. Instead, you can access your messages using your email client. On your local machine, you can configure your client to manipulate the mail spool file directly. If your mail was located on another machine, you would not have access to the mail spool file. Instead, your email client would connect to a POP server.

Like SMTP servers, POP servers are software that listen on a TCP/IP port (in this case, 110) and allow connections from client software. Unlike SMTP, which requires no user or password information, POP will connect to your client immediately and ask it for identity (with USER and PASS commands) before proceeding. Once your identity has been authenticated, the POP server will respond to commands from your client. These include instructions to the POP server to send a of your waiting messages, send one of the waiting messages, or delete a message, unless you've configured your email client to keep the messages on the server.

You can experiment with POP servers by issuing the following command:

```
telnet yourPopServer 110
```

Once connected, issue USER username, then issue password for your account. The HELP command will give you a list of commands that the POP server understands.

Once you've received your messages, they exist nowhere but in your local mailbox. This isn't much of a problem, unless you use multiple computers. Then, it seems that the email you want to review is always "where you aren't." Sure, with a little creativity, you can configure all of your computers to leave messages on the server. The better solution, is to use IMAP. This is typically no more complicated than using your email client to use IMAP (assuming your ISP provides one) instead of a POP server. The difference, though, is that the mail is left at the server. This is much more convenient for a user who has many computers, since the mail is in one place. Most ISPs do not offer IMAP simply because of the space requirements on their end. They're more than happy when you connect and delete your messages from their disk drives. Fail to do that too long, and people who send you messages will start getting them bounced back.

No discussion of Internet email would be complete without a MIME, the Multipurpose Internet Mail Extension. You have probably attached some kind of file to an email message before. Unless the attachment was text, it was using MIME.
