Bill Gates' mother figured to find out IBM's research and suggested that an IBM executive acquaintance of hers gave her son a call. At that time, Gates' expertise was in the development of BASIC compilers, when IBM asked if his company (which only amounted to a couple of people at the time) could create an operating system for the PC, he said Gates bought the rights to an existing operating system for $40,000, tethered it to IBM's specifications, and called it Disk Operating System (DOS). Shrewdly, Gates asked IBM for royalties on DOS rather than a lump-sum payment. Millions of PCs were so with DOS bundled, and Microsoft started its rise to dominance.

IBM realized that PCs required a more powerful operating system than DOS, so it invited Microsoft to partner with it in the development of a multitasking operating system. Microsoft agreed, and the two companies began the collaborative development on what would become OS/2. While Microsoft was developing OS/2 with IBM, it was also developing its own new multitasking operating system. Then, after OS/2 was introduced, Microsoft came out with Windows.

OS/2 was superior to Windows because OS/2 was a full multitasking operating system, while Windows was on top of DOS and lacked multitasking capabilities. Nevertheless, because Windows was backward-compatible with DOS applications, and partly because of Bill Gates' marketing genius, Windows became the dominant PC operating system, while IBM's technically superior OS/2 flourished.

Later, Windows 95 and 98 provided improved GUI capabilities, but the operating system still crashed regularly, and users began rebooting their machines daily as an acceptable part of owning a PC. Even in the 1980s, operating system crashes, even on personal workstations or corporate servers, should not have been acceptable because sophisticated multitasking operating systems had been available since the 197s.

It wasn't until the introduction of NT that Microsoft provided a full multitasking operating system. (Meanwhile, OS/2 flourished to the point of being discontinued.) With Windows NT, Microsoft finally produced an operating system that could be compared to Linux. Still, NT was known to crash regularly, and many system administrators found that for reliability, they needed to have one server per application: one for Web, one for file serving, and one as a Domain Name Service. In 2000, Microsoft came out with Windows 2000, and the reliability of Windows 2000 as a server has been far superior to its Microsoft predecessors.

# Linux Growth

When Linux version 1.0 was introduced in 1994, the first people to use it were UNIX programmers who wanted UNIX-based systems at home. They found that not only was Linux a solid operating system for their Intel-based PCs, but it also had many of the same capabilities as the UNIX operating systems running on the costly-mid-large systems at their workplaces.

As mentioned earlier, when the Internet exploded, network administrators were given the task of getting their companies on the Web. That task required additional hardware and software.
