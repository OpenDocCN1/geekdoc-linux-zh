SCSI has any downside, it would have to be. Along with the higher performance and reliability comes a higher price. SCSI devices are manufactured with higher-quality parts and within finer tolerances than other device, which increases their production costs. Since they are sold in fewer numbers than their consumer-grade equivalents, there aren't many items over which the costs can be distributed, resulting in a higher-per-unit cost.

The unit of cost leads to the second disk interface: IDE (Integrated Drive Electronics). In addition to SCSI, early interfaces MFM and RLL, both of which were limited in their performance capabilities. A cheaper, yet better-performing, alternative was created that moved the "smarts" from the controller card to the hard drive itself—hence the integrated upgrade. IDE was a major improvement over its MFM/RLL predecessors, offering better performance at a lower cost. Although it wasn't a contender in performance compared to SCSI, it was a step up for the burgeoning PC industry. A later version, IDE (Enhanced Integrated Drive Electronics) added both the speed and the ability of the drive to report its identity (how many heads/sectors/cylinders it has) to the computer's BIOS, making hardware installation much easier for the user to tackle.

Why should this be of any interest to you? Because IDE is one of those gizmos we alluded to. The high CPU bus speeds available in modern PCs have highlighted the dismal performance exhibited by the disk subsystem. A CPU at 1GHz has exactly the same performance as one running at 33MHz if both are awaiting arrival of data from the disk subsystem. To combat this phenomenon, and to give you another way to compare the performance of your system with that of a neighbor's (with spawning a hardware upgrade), manufacturers have come up with some enhancements, with specifications known as UMA-33, UMA-66, and UMA-100. These drives carry data at 33 Mb/second, 66 Mb/second, and 100 Mb/second, so the I/O bottleneck isn't as much of a problem as it was once. Unfortunately, these are new kids on the block, and for controllers they aren't supported by many out-of-the-box Linux distributions. Fortunately, Red Hat is fairly up-to-date and can handle many of the commonly available interfaces. To be safe, you'll need to check.

Although we have discussed the CPU and drive interface so far, you should take the time to make a checklist of all your computer's hardware, and then check the hardware compatibility to see if all of it is supported. Red Hat users check http://hardware.redhat.com to find a list of compatible products. Two other resources are the "Linux Hardware Compatibility HOWTO" (www.linuxdoc.org/) and the Hardware Database (http://fld.linuxdoc.org).

No matter which Linux distribution you choose, make sure that the hardware you choose is supported. Although many of the major PC vendors now offer computers with Linux preloaded, guaranteeing hardware compatibility, you will probably be loading Linux onto whatever you've decided to recycle. Ensuring that your distribution either supports or can be made to support your hardware will make the installation process less irritating. It is time well spent.
