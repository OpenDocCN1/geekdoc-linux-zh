The Hermes chipset, manufactured by Agere (www.agere.com) is found in Lucent/Orinoco/Agere and other cards, its most popular using NetStumble and AeroPeek.

Although there a couple scanners for Windows platforms based on the Prism 2 chipset, most users for this OS are going to be armed with a Hermes chipset card. Newer versions of NetStumble use the Microsoft NDIS 5.1 802.11 application programming interface (API) functions to enable functionality on Cisco and other Prism cards. Be warned that the 802.11 original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) have selected to alter functionality in their chipsets between firmware revs, especially regarding monitor mode, so you may have to upgrade or downgrade your card's software version depending on the tool being used. If you start to have problems with a particular tool, be sure to check the documentation for any reference to firmware levels.

# Selecting an 802.11 Antenna

If you are concerned only with lab testing or internally walking your facility, you could probably get away with the built-in antenna in your wireless NIC. The power output of an average card is about 35–100mw, and assuming you aren't testing the external exposure of the network, it’s more satisfactory. Some, not all, 802.11 client NICs come with an external antenna jack to allow the connection of an external antenna. Cards from Lucent/Orinoco or those directly based on their form factor have a small jack that accepts a proprietary connector.

Most wireless equipment vendors sell pigtails, adapters that convert a card’s small proprietary connector into a conventional RF insight fitting (usually an N-Male). Certain Cisco cards also come without an internal antenna and instead provide sets of these adapters using the Cisco proprietary connector.
