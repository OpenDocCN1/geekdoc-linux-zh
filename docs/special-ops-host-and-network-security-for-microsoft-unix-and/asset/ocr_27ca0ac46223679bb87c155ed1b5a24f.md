# Personnel as a Security Risk

Think of an incident that you've responded to in the past. Trace back the sequence of events that triggered your involvement, and you will undoubtedly be able to cite at least one critical juncture where human intervention contributed directly to the event, be through ignorance, apathy, coercion, or malicious intent. Quite often these মিসেস are entirely forgible, regardless of the havoc they wreak. The best example of user-initiated events comes from the immensely successful mail-borne viruses of the recent past, including Melissa, LoveLetter, and Mournikova. These viruses, and their many imitators (LoveLetter and Kornnikova were in and of themselves imitations of the original Melissa virus) made their way into record books by compromising the user, the most trusted element of corporate infrastructure.

Personnel are the autonomous processing engines of an organization. Whether they are responsible for processing paperwork, managing projects, finessing public relations, establishing corporate direction, or providing final product delivery, they all work as part of a massive system known collectively as the company. The practices and philosophies guiding this intricate system of cogs, cindles, spindles, drivers, and output have evolved over decades. Computers and networked systems were introduced to this system over the past thirty years, and systematic information security procedures have only begun in earnest over the past twenty years. Your job as a security administrator is to design and implement checkpoints, controls, and defenses that can be applied to the organizational machine without disrupting the processes in place.

You have probably heard of the principle of least privilege, an adage that states for any task, the operator should have only the permissions necessary to complete the task. In the case of macro viruses, usability enhancements in the groundwork application suite were hijacked to hook the code spread, and in many instances a lack of permissions on large-scale distribution lists led to disastrous consequences. Small enhancements for usability were not counterbalanced with security measures, creating a pathway for hostile code.

Individuals can impact the organizational posture in a variety of ways, both passive and active. Worms, Trojans, and viruses tend to exploit the user passively, and do so on a grand scale, which draws more attention to the issue.
