## Evaluate Your Needs and Current Status

One of the most difficult tasks to undertake as security professionals is to perform accurate and honest evaluations of the systems and users. Much the purchaser of a new automobile, it is very tempting to operate our systems with all of the bells and whistles at full tilt. However, it is the rare system that truly needs to operate with all of the functionalities of the operating system and hardware engaged and available. In fact, much of the time extra services are just idling long; and because they don't cause problems, they don't get much attention from us, making them easy targets for an attacker. Make the effort to do your evaluations honestly and completely. You will want to utilize one of the tools available for performing this evaluation, such as GFI's Network Scanner, which I mentioned earlier in the chapter “Attacking Windows 2000 system”. Other tools that you might consider using for this purpose are available from sources such as eEye Digital Security (Retina), and Internet Security Systems (Security Scanner). All of these automate the process of evaluation by looking for and identifying problems that may be present. They can be extremely helpful as you evaluate your systems for vulnerability. The next few sections are important in the provision of security in your network and equipment. An appropriate part of the evaluation is to conduct the scanning of the equipment evaluated to determine open ports and services that are running on them, to assist in the effort of securing them more fully.

## Evaluating Access to Protocols

Discussion of protocols is always a problematic area, because most people tend to think of network protocols rather than service protocols when looking at controlling or securing systems. I believe it is a more important need to think along lines of blocking access to particular protocols that allow penetration testing the local and remote systems. It's pretty well understood, for instance, there is a need to block ICMP traffic at the border of the network to block casual enumber of the network. However, it is also important to remember to block or filter for other things. What are the areas to consider blocking in Windows 2000? Let's look at the things that might be a good choice block or not allow:

- SNMP This should be disabled if not being used.
- PPTP and L2TP Unneeded unless using VPN tunneling.
- Unused network protocols Such as IPX/SPX, NetBeui, NetBIOS, AppleTalk
- LDAP At the border, not internally; it's needed for Active Directory location services in an Active Directory domain.
- Microsoft's File and Print Sharing On any machine not hosting shared resources.

## Applying Patches and Service Packs

Most of us are aware that the task of keeping track of patches and service packs is daunting, to the least. In the current climate, it seems that there is a stream of new service packs and patches a number of times per week, and sometimes a number of times given day. What are administrators and security professionals supposed to do to keep up? I suggest that you should employ one of the tools available to scan your systems on a regular basis and identify needed patches and fixes to keep up to date.

Windows 2000 updates, service packs, and hotfixes can be obtained from the Microsoft updates.

## Securing Access Control Methods

Working with access control methods, many environments will experience large swings in the complexity of configuration needs depending on the needs of the system that is to be secured.

In the case of the Windows 2000 workgroup configurations, the administrator must individually configure each of the machines to reflect the administrative and user needs of the company. This might include making decisions to not allow the users of the machine to have administrative privileges on their local machines, and instead delegating a person or two in the workplace to have knowledge of administrator name and passwords for local machines to perform administrative.
