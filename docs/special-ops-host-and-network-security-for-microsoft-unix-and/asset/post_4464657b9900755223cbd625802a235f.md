However, a more in-depth understanding of the policies occurs when we interview specific people about the policies currently in place. This helps us to understand how well current policies are followed.

## Assessing Risks

Performing a risk assessment can be thought of as part of the evaluation phase for policy development, because its results are used to revise the policies so they address identified risks.

A risk assessment is a common tool used when creating policies. However, an inexperienced individual creating policy may skip this to save time. The same inexperienced individual will probably use templates that are inappropriate for his organization’s risks. A security policy professional will perform a risk assessment using some of the methodologies I discuss later in this section. The inexperienced individual can use these tools and techniques to create a fair and useful risk scenario. The necessity of a risk assessment can be brought to light simply if you have completed your security policy, and someone asks the question “why do you define that control?” or “Why did you specify this control over this control?” They come down to the simple idea of “how do you know which assets require more protection?” All these questions can be answered in a risk assessment.

The field of risk assessment is large enough to fill a book, as are most things in policy. This subsection only scratches the surface of risk assessments. There are multiple tools available to perform risk assessments and usually accepted in the industry, as follows:

**Risk = Vulnerabilities × Threat × Exposure**

Many of you are probably familiar with this formula, but I will recap. Vulnerabilities allow your organization to be injured. These may be due to weaknesses in your hardware or software, but also may be the lack of necessary diligence in staying current on government regulations. These are the holes in your system that can be exploited, either by an attacker or a surprise change in government regulations. The threat is the probability that a vulnerability will be exploited. A threat can come from a malicious user, an insider user, or an autonomous worm, or legislation that hurts your company. Your exposure is how much money you will be hurt if a threat attacks a vulnerability. How much or

respect will your company lose, or what damages will be done to your assets? The Exposure factor can also be represented as Asset Value; however, Exposure captures the actual loss, whereas Asset Value represents an all-or-nothing view of asset loss.

Let's use example: Say a new buffer overflow is discovered for the Apache Web server. Your vulnerability is the newly discovered buffer overflow. The threat is the ease with which an attacker can exploit the new vulnerability, such as if an exploit is active in the wild. Finally, the exposure is how much damage you will suffer if you were exploited. This is a server with client information, or a corporate honeypot set by network administrators to lure hackers away from the real critical systems with juicy names and listening ports? A properly configured honeypot will have no exposure if properly configured, because attackers could own it without giving access to any corporate assets.

You have probably seen other forms of risk assessment formulas, such as Annualized Loss Expectency (ALE) or Estimated Annual Cost (EAC). I believe the risk formula earlier is the best to use for all-around risk assessment, though individuals may need many.

At this point in your policy, you should have a scope defined for each policy you hope to implement, and you should have created a rough outline. You should have acquired management support and defined areas of responsibility. Management support is critical when performing a risk assessment, because you will be reviewing their most sensitive information. They may not feel comfortable telling you where their soft underbelly is located, which of their points are the most vulnerable. There could be any number of reasons why they may not be forthcoming with information, but in many ways you are like a doctor and they are the patient. You need to be honest and tell truthfully what problems and what vulnerabilities they are having. It doesn't hurt to have a manager on your side to emphasize the need for forthcoming information. This is why it is important to have identified areas of responsibility, so you know who you need to talk to each organization to get the answers you need. A little beforehand will help you from being bounced around staff members. It's much more powerful to come in with a manager and an organizational chart or job description, and know exactly who you need to talk to.

## Performing a Security Risk Assessment

Risk assessments are necessary to understand the assets in your system, and help you develop your security posture. An appropriate risk assessment will bring to light information you did not have about your network assets. It will

![](img/729efc56f204a2ebffb8295c3a2dfdfb_996_0.png)

show where you store your critical information, and if that information is easily susceptible to attack.

A risk assessment considers this scenario with a multitude of different variables, and defines a risk number to each item. This can be quite a daunting task to perform on your own, but there are tools developed to help you perform this. Here is a list of four products that can help you conduct your risk assessment (note that there are a multitude of products):

- Operationally Critical Threat, Analysis, and Vulnerability Evaluations (OCTAVE) A process document that provides an extended risk assessment format (www.cert.org/octave).
- GAO Information Risk Assessment Case studies of organizations that implemented risk assessment programs (www.gao.gov/special_pubs/ai9139.pdf).
- RiskWatch Software created that asks a series of questions to help individuals perform a risk assessment. Also includes modules for review against the ISO1799 standard (www.riskwatch.com).
- Consultative, Objective and Bi-functional Risk Analysis (COBRA) Another risk assessment software program. Also includes questions that map against the ISO1799 (www.security-risk-analysis.com/index.htm).

> Simply running a vulnerability scan does not perform a risk analysis. However, most risk assessments include requirements for a vulnerability scan. Vulnerability scanners are covered in Chapter 3.

Let's take a closer look at OCTAVE as an example. It is a tool developed by the Software Engineering Institute (SEI) at Carnegie Mellon University. The goal of OCTAVE is to allow an organization to fully understand and manage the risks they face. OCTAVE usually has more components than most people want to throw at their risk assessment process, most because it was developed with large organizations in mind (over 300 employees). Due to demand for a simpler risk assessment, the SEI is creating OCTAVE-S, designed for smaller organizations. The OCTAVE-S is due in Q2 of 2003.
