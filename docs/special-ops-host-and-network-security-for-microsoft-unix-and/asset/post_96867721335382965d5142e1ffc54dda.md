# Performing Asset Inventory

- * The asset inventory can be used to understand how and where to implement layered security to reduce exposure.
- The prerequisite for a basic asset inventory is one or more range(s) of IP addresses. This list can be obtained by ICMP sweeps and TCP sweeps.
- Another technique for performing an asset inventory is a comprehensive scan, which requires scanning all possible 65535 TCP and UDP ports.

# Wardial to Discover Corporate Assets

- There are several decent wardial technologies available, but the DOS-based freeway applications are typically the best for identifying live dialup systems.

- Typically, wardial is performed after hours to avoid interrupting the normal course of business or alerting any employees. Additionally, after-hours dialing is preferred because that is typically the remote desktop applications are in use for employees who work from home.

- Oftentimes, a lot of information is given away in the carrier banners, such as company name, host or application name, and operating system, making it easy to identify the system that has dial-up access.

- The outlined methodology to accurately remove dial-up systems for an organization and assess the security controls surrounding them is key when conducting a wardial. In this process, systems that do not need dial-up access can be identified and analog lines via dial-up, proper controls can be assessed to ensure that assets are protected by hackers.

# Managing Asset Exposure

- Exposure or exposure level refers to a host or network's relationship with another host or network and the bi-directional visibility they share. When generically, the term exposure level should take inbound and outbound exposure into account. The goal of understanding the exposure of your assets is to determine which exposures are accepted risk and which ones are not. This will allow for exposure remediation and subsequently a reduction in overall risk.

# Chapter 3 · Hunting for Vulnerabilities (HSV)

# Introduction

Confucius is attributed as saying, “Why do they use an axe when a hatchet would work as well?” This lesson is very applied in most successful attacks. Attackers commonly start simple, using broad techniques to find network weaknesses. According to Information Week's Global Security Survey, almost half of the companies cited known operating-system flaws as the primary means of system compromise. Consider the damage and cost inflicted by Internet worms such as Slammer, Code Red, and Nimda. These all could have been avoided by hardening techniques.

This chapter was written to help you find high-severity vulnerabilities (HSVs) so that you can remediate them. The process is not necessarily complicated to understand or carry out—consider this analogy from my childhood: Dragon fishing, a simplistic method used by children who cannot afford to buy bait, involves catching little fish to catch big fish. Each kid drags a broom handle attached with a 4x8-foot net and drags the net through the water. Even though the goal was to catch minnows to use bait, there was a day we pulled up the net and found an 8-inch fish!

This method is what I am proposing for your internal networks. Hunting HSVs is just like dragging a fishing net through your networks to catch basic vulnerabilities such as poor passwords, missing patches, and configurations that will assist you in the larger goal involving bigger “catches.” This chapter takes you on a tour of the tools and techniques that can be used to net the HSVs on your internal networks.
