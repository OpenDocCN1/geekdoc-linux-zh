# Defining the Scope

Defining the scope for your security policy is one of the first steps you need to perform once you've been able to justify why a security policy needs to be created, and it has been approved. The scope you define will be at the very beginning of your security policy, and if you choose to create multiple policies for different business groups, each of those needs a scope definition as well.

The dangers of failing to define your policy’s scope are demonstrated in the following example. One of my clients created their own security policies, because the data center thought it was within their scope of responsibility. However, when the parent organization created the scope for their security policies, they included the data center in their policy creation program. Because the data center had already created their own security policy program, they thought they were not included in the corporate policy program. When it came time to review their policies, it became apparent that this was not the case. The parent organization’s policies expected the data center would track inventories of their equipment by making a “daily check” of the servers they were maintaining. However, they did not have any sort of an asset inventory or tracking system, as corporate security policy required. The result was that the data center had a security program, not due to poor planning, but to poor scoping of responsibilities.

Included in the scoping statement will be a statement of intent, the business units that this policy pertains to, and any special groups that also need to know this this policy pertains to. The scope may also include specifications of responsibility—how groups know if various policies apply to them and not to other groups.

It’s important to define the areas of responsibility, because many people will go through the most amazing feats to avoid having a policy to apply. Many times it may be as simple as signing a statement of receipt or acknowledgment of a policy. Other times, it may go as actively activating duties and tasks so they are not subject to the policies underneath. For a example, a network administrator may avoid configuring a system on a frequent basis because he doesn’t record all the changes in a log book. At this point, it becomes an issue of policy enforcement, instead of simply scoping. This is an extreme case, but it can and does happen.
