A manager whose team lost two days worth of productivity because his department was not made aware of a major network topology shift will be far less eager to cooperate with IT than the manager who has never had such difficulties. This is the essence of security usability—when usability is adversely impacted security efforts suffer the consequences.

Most managers are privy to more information than their subordinates, and bear the responsibility of keeping that data private. Often, the information they have is exactly what their subordinates want to see, be it salary reviews, disciplinary communication, or directed directives from above regarding company-wide layoffs. But since management works closely with their teams, they do not lend themselves to the DMZ security model like Accounting and Human Resources. Further complicating matters, managers are not usually the most tech-savvy members, so there is little they can do on their own to ensure the privacy and integrity of their data.

Fortunately, there are tools available that require minimal training and can provide a great deal of security to these distributed users, shielding them from network-based intrusions and protecting sensitive data when they leave their PC unattended and forget to lock the console. Two of the most effective these tools are personal firewalls and data encryption tools.

Personal firewalls have gotten a bad rap in many organizations because they can be too invasive and intrusive. Nobody likes to have a window pop up in front of the document they're working on informing them of some event in arcane language they don't understand. The default installations of these applications are very intrusive and the benefits to these intrusions do not outweigh the hassle imposed when programs interrupt workflow. Some of these tools even attempt to profile applications and inform the user when new applications are launched, potentially representing a substantial intrusion to the user.

Vendors of personal firewalls have heard these complaints and reacted with some excellent solutions to managing these problems. Many personal firewall vendors now provide methods with a customized default policy that minimizes user interaction while still providing adequate protection. Desktop protection can be substantially improved by denying most unsolicited inbound connections.

Although it is important to provide network-level defense for personnel who have access to sensitive information, it is far more likely that intrusions and information disclosure will occur due to simply chance. Data encryption, in transit and locally, can prevent such accidents as those who held proved keys. A step beyond password protection, encrypted data does not rely on the application to provide security, so not one byte-wise of the storage medium can ascertain the contents of encrypted messages or files.

A number of encryption applications and algorithms are available to address varying levels of concern, but perhaps the most popular data encryption tools are those built around Pretty Good Privacy (PGP) system, developed by Phil Zimmermann in the early 1990s. PGP has evolved over the years, from its initial freeware releases, to purchase and commercialization by Network Associates, Inc., who recently sold the rights to the software to the PGP Compatible versions of PGP are still available as freeware from the International PGP Home Page (www.pgpi.org). The commercial aspects of PGP lie in the usability enhancements and some additional functionality such as enterprise escrow keys and file system encryption.

PGP does take some getting used to, and unlike personal firewalls PGP is an active protection method. However, in my experience users tend to react positively to PGP and its capabilities; there tends to be a James Bond-esque quality to dealing with encrypted communications. For e-mail applications, PGP adoption is brought about more word-of-mouth, with users reminding one another... In the case of a misdirected financial report, PGP key selection forces the user to review the recipient list one more time, and if encrypted messages later find their way to individuals for whom the message was not intended, they will be unable to decrypt the contents.

### Quantifying the Need for Security

One of the first things you can do to increase awareness is to attempt to quantify the unknown elements of risk. By simply monitoring network traffic, you can get an understanding of what traverses your network. Depending on the resources available to you and your department, both fiscal and time-based, there are a number of approaches you can take. If your environment is such that you've already implemented intrusion detection, QoS (quality of service) or advanced traffic monitoring, feel free to skip ahead. Right now we are going to offer some suggestions to the less fortunate.

Get your hands on a midrange PC system, and build up an inexpensive traffic monitoring application using the Snort IDS (www.snort.org). Snort is an open source, multipatform intrusion detection system built around the libpcap capture library. Arrange to gain access to a spanning port at one of your internal peering points. Make sure the system is working by triggering a few of the sensors, make sure there's enough disk space to capture a fair number of incidents, and leave the system alone for a couple of days.

Once you've got your system to know your network, take a look at the results. If you enabled a good number of capture rules, you will undoubtedly have a mighty collection of information about what kind of data is traversing the peering point you chose. Look for some of the obvious threats: SQL connections, NetBIOS traffic, and various attack signatures. If your data isn't, don't make the mistake of assuming that you're in the clear; many organizations with extensive IDS infrastructures can go days at a time without any sort of alert being generated. Just take what you can from the data you've gathered and put the system back online.
