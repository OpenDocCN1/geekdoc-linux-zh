# A Note on Procedural Development

Now we have the current policies read and interviews performed. We may have performed a gap analysis and identified locations where we require policies or procedures. We also have performed a risk assessment, to help us identify the locations that require the most immediate or directed attention. We have also broadly identified the policies that we want to change or new policies we want to create.

This is lofty policies usually daily, and good policies show their value as a guiding document. This is procedural development, which can be much more extensive than policy development, because there are so many roles and responsibilities to address. Procedures define controls that need to be followed to enforce the policies we established. If we had policies without procedures, it would be like having a constitution without laws.

Choosing yours is a critical decision point in your policy development process because it is a direct expense from your company that has always been difficult to cost-justify. Your goal should be to protect your assets by closing your holes in the most cost-effective way possible. For example, if you discover a buffer overflow in your Web server software that allows compromise, you can patch that right away. However, you want to get to the root of the problem to prevent this from happening. Usually this can be accomplished through the creation of a build policy for new systems, and a maintenance policy for systems online. A less effective decision could be to migrate your Web server to a different platform with an different OS and server software. Although this may fix the immediate problem, chances are the new platform will eventually become vulnerable as well. Consideration should be given to long-term fixes, otherwise you might end up with a series of ineffective, short-term, or ill-chosen fixes. Other examples of controls include password policies, encrypted disk policies, and laptop handling policies, to name a few.
