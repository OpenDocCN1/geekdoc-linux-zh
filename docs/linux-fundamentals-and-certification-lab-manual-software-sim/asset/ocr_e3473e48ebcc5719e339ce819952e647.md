Tape drives offer huge amounts of readily available storage area, allowing backup of local and remote computer systems. Many tape drive systems contain several tapes at once as well as multiple drives to allow access to vast volumes of storage capacity. Tape devices are accessed through device files. For SCSI drives the filenames are /dev/stX, where X is the number of the device. EIDE drives are named /dev/htX. **Hard drives are the fastest of backup devices and the most expensive.** Even though the cost of hard drives has dropped significantly, the cost per megabyte is still quite high. Hard disk backups are good for small, immediate backup needs. For example, if you need to reinstall a program on a system, you might back up to a hard drive before installing. Tape drives have come a long way recently in terms of capacity and performance. Modern tape drives are faster than optical drives and rival disk drives in some cases. The shelf life of modern tape cartridges can exceed 30 years. The real advantage of disk media, whether it is hard disk or optical, is how quickly data can be restored. With disk media, direct access to data is possible; but with a linear tape the data may be located several hundred feet into the tape, so access to the data will take considerable time compared to disk. Hard disks are beginning to be used for backups, but in these cases backup data is usually initially written to disk and then copied or moved to tape. This procedure is sometimes referred to as *staging*, and it offers some advantages in how the data is organized on tape. Optical drives are most often used for archiving and near line storage. They are expensive backups and disaster recovery, tape is still the media of choice. **Optical drives are slightly faster than tape and have a longer storage life.** The cost of the optical media has dropped to a price comparable to that of tape. With newer drive systems that support “juke box” technology (the ability to have a large volume of optical media available), this is a viable option. **High-capacity floppy drives** provide up to 200 MB of storage on a floppy cartridge that is about the size of a standard floppy disk. The storage space is about the same as that of tape drives, but the media is considerably more expensive. A common type of high-capacity floppy drive is the Zip drive from Iomega. Generally, the backup device of choice is the tape drive. Even in a home system, a tape can be installed for under a hundred dollars, and because tapes are reusable the long-term cost is minimal; however, many newer machines are equipped with CD-R or CD-RW drives, and the CD-R media is inexpensive. On a single-machine system, the CD-R is a good choice for a backup. There are three basic types of backups: full, incremental, and differential. Full backups copy the entire volume of data and then mark the files as having been backed up. Incremental backups copy only those files that have been changed since the last backup and then mark the files as having been backed up. Differential backups copy only the files that have been changed since the last backup and do not mark the files as having been backed up. It is a good idea to keep multiple backups of your systems. Most administrators follow a three-backup pattern with daily backups. In this pattern you perform a full backup at a scheduled time once a week. During the week perform daily incremental backups, rotating the tapes over several days or weeks. At the end of the second week, start over with a new set of tapes for the full backup. At the end of the third week, do a full backup with another set of tapes. At the end of the fourth week, overwrite the first week’s full backup tapes. Rotate the tapes every week thereafter. Your backup rotation requirements may be different, but you get the idea. Never have only one backup. It is a good idea to have at least one full backup set off site. If disaster does strike and you lose the entire facility, you will have at least one backup set that will survive and can use it to get up and running. Linux has several different programs for backing up and restoring data. A backup program is needed to perform the backup itself, but a backup policy is needed to document how, what, and when to back up. How to perform a backup is covered in this chapter; however, what and when to back up can be different for every Linux installation. What to back up depends on the needs of the organization. When to back up depends on how critical the data is.
