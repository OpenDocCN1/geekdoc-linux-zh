# ISDN Services

Integrated Services Digital Network (ISDN) was designed to solve bandwidth problems that small offices or dial-in users have with telephone dial-in services. ISDN was developed to use the existing telephone wiring system but provide a totally digital network. It works similar to a standard phone dial-up connection in that it places a call when needed.

ISDN has several advantages over traditional dial-up systems. ISDN can be used to transmit more than traditional computer data. It can provide access to digital video and telephone network services (voice) as well.

ISDN initially connects much faster than a modem because ISDN uses out-of-band signaling. This means that ISDN uses a separate channel for connection maintenance and does not use the same channel(s) that are used for data. This out-of-band channel is called the D channel. ISDN provides faster data transfer than a modem. A modem is limited to a maximum of 56 Kbps (actually slower), whereas an ISDN bearer channel (B channel) can provide 64 Kbps. ISDN can use multiple B channels to provide faster rates.

Two ISDN services are available—Basic Rate Interface (BRI) and Primary Rate Interface (PRI). BRI offers two B channels and one D channel, referred to as 2B+D. This delivers a total bandwidth of 144 Kbps, two 64 Kbps B channels, plus one 16 Kbps D channel. The D channel is used only for control of the connection, not data transfer, so the total usable bandwidth with 2B + D is 128 Kbps. PRI offers 23 B channels and one D channel. This provides a total bandwidth of 1.544 Mbps. The D channel provides 64 Kbps with PRI.

A terminal adapter is used to connect to the Network Terminating Unit that the communications company installed for your ISDN connection. The terminal adapter has serial interfaces that are used to connect to a computer system. Linux works with ISDN just as with any other serial device.

The Linux device names for ISDN use the format /dev/ttyIn, where n is the number of the device. For example, tty10 is similar to tty$0.

# DSL and Cable Modem Services

Digital Subscriber Lines (DSL) and cable modem services provide always-on service, meaning that the connection to the remote server is always available. Each of these services has advantages and disadvantages.

+   DSL allows for transmission of voice, video, and data over existing telephone lines. The DSL modem usually has an Ethernet connection to your computer. DSL is a term that refers to a family of services, each with different characteristics.

+   Asymmetric Digital Subscriber Line (ADSL) is the most popular DSL type. It supports up to 8 Mbps for downloading and 1 Mbps for uploading. ADSL Lite technology provides bandwidth downstream up to 1.544 Mbps and upstream up to 512 Mbps. The maximum range of ADSL service is 12,000 feet. You must be within this range of the provider in order to use this service.

+   Symmetrical Digital Subscriber Line (DSL) uses a single pair of phone lines at the same speed in both the upstream and downstream directions. SDSL provides a maximum bandwidth of 1.544 Mbps at a maximum range of 15,000 feet. SDSL is often used for video conferencing, where the downstream and upstream speeds need to be the same.

+   High bit-rate Digital Subscriber Line (HDSL) provides up to 2,048 Mbps with three phone lines at a maximum range of 20,000 feet. HDSL is similar to SDSL and is most commonly used to provide T1 lines to businesses.

+   ISDN Digital Subscriber Line (IDSL) provides symmetric download and upload speeds from 64 to 144 Kbps on a single phone line. Its maximum range is 39,600 feet, which can be doubled with a repeater.

+   Very high bit-rate Digital Subscriber Line (VDSL) is the fastest DSL technology. It provides 13 to 52 Mbps upload and 1.5 to 2.3 Mbps download speed. The range of VDSL is from 1,000 to 4,500 feet.
