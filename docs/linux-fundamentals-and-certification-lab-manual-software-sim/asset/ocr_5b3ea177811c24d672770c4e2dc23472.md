**Disk Backup** One inexpensive solution to file system failure is to make sure you always have a backup. RAID provides high availability, but at a higher cost. Disk backup provides data availability, but at a much slower rate if something goes wrong. (Chapter 6 discussed disk backups.) Tape backups are more associated with providing disaster recovery capabilities.

The typical backup hardware is a tape drive; however, smaller installations also use removable hard drives, optical disks, and high-capacity floppy disk devices.

**Tape drives** are the slowest backup devices; but tape media is the least expensive per megabyte. Tape drives offer huge amounts of readily available storage area, allowing backup of local and remote computer systems. Many tape drive systems can contain several tapes at once and have multiple drives to allow access to vast volumes of storage capacity.

The new LTO-2 tape drives are faster than most magnetic disk drives in sequential I/O. They can sustain 30 MB/sec. The biggest advantage of disk drives is that they are random access devices. Tape media has a much longer shelf life than can be expected from a disk drive.

Tape devices are accessed through device files. For SCSI drives the file-names are /dev/stX, where X is the number of the device. EIDE drives are named /dev/htX.

**Hard drives** are the fastest backup devices and the most expensive. Even though the cost of hard drives has dropped significantly, the cost per megabyte is still quite high. Hard disk backups are good for small immediate backup needs. For example, if you need to reinstall a program on a system, you might back it up to a hard drive before installing.

**Optical drives** are slightly faster than tape and have a longer storage life. The cost of optical media has dropped to a price comparable to that of tape. With newer drive systems supporting “juke box” technology (the capability to have a large volume of optical media available), this is a viable option.

**High-capacity floppy drives** provide up to 200 MB of storage on a floppy cartridge about the size of a standard floppy disk. The storage speed is about the same as that of tape drives, but the media is considerably more expensive. A common type of high-capacity floppy drive is the Zip™ drive from Iomega.

Generally, the backup device of choice is the tape drive. Even in a home system, a tape can be installed for less than one hundred dollars; and tapes are reusable, making the long-term cost minimal. However, many newer machines are equipped with CD-R or CD-RW drives, and the CD-R media is inexpensive. On a single-machine system, the CD-R is a good choice for a backup.

There are three basic types of backups: full, incremental, and differential. Full backups copy the entire volume of data and mark the files as having been backed up. Incremental backups copy only those files that have changed since the last backup and then mark the files as having been backed up. Differential backups copy only the files that were changed since the last backup and do not mark the files as having been backed up.

It is a good idea to keep multiple backups of your systems. Most administrators follow a three-backup pattern with daily backups. The pattern is to perform a full backup at a scheduled time once a week. You should perform incremental backups daily during the week, rotating the tapes over several days or weeks. At the end of the second week, start over with a new set of tapes for the full backup. At the end of the third week, do a full backup with another set of tapes. At the end of the fourth week, overwrite the first week’s full backup tapes. Rotate the tapes every week thereafter. Your backup rotation requirements may be different, but you get the idea: Never have only one backup.

It is a good idea to have at least one full backup set offsite. If disaster does strike and you lose the entire facility, you will have at least one surviving backup set that you can use to get your system up and running.
