# SCSI Hard Drive Problems

SCSI hard drives have changed over the years, as has most technology. The first hard drives, called SCSI 1 or Narrow SCSI, used a 50-pin connector that ran at a maximum data rate of 5 MHz. To increase the bandwidth, the bus speed was increased to 10 MHz, creating the new standard SCSI 1 or Fast SCSI. The current SCSI technology under development is called ULTRA 320 and provides for bus speeds of 320 MHz, or up to 320 MB/s data transfer. Table 13-1 shows a partial list of bus speeds and SCSI terminology, as endorsed by the SCSI Trade Association (STA).

Changes in SCSI technology make changes necessary in the requirements for cabling and termination. For example, the faster the bus speed, the shorter the cable connecting the devices must be.

The hdparm command can be used to test performance, but it cannot be used to change the parameters of the hard drive. Usually, problems encountered with SCSI devices can be traced to hardware issues.

Often a SCSI controller has firmware that is accessible by the user during the boot process, which may help troubleshoot SCSI device problems.

**Proper termination** is essential to SCSI hard drive function. A pulse propagating along the SCSI bus will reflect from any part of the bus that is different from the rest. These reflections add and subtract in odd combinations and cause the original pulse to be distorted and corrupted. To prevent or minimize reflections from the ends of the bus, terminators are added to absorb the energy from the pulses.

Terminators must be present at two positions on the SCSI bus, at the beginning and the end of the bus, and must occur within four inches of the physical ends of the SCSI bus. Termination can be internal or external.

**Internal** termination SCSI drives provide termination within the workings of the drive and can be enabled/disabled with jumper settings.

**External** termination is done with inline, or feed-through, terminators. These are close-ended terminator plugs connected to the end of the bus.
