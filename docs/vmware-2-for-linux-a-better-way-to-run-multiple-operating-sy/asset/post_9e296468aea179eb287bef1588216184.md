## Conclusion

Windows 2000 is a feature-rich operating system and arguably puts a greater strain on VMware and your host computer than any of the other operating systems you can install. It certainly requires the most powerful computer to get started.

By bringing Windows 2000 under the VMware wing, you can stay up to date with the latest developments in Microsoft-powered enterprise software, while retaining the core open standard flexibility of Linux. It's hard to imagine a more productive way to run your computer.

You’ve seen how VMware performs hosting two of the most popular desktop and server operating-system families: Windows and Linux.

Windows is a logical guest OS choice simply because of its near-universal acceptance and importance in day-to-day personal and professional computing. And how can you argue with Linux? ## Exploring the “Other” UNIX Alternative

Linux gets great press for a number of things, including being a zero-cost drop-in replacement for many of the programs and tasks normally reserved for expensive UNIX systems. But the truth is that until fairly recently, the UNIX-alike operating system FreeBSD (earlier NetBSD) was ahead of Linux in features, functionality, and stability.

FreeBSD is derived from a different source tree but is similar to Linux in that it is an open-source operating system based around classical UNIX principles, with an eye toward establishing itself in all sectors of the computing market.

Discussions about Linux versus FreeBSD can get just as impassioned as any other operating-system evangelism. It is often contended, for example, that FreeBSD’s TCP/IP networking is more robust than Linux’s. Many developers prefer the looser terms of the Berkeley open-source license that governs FreeBSD. The General Public License (GPL), which applies to the Linux kernel and many of its most important components, requires companies and developers to disclose information many consider vitally proprietary.

Undeniably, Linux has enjoyed much more of the spotlight than FreeBSD in recent years, but FreeBSD users benefit from their own ongoing development, including a Linux compatibility layer that can make the decision between operating systems largely a matter of taste. In general, over the past few years, what has been good for UNIX, Linux, or FreeBSD individually has been good for all three as a group.
