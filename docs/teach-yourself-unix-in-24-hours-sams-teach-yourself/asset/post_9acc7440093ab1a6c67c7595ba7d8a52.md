# Task 22.1: Sending E-Mail to Internet Users

The common use of the Internet is properly to send electronic mail between individuals and mailing lists. What's really a boon is that everyone, from New York to Los Angeles, Japan to Germany, South Africa to India, has an address that's very similar, and you've already seen it here. The notation is user@host.domain, where user is the account name or full name, host is the name of the user's machine, and domain is the user's location in world.

Reading the host and domain information from left to right (from the outside in, really), you can decode information about someone by looking at the person's e-mail address. My address at a system called Netcom, for example, is taylor@netcom2.netcom.com, which right to left, tells you that I'm at a commercial site (com), with a company by the name of Netcom (netcom), and the name of the computer I am using is netcom2. My account on Netcom is taylor.

There are lots of top-level domains, and the most common are shown in Table 22.1.

The national convention for the From: line in electronic mail clearly varies. There are three basic notations you see in this line: an address, such as the one from gopher@scorpio.kent.edu; an address with the name in parentheses, such as the message from Linda Wei halfway down the list; and a line with the person's name followed by his or her e-mail address in brackets, such as the first listed line.

Notice the various sites from which I've received electronic mail in the past few days: SFSU.edu is San Francisco State University, oracle.com is Oracle Corporation in California, PURD.edu is Purdue University, CompuServer.Com is InfoWorld magazine's Macintosh network running cc:Mail, xalt.com is XALT Corporation, and kent.edu is Kent State University. The message is from an educational institution in The Netherlands!
