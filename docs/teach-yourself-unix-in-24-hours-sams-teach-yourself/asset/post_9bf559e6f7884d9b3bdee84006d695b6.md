The percent sign is UNIX's way of telling you that it's ready for you to enter some commands. The percent sign is the equivalent of an enlisted soldier saluting and saying, "Ready for duty!" or an employee saying, "What shall I do now, boss?"

![](img/f32f6f53c129de2ebafcd2b4ff01b1c9_37_0.png)

Your system might be configured so that you have some slightly different prompt here. The possibilities include a $ for the Korn or Bourne shells, your location in the file system, the current time, the command-index number (which you'll learn about as you learn how to teach the UNIX command-line interpreter to adapt to your work style, rather than versa), and the name of the computer itself. Here are some examples:

- [/users/taylor]
- (mentor) 33 :
- taylor@mentor %

Your prompt might not look exactly like any of these, but it has one unique characteristic: it is at the beginning of the line that your cursor sits on, and it reappears each time you've completed with any UNIX program.

- At this point, you're ready to enter your first UNIX command—exit—to sign off from the computer system. Try it. On my system, entering exit shuts down all my programs and hangs up the telephone connection. On other systems, it returns the login prompt. Many UNIX systems offer a pith quote as you leave, too.

```
% exit
He hesitates is lost.
4.3850 UNIX mentor.utech.edu 5:38pm on Fri, 7 Feb 1993
login:
```

| UNIX is case-sensitive, so the exit command is not the same as EXIT. If you enter a command in all uppercase, the system won't find it and instead will respond with the compliant command not found. |

# Task 2.2: Changing Passwords with passwd

Having logged in to a UNIX system, you can clearly see that there are differences between UNIX and a PA or Macintosh computer. Certainly the style of interaction is different. With UNIX, the keyboard becomes the exclusive method of instructing computer what to do, and the mouse sits idle, waiting for something to happen.

The greatest differences is that UNIX is a multiuser system, as you learned in the previous hour. As you learn more about UNIX, you'll find that this characteristic has an impact on a variety of tasks and commands. The next UNIX command you learn is one that exists because of the multiuser nature of UNIX: passwd.

With the passwd command, you can change the password associated with your individual account name. With the personal identification number (PIN) for your automated-teller machine, the value of password is directly related to how secret it remains.

> UNIX is careful about the whole process of changing passwords. It requires you to enter your current password to prove you're really you. Imagine that you are at a computer center and have the room to make a quick phone call. Without effort, a prankster could learn and quickly change your password to something you wouldn't know. That's why you should log out if you're not going to be near your system, and that's also why passwords are never echoed in UNIX.

- 1. Consider what happens when I use the passwd command to change the password associated with my account:
  % passwd
  Changing password for taylor.
  Old password:
  New password:
  Retype new password:
  %

2. Notice that I never received any visual confirmation that the password I actually entered was the same as the password I thought I entered. This is not as dangerous as it seems, though, if I had made any typographical errors, the password I entered the second time (when the system said Retype new:) wouldn't have matched the first. In a no-match situation, the system would have warned me that the information I supplied was inconsistent:

```
% password
Changing password for taylor.
Old password:
New password:
Retype new password:
Mismatch password unchanged.
```

Once you change the password, don't forget it. Reset it to a known value if you don't know the current password requires the assistance of a system administrator or other operator. Renumbering your password can be a catch-22, though you don't want to write down the password that reduces its secrecy, but you don't want to forget it, either. You want to make sure that you pick a good password, as described in Task 2.3.

# Task 2.3: Picking a Secure Password

If you are an aficionado of old movies, you are familiar with the thrillers in which hoods break into an office and spin the dial on the safe a few times, snicker a bit about how the boss shouldn't chosen his daughter's birthday as the combination, and crank the open. If you're really familiar with the genre, you recall films in which the rifle rifles the drawers and find the combination of the safe taped to the underside of a drawer as a fail-safe—or a failed safe, as case may be). The moral is that you should choose good secret passwords or combinations and keep secure.

For computers, security is tougher because in less than an hour, a fast computer system can test all the words in an English dictionary against your account password. If your password is kitten, or worse, your account name, any semi-competent bad guy could be in your account and messing with your files in no time.

Many of the more modern UNIX systems have heuristics, or smarts, built into the command; the heuristics check to determine whether what you've entered is reasonably secure.

The tests performed typically answer these questions:

- Is the proposed password at least six characters long? (A longer password is secure.)
- Does it have digits and letters? (A mix of both is better.)
- Does it mix upper- and lowercase letters? (A mix is better.)
- Is it in the online dictionary? (You should avoid common words.)
- Is it a name or word associated with the account? (Dave would be a bad password for your account taylor because your full name on the system is Dave Taylor.)
