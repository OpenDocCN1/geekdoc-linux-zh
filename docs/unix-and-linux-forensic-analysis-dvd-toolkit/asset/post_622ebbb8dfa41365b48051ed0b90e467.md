## Introduction

Once an attack has occurred or a system or network has been compromised, it’s essential to be able to sift through the evidence of what’s happened. From a technical information technology (IT) perspective, this means how to find, recognize, and locate the visible evidence of a cybercrime. From a law enforcement perspective, this means knowing how to handle such evidence to make sure it is admissible in court if necessary. However, these roles overlap somewhat. A good investigator also needs to know the technicalities of where and how evidence can be located, to properly put together the offense report and help the prosecutor formulate questions for witnesses. Likewise, the IT professional needs an understanding of how evidence must be treated to preserve its integrity in the eyes of the law.

In this Appendix, we focus primarily on the former activity; we introduce various sources and potential types of evidence that investigators gather to provide evidence of attempts to perpetrate cyberimes. In some cases, this evidence may be collected whether the attempted crime succeeds or fails; in other cases, such evidence may be available only as a byproduct of a successful attack.

To some extent, computers and other network devices are capable of recording information about activity that occurs within or passes through them. When evidence of cybercrime is needed, this kind of data can be an essential element in making a decision to prosecute the people responsible. But with so many other aspects of system and network security, it’s necessary to understand the underlying technologies and software that must be put to work to make it possible to produce such evidence. It’s also necessary to understand what this evidence looks like, how it may be interpreted, and what kinds of telltale signs or data to look for that could not only help document that a cybercrime was committed, but also help identify the responsible party or parties and prove to the satisfaction of a jury that they did.

A lack of diligence in protecting IT assets and information is very often involved in exposing companies and organizations to loss or harm. This loss or harm may occur as a result of either an insider attack (from an employee, consultant, or other person) or an attack mounted from outside the network boundary. We’ve also mentioned that there is no such thing as perfect security, so it’s also necessary to concede that even a remote chance of successful attack, penetration, or compromise means that it’s necessary to be able to monitor, detect, and react to security incidents if and when they occur.

Thus, an important part of the due diligence necessary in dealing with security matters is to be ready to perform subsequent analysis and investigations to determine causes and to identify perpetrators whenever possible. Whether or not an organization decides to prosecute a security incident is almost beside the point. The organization and its IT professionals, the real value of understanding how to gather and interpret evidence of cybercrimes comes from the ability to improve or harden security after the fact, to prevent any recurrence of the attacks or circumstances that permitted such crimes to occur in the first place.

Even if the company or organization never decides to pursue legal remedies for attempted or successful attacks, the ability to gather, interpret, and respond to the information inherent in the tracks and traces of such events is an essential part of a proper security regime. Finally, auditing is the process of tracking access and use of resources so that activity may be logged.

## Security Auditing and Log Files

An important concept in system and network security is what’s often called AAA, or “triple-A” model. In this case, the acronym is subject to several interpretations, including:

- Administration, authorization, and authentication
- Authentication, authorization, and accounting

Although both expansions of the acronym are pretty widespread, the second is the one we use.

The idea behind AAA is that strong security rests on a three-legged foundation in which:

- *Authentication* ensures that users, processes, and services that consume system resources or access their contents provide sufficient proof of identity to enter systems before any requests may be issued.
- *Authorization* (sometimes called access control) ensures that requests for resources will not be granted unless requesters have the permissions necessary not only to read or otherwise inspect the contents of the resources they want to access, but also that they have explicit permissions to perform the kind of operation they seek to perform. Some individuals may be granted read-only access to information which they have no permissions to make changes, whereas others may be granted the ability to modify or.
- *Accounting* relates to monitoring and tracking system activity. Some companies or organizations put a monetary value on computer resources, usage, and access. In this situation, accounting tracks activity to access so-called “chargebacks” for use of computer or network services based on actual consumption. From a security standpoint, the other form of monitoring or tracking under the general heading of accounting is called *auditing*.

Note that both authentication and authorization put various kinds of barriers or checks between users (consumers) and the resources they seek to utilize. Only accounting tracks what actually happens on the networks and systems it monitors.
