And “Linux” is an enormously popular, free UNIX-like operating system that is designed with the philosophy of Unix, but in actuality is not a truly UNIX-compliant implementation of that philosophy. The history of *nix is very rich, incestuous, and convoluted to the point we can't begin to give it justice. Knowing the heritage of “System V” *nix versus those cut from the Berkeley Software Distribution (BSD) cloth is useful in understanding why commands never seem to have the same switches and options (-ef vs ps -aux). If you'd like to see a family tree of all the *nix variants out, spend a few minutes soaking in the diagrams at www.levenez.com/unix/ just trying to find Linux.

While these distinctions make your head hurt, the good news is that the nomenclature isn't important to your daily work, though a familiarity with the history can certainly be helpful. To deal with all these particulars, we will follow the lead of other authors and avoid the clunkiness of trademarks, or of hyphenated concoctions of “Unix-like” and try to refer to the whole mess of Unix and friends as “*nix” when referring to Linux or compliant operating systems.

The further good news is that with some basic Linux skills, you'll be opened up to the larger realm where multitude of proprietary and free *nix implementations await you. Knowing Linux and its terminology will help you feel somewhat at home with these popular *nix operating systems below. Some you have probably heard of if not encountered first hand. In a hand-wavingly rough order-of-likely-encounter, *nix operating systems you may see in your work at some point:

- Linux, our best friend, available in a colorful array of distributions.
- Apple OS X, little known to be hosting a fully compliant UNIX-based on FreeBSD behind all these pretty graphics. Try the Terminal application in the Utilities folder and embrace the Unix goodness.
- Solaris, from Sun Microsystems.
- HP-UX, from Hewlett-Packard.
- AIX, IBM’s Unix variant.
- Tru64, now owned by Hewlett-Packard, via Compaq DEC, and lived its early life as Digital Unix.

# Linux Distributions

Like in discussions of *nix where many players are at the table, even Linux itself has a rich array of choices, for better or worse. Linux is available in a variety of different flavors that express the rich diversity of people who are using the operating system, and who are taking advantage of its open source nature to tweak and create a Linux that solves problems in their own way.

Some of the common Linux distributions include: Ubuntu (our choice for this), Red Hat Enterprise (RHEL), Fedora, SUSE Linux Enterprise (Novell), OpenSUSE, Gentoo, Debian, Mandriva, and hundreds others. If you want to keep an eye on free open source distributions, you can drop your jaw at your first visit to http://distrowatch.com/.

All distributions implement the Linux kernel, and at the command prompt you'll experience similar things in all of them. Where distributions differ from one another is in the realm of what software is included in a default installation, how software packages are added to the system, what window manager user interface (GUI) (if any) is used by default, and the release philosophy when it comes to stable updates.

For forensic work, it's useful to be aware that there are different flavors, and that the location of program, log, and configuration files vary from one Linux distribution to another. The mantra of “there’s more than one way to do it” is alive and well in Linux.

# Booting Ubuntu Linux from the LiveCD

Once you have the ISO image properly burned to a CD-ROM, pop it into your computer's CD-ROM drive, and reboot.

If you end up back in Windows, it's likely that your Basic Input Output System (BIOS) has your hard drive at a higher priority than your CD-ROM. A quick tweak of your BIOS settings will fix that. When you boot your computer, generally hitting F2 or DEL or some other key mention on the first splash on bootup will get you into your BIOS configuration. Under the Boot option you can modify the order in which your computer searches for bootable media. Read the instructions on the screen how to change the order (for Phoenix BIOS, use the right arrow to get to the Boot menu and arrow down to the CD-ROM device and press Shift and 1 the same time).
