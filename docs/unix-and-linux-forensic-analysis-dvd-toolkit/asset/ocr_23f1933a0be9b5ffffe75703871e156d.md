# Appendix A • Implementing Cybercrime Detection Techniques on Windows and *nix

Although both expansions of the acronym are pretty widespread, the second is the one that we use in this Appendix.

The idea behind AAA is that strong security rests on a three-legged foundation in which:

+   - *Authentication* ensures that users, processes, and services that consume system resources or access their contents provide sufficient proof of identity to enter systems and networks before any requests may be issued.
- *Authorization* (sometimes also called *access control*) ensures that requests for resources will not be granted unless requesters have the permissions necessary not only to read or otherwise inspect the contents of the resources they want to access, but also that they have explicit permissions to perform the kind of operation they seek to perform on the resource. Some individuals may be granted read-only access to information which they have no permissions to make changes (or to delete such information altogether), whereas others may be granted the ability to modify or delete such information at will.
- *Accounting* relates to monitoring and tracking system activity. Some companies or organizations put a monetary value on computer resources, usage, and access. In this situation, accounting tracks activity to access so-called “chargebacks” for use of computer or network services based on actual consumption. From a security standpoint, the other form of monitoring or tracking under the general heading of accounting is called *auditing*. As in its formal naming in financial accounting, auditing means tracking access and use of resources—in this case, communication links, systems, networks, and related resources, so that activity may be logged. This auditing deposits tangible data into various kinds of computerized records so that they may be analyzed for all purposes after the fact. Such logs provide a key source of evidence in detecting and analyzing cybercrimes, whether only attempted or successfully completed.

Note that both authentication and authorization put various kinds of barriers or checks between users (consumers) and the resources they seek to utilize. Only accounting tracks what actually happens on the networks and systems it monitors.
