# Introduction

Once on-site at a customer location, it's important to sit down with the customer and find out what has transpired. Understand that this conversation will probably provide you with different information than you may have initially received from any prior triage calls. I am not sure if it has to do with a lack of understanding of the full breadth and depth of the situation, or if the stress of the incident leads to details being missed, but from my experience this is a pretty solid rule of thumb. Expect things change once you get on-site and physically get a feel for the network and the systems that are in scope.

Something I try to avoid is what I refer to the “shotgun” approach. Too many investigators simply show up at a customer location and starting hosts left and right, which I suppose is fine if you want to create more work for yourself. Maybe they think that by casting a really wide net, they will surely get whatever critical data may be and not return to the customer site later. While this approach will technically work, it's far too consuming and generates many erroneous data in most cases. I prefer to take a more methodical approach by finding which hosts were involved in the incident, and eliminating (if possible) all other hosts.

With a decent understanding of networking concepts, and with the help available from the customer’s systems administrators, eliminating out-of-scope hosts is not all difficult. It's a matter of gauging technical possibility and log file review. For example, if host X is on a Virtual Local Area Network (VLAN) with other hosts, obviously these five hosts will be in scope for the assessment. Now, what if that VLAN only has a route to one of three other VLANs? Logically, only that one VLAN would be considered in scope for the incident, even if the customer network is comprised of several VLANs. To be on the safe side, you should perform a log file review to ensure that no connections were made to any of the VLANs, which you have technically determined to be out of scope, as a router compromise could lead to new routes added by an intruder. The same should be done for the VLANs and hosts within the two VLANs that were determined to be in scope. Provided the customer has the appropriate level of logging, you can determine if a host was touched by another. If you can show that a particular host was not touched, you can eliminate that host from the scope of the assessment.

The practice of eliminating hosts for the lack of information is commonly referred to as “negative evidence.” The key proponent in this methodology is the burden of proof. You have to able to show that something absolutely did not happen.
