Other forms of spoofing may be detected by a traceback or reverse DNS lookup to compare domain names and associated IP addresses (when that is available) and dropping all packets where these information items show no correlation (as when the reported IP address originates outside the range of addresses assigned to the organization from which it claims to originate).

The real problem with spoofed traffic occurs when IDS or human administrators try to follow the traffic back to its source and various types of deands. Recall, for example, that various types of DoS or DDoS attacks rely on compromised intermediate computers, sometimes called zombies or agents, and you'll quickly understand why tracing back to their source can't always identify attackers. When you determine that certain attacks originate, you may only be able to identify victims rather than finding a “smoking gun” points to an attacker. The savvier and more sophisticated hacker who perpetrates an attack, the less likely is that he or she will provide direct clues that lead to his or her primary presence on the Internet. Rather, your identification efforts will lead you a trail of intermediaries, cut-outs, and anonymizer services, each of which you must then investigate to look for clues to the identity of the mastermind behind the cyberimes you are pursuing.
