Early computers were used mainly to perform numerical calculations, while the mathematical symbolic manipulations were still done by humans. # ## Mathematics

**Mathematics**—Mathematics is frequently encountered in association and interaction with astronomy, physics, and other branches of natural science, and it also has deep-rooted affinities to the humanities. It is a realm of knowledge entirely unto itself, and one of considerable scope.

**Relation to science.** Mathematics is not a branch of natural science itself. It does not deal with phenomena and objects of the external world and their relations to each other but, strictly speaking, only with objects and relations of its own imagery. One can practice meaningful mathematics without being concerned with science at all, and philosophical attempts to reduce all origin of mathematics to utilitarian motives are wholly unconvincing. However, mathematics is the language of science in a deep sense. Mathematics is an indispensable medium by which and within which science expresses, formulas, confirms, and communicates itself. And just as the language as such is not a tool like scissors and hammers, but a framework for thinking, so mathematics is not only specific and expresses thoughts and processes of thinking, but also creates them in turn. In this sense, mathematics does more than merely describe phenomena. It not only makes rigorously workable concepts and laws of science, but at certain crucial instances it makes them exist for the first time. Mathematics became an indispensable constituent of their creation and emergence as well.

**Creative formulas.** A formula is a string of mathematical symbols subject only to certain general rules of composition. To a working mathematician a string of symbols is a formula if it is something worth remembering. Much mathematics is concentrated and propelled by certain formulas of unusual import.

**Foundations**—Mathematical logic. A prime demand on mathematics is that it be deductively rigorous, and a traditional model for intended rigor is Euclid’s presentation of mathematical assertions in theorems. A theorem is a proposition which has been proved, excepting certain first theorems called axioms, which are admitted without proof, and to prove a theorem means to obtain it from other theorems by certain procedures of deduction or inference. It had long been commonplace that each branch of mathematics was based on its own axioms, but during the 19th century, mathematicians arrived at the insight that even the same branch might have alternate axioms. Specifically, there were envisaged alternative versions of two- and three-dimensional geometry, the axiom varied being the axiom on parallelism. It was also recognized that a set of axioms becomes mathematically possible if it is logically consistent, that is, if one cannot deduce from the axioms to theorems one of which, as a proposition, is the negation of the other. At the same time certain developments led to the realization that not only the axioms but the rules of inference themselves might be, and even ought to be, subjected to variations. Now if axioms and rules of inference are both viewed as subject to change, it is customary to speak of a mathematical system or also a formal system, and, of course, an indestructible first requirement is that the system be consistent after that is the first stated. Consistency alone is a somewhat negative property. There is a further property, called completeness, which is more positive, and which, if present, is very welcome. A system is complete if for any proposition which can be formulated it either can be proved that it holds or that its negation holds.

Other versions are little more than the negative requirement that certain combinations of inference be avoided.
