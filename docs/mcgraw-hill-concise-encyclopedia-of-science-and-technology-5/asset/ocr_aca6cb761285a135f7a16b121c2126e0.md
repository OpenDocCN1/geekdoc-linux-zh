Gram-negative bacteria, such as *Salmonella* and *Haemophilus*,
have a toxic component in their cell walls known as endotoxin
or lipopolysaccharide. Among other detrimental effects, endo-
toxins cause white blood cells to produce interleukin-1, a hor-
monelike substance that mediates the acute phase response.
Specifically, they are responsible for fever, malaise, headache,
muscle aches, and other nonspecific consequences of infection. The
exotoxins of *Clostridium botulinum* (botulinum toxin), *Clostridium
tetani* (tetanus toxin), *Clostridium perfringens* (alpha toxin),
and *Bacillus anthracis* (anthrax toxin) are responsible for botulism,
tetanus, gas gangrene, and anthrax. See ANTITOXIN, TOXIN,
TOXOGENIC ORGANISMS, and ENDOTOXIN.

Toxoids are toxoids that have been exposed to formaldehyde
or other chemicals that destroy their toxicity without impair-
ing immunogenicity. When injected into humans, toxoids elicit
specific antibodies known as antitoxins that neutralize circulating
toxin. Their immunization (vaccination) is very effective for sys-
temic toxoises, such as diphtheria and tetanus. See ANTIBODY,
IMMUNITY, TOXIN, and VACCINATION. (D.M.G.I.)

# **Toxin-antotoxin reaction**  A term used in serology to
denote the combination of a toxic antigen with its correspond-
ing antitoxin. If the antitoxin is derived from any species other
than the host, precipitation occurs over a wide range of reac-
tant ratios, 20:1 or more, as in heter-antigen-antibody reactions.
With horse antitoxin, flocculation occurs only if toxin and anti-
toxin are in equal proportion. A two-fold excess of either reactant
gives soluble complexes. In both instances, the reaction results in
partial or complete neutralization of the toxic activity of the
antigen. See ANTITOXIN, ANTIGEN, ANTITOXIN, NEUTRALIZATION
REACTION (IMMUNOLOGY), SEROLOGY. [H.P.T.]

# **Toxoplasma**  A genus of the subphylum Sporozoa. The
organisms are small and crescent-shaped. They move by body
flexion or gliding and have no flagella or pseudopodia. Character-
istic structures are the two-layered pellicle and underlying longi-
tudinal microtubules, micropyle, a conoid, paired organelles,
and micronemes.

The most distinguishing characteristic of the *Toxoplasma* is
the unique means of reproduction. Electron microscope studies
indicate that endogeny is the sole method. Endogeny is an
internal budding where one daughter cell is produced within a
mother cell which is destroyed in the process.

Only two stages are known in the life cycle of most animals.
One stage, the proliferative or trophozoite, occurs singly or in
groups within host cells. The other stage, the so-called coccidial
stage, consists of a large number of organisms which, with minor
differences, are structurally similar to the proliferative forms. See
SPOROZOA, TOXOPLASMIDIA. [H.G.S.]

# **Toxoplasmidia**  An order of the class Toxoplasma. Four
genera, *Toxoplasma*, *Besoita*, *Sarcozystis*, and *Enccephalozoon*,
make up the order. The organisms are parasites of verte-
brates. *Toxoplasma* is often found encysted in nerve tissue,
*Besoita* in connective tissue, and *Sarcocystis* in muscle. Very
little is known about *Encéphalozoon*, but the parasite has been
found in the brain of rabbits. See TOXOPLASMA; TOXOPLASMIOSIS. [H.G.S.]

# **Trace fossils**  Fossilized evidence of animal behavior, also
known as ichnofossils, bionomic sedimentary structures, biore-
solution structures, or lebensspuren. The fossils include burrows,
trails, and trackways created by organisms in unsubdivided sed-
iment (see illustration), as well as borings, gnawings, rasping,
and scrapings excavated by animals in hardened materials, such
as rock, shell, bone, or wood. Some workers also consider coprol-
ites (fossilized feces), fossilized pellets, burrow excavation
pellets, rhizoliths (plant root penetration structures), and algal
stromatolites to be trace fossils. See STROMATOLITE, and ICHNOFOS-
SIL. [A.E.E.]

Trace fossils are important in paleontology and paleoecology,
because they provide information about the presence of unpre-
served soft-bodied members of the original communities, life
habits of fossil organisms, evolution of certain behavior patterns
through geologic time, and biostratigraphy of otherwise unfossil-
siliferous deposits. Trace fossils are useful in sedimentology and
paleoenvironmental studies, because they are sedimentary
structures that are preserved in place and are very rarely re-
wrought and transported, as body fossils of animals and plants
commonly are. This fact allows trace fossils to be regarded as
reliable indicators of original conditions in the sedimentary envi-
ronment. The production of trace fossils involves disruption of
original stratification and sometimes results in alteration of sedi-
mentary texture or composition. See FOSSIL, PALEOECOLOGY, PALEO-
ONTOLOGY, SEDIMENTOLOGY.

Trace fossils occur in sedimentary deposits of all ages from
the late Precambrian to the Recent. Host rocks include limestone,
sandstone, siltstone, shale, coal, and other sedimentary rocks.
These deposits represent sedimentation in a broad spectrum of
settings, ranging from submarine (such as eolian dunes and
soil horizons) to subaqueous (such as rivers, lakes, swamps, tidal
flats, beaches, continental shelves, and the deep-sea floor). See
DEPOSITIONAL SYSTEMS AND ENVIRONMENTS.

Organisms may produce fossilizable traces on the sediment
surface (epigenic structures) or within the sediment (endoge-
nic structures). Those fossils may be preserved in full three-
dimensional relief (either wholly contained within a rock or
weathered out as a separate piece) or in partial relief (either
as a depression or as a raised structure on a bedding plane). Sim-
ply because a trace fossil is preserved on a bedding plane does
not indicate that it originally was an epigenic trace. Diagenetic
alteration of sediment commonly enhances the preservation of
trace fossils by differential cementation or selective mineraliza-
tion. In some cases, trace fossils have been preferentially replaced
by chert, dolomite, pyrite, glauconite, apatite, siderite, or other
minerals. See DIAGENESIS.

The study of trace fossils is known as ichnology. The prefix
"ichno-" (as in *ichnofossils* and *ichnotaxonomy*) and the suffix
"-ichnia" (as in *epichnia* and *hyptopachia*) commonly are em-
ploied to designate subjects relating to trace fossils. The suffix
"-ichnium" commonly is attached to the ichnogenous name of
many trace fossils (as in *Dimorphichnus* and *Teichichnus*). [A.E.E.]
