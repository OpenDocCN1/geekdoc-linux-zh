# Wind measurement

The determination of three pa-\nraters: the size of an air sample, its speed, and its direction\nof motion. Air movement or wind is a vector that is specified by\nspeed and direction; meteorological convention indicates wind\ndirection as the direction from which the wind blows (for exam-\nple, a southeast wind blows toward the northwest). Anemome-\nters measure wind speed, while wind vanes indicate direction.\nOn average, the wind blows horizontally over flat terrain; how-\never, gusts, thermals, cloud outflows, and many other conditions\nare associated with them significant short-term vertical wind\ncomponents. While classical wind instruments typically measure\nboth horizontal and vertical air movement, operational and per-\nsonal wind sensors measure only the horizontal component. See\nANEMOMETER.

There are many types of wind measurement instruments. In\nsitu devices measure characteristics of air in contact with the\ninstrument; often they are referred to as immersion sensors be-\ncause they are immersed in the fluid (air) they measure. Remote\nwind sensors make measurements without physical contact with\nthe portion of the atmosphere measured. Active remote sen-\nsors emit electromagnetic (for example, light or radio waves) or\nsound waves into the atmosphere and measure the amount and\nnature of the electromagnetic or acoustic power returned from\nthe atmosphere. See LIDAR; METEOROLOGICAL INSTRUMENTATION;\nMETEOROLOGICAL RADAR; Wind. [W.F.D.]

# Wind power

The extraction of kinetic energy from the\nwind and conversion of it into a useful type of energy: ther-\nmol, mechanical, or electrical. Wind power has been used for\ncenturies.

It has been estimated that the total wind power in the atmo-\nsphere averages about $3.6 \times 10^{12}$ kW, which is an annual energy\nof about 107,000 quads (1 quad $= 2.931 \times 10^{11}$ kWh). Only\na fraction of this wind energy can be extracted; estimated to be\na maximum of 4000 quads per year. According to what is com-\nmonly known as the Betz limit, a maximum of 59% of this power\ncan be extracted by a wind machine. Practical machines actu-\nally extract from 5 to 45% of the available power. Because the\navailable wind power varies with the cube of wind speed, it is\nvery important to find areas with high average wind speeds to\nlocate wind machines. See WIND.

Most research on wind power has been concerned with pro-\nducing electricity. Wind power is a renewable energy source that\nhas virtually no environmental problems. However, wind power\nhas limitations. Wind machines are expensive and can be located\nonly where there is adequate wind. These high-wind areas may\nnot be easily accessible or near existing high-voltage lines for\ntransmitting the wind-generated energy. Another disadvantage\noccurrs because the demand for electricity varies with time, and\nelectricity production must follow the demand cycle. Since wind\npower varies randomly, it may not be available when needed.\nThe storage of electrical energy is difficult and expensive, so\nthat wind power must be used in parallel with some other type\nof generator or with nonelectrical storage. Wind power teamed\nwith hydroelectric generators is attractive because the water can\nbe used for energy storage, and operation with underground\ncompressed-air storage is another option. See ELECTRIC POWER\nGENERATION; ENERGY SOURCES; ENERGY STORAGE.

The most common type of wind turbine for producing elec-\ntricity has a horizontal axis, with two or more aerodynamic blades\nmounted on the horizontal shaft. For a horizontal-axis machine,\nthe blade tips can travel at several times the wind speed, which\nresults in a high efficiency. The blade shape is designed by us-\ning the same aerodynamic theory as for aircraft. See PROPELLER\n(AIRCRAFT); TURBINE. (G.T.h)

# Wind stress

The drag or tangential force per unit area ex-\nerted on the surface of the Earth by the adjacent layer of moving\nair. Erosion of ground surfaces and the production of waves on\nwater surfaces are manifestations of wind stress. Surface wind\nstress determines the exchange of momentum between the Earth\nand the atmosphere and exerts a strong influence on the typi-\ncal variation of wind through the lowest kilometer of the atmo-\nsphere. Estimated values of surface wind stress range up\nto several dynes per square centimeter (0.1 pascal), depending\non the nature of the surface and the character of the adjacent\nairflow. See METEOROLOGY.

Significant stresses arise within the lower atmosphere because\nof the strong shear of the wind between the slowly moving air\nnear the ground and the more rapidly moving air a kilometer\nabove and because of the turbulent nature of the airflow in this\nregion. The turbulent eddies referred to here have characteristic\ndimensions ranging up to a few hundred meters.

Wind pressure is the force exerted by the wind per unit area\nof solid surface exposed normal to the wind direction and is also\nknown as dynamic pressure. In contrast to shearing stresses, the\nwind pressure arises from the difference in pressure between the\nwindward and lee sides of the exposed surface. Wind pressure\nthus represents a substantial force when the wind speed is high.\nSee WIND.

The drag or tangential force of the wind on the sea is expressed\nin units of dynes per square centimeter or micronewtons per\nsquare meter but is normally taken to represent the mean drag\nover an undefined area, perhaps several kilometers square, con-\ntaining many waves. It is usually related to an appropriate time-\nand space average of the wind near the sea surface (at 10 m\nabove the mean level, for example). [F.S.]

The drag coefficient over the sea is an important quantity in\nboth meteorology and oceanography since it relates the wind\nspeed to the drag, which generates ocean waves, drives the\nocean currents, and sets the scale of the atmospheric turbulence\nthat transfers water vapor and heat from the ocean to the atmo-\nsphere to provide the energy for clouds and weather systems.\nThe drag coefficient of the sea surface depends on the wave\nfield and on the turbulent structure of the flow in the air and\nthe water. Present knowledge of the complicated fluid mech-\nanics involved is not sufficient to allow theoretical calculation of it.\nSee ATMOSPHERIC GENERAL CIRCULATION; MARITIME METEOROLOGY;\nOCEAN CIRCULATION; OCEAN WAVES.
