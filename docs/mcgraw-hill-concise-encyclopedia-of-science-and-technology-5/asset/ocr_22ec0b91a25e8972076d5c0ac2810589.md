# Chrysoberyl

A mineral having composition BeAl$_{2}$O$_{4}$ and crystallizing in the orthorhombic system. The hardness is 8.5 (Mohs scale) and the specific gravity is 3.7-3.8. The luster is vitreous and the color various shades of green, yellow, and brown. There are two gem varieties of chrysoberyl, Alexandrite, one of the most prized of gemstones, is an emerald green but in transmitted or artificial light is red. Cat's eye, or cymophane, is a green chrysoytar variety with an opalescent luster. When cut en cabochon, it is crossed by a narrow beam of light. This property results from minute tabular cavities that are arranged in parallel position. Chrysobery is a rare mineral found most commonly in pegmatite dikes and occasionally in granitic rocks and mica schists. Gem material is found in stream gravels in Ceylon and Brazil. The alexandrite variety is found in the Ural Mountains. In the United States chrysoberyl is found in pegmatites in Maine, Connecticut, and Colorado. See **BERYLUM**; **GEM**.

# Chryso-colla

A silicate mineral, composition CuSiO$_{3}$ \+ 2H$_{2}$O. Small acicular crystals have been observed, but it ordinarily occurs in impure cryptocrystalline crusts and masses with conchoidal fracture. The hardness varies from 2 to 4 on Mohs scale, and the specific gravity from 2.0 to 2.4. The luster is vitreous and it is normally green to greenish-blue, but may be brown to black when impure. Chrysocolla is a secondary mineral occurring in the oxidized zones of copper deposits, where it is associated with malachite, azurite, native copper, and cuprite. It is a minor ore of copper. See **SILICATE**.

# Chrysomonadida

An order of the class Phytamastigophorea. Chrysomonads, also known as Chrysomonadina, are usually small flagellate. They are yellow to brown because of phycoerythin in the usual one or two chromatophores, but some lack chromatophores. Many species form diagnostic siliceous cysts. Palmedoid colonies (*Hydrurus*) consist of a tough, gelatinous matrix holding many nonflagellate cells. Starch is not formed, but fats are, and the refractive carbohydrate leucosin is common. The flagella are usually two, rarely three, and are subequal. Nuclei are small. See **PHYTO**MASTIGOPHO**R**EA.

# Chry**so**pyceae

A relatively large and diverse class of algae in the chlorophyll $\alpha-\gamma$ relative line (Chromophyceae). In protozoological classification, these organisms constitute an order, Chrysomonadina, of the class Phytomastigophora. # Chrysotile

Chryosite is a fibrous mineral with a tubular morphology for each fibril. It is a member of the serpentine mineral group, as are antigorite and lizardite. Chryosite aggregates make up serpentine asbestos, which is the most important type of commercially mined asbestos. Russia and Canada are the main producing countries. Chryosite displays intersting properties such as being thermally and electrically insulating, sound insulating, chemically inert, fire-resistant, resistant to corrosive energy, absorbing, and flexible with enough tensile strength to be woven. There are hundreds of applications for chryosite including fire retarder in buildings, roofing tiles, brake pads, weaveable material for refractory clothas, filters, and fibers in fibrocement and road surfaces. See **AMEBIOS**; **SERPENTINE**; **SERPENTINE**.

Intensive inhalation of long and short asbestos fibers over a considerable time period can induce pulmonary diseases such as asbestosis and lung cancers, as well as pleural diseases such as plaques, fibrosis, and mesothelioma. Such health hazards have drastically reduced the use of chryosite, which is strictly regulated by law in western countries. See **RESPIRATORY SYSTEM DISORDERS**.

# Chytridiomycetes

A monophyletic group of true fungi in the subdivision Mastigomycotina. The zoospore has a single, posteriorly directed, whiplash-type of flagellum; a few species are polyflagellate. The Chytridiomycetes are generally regarded as primitive and are probably representative of the ancestral group for the true fungi, but they have also been classified within the Kingdom Protista and Kingdom Protoctista. The thallus may be unicellular, colonial, or filamentous and consists of a single reproductive body (mononcentric) or many reproductive bodies (polycentric). The asexual reproductive body comprises a zoosporangium that releases zoospores. The five orders are classified primarily on differences in zoospore ultrastructure and include the Chytridiales, Spizellomycetes, Blastocladiomycetes, Monoblastidiales, and Neocallimastigales. Chytrids are cosmopolitan, and are found in environments from the tundra to the equatorial rainforest, in alkaline or acid soil, peat bogs, and in fresh or brackish water. Many are saprobes, but some are parasites of microflora and fauna such as algae and rotifers. Some are of interest because they destroy mosquitoes and others are parasites of vascular plants and are of economic concern as vectors of plant viruses. The Neocallimastigales are obligate anaerobes in the rumen and digestive tract of herbivores, where they are important in the breakdown of cellulose and hemifibrous fiber. See **EUMYCOTA**; **FUNGI**; **MASTIGOMYCETINA**.

# Giconiiformes

An order of predominantly long-legged, long-necked wading birds including herons, ibises, spoonbills, storks, and their allies. Also the name of the order that previously birds in the Falconiformes. The Order Ciconiiformes is divided into four suborders and seven families: Ardeae, with the family Ardeidae (herons; 62 species); Balaenicipitae, with the family Balaenicipitidae (shoe-billed stork; 1 species); Ciconiinae, with the families Scipadidae (hammerhead; 1 species), Threskiornithidae (ibises and spoonbills; 33 species), and Ciconiidae (storks; 17 species), and Cathartinae, containing the families Teratotioniidae (Mioenne to Pleistocene of South and North America) and Catherinae (New World vultures; 7 species). The herons, shoe-billed stork, hammerhead, ibises and spoon-bills, and storks are mainly wading birds with strong legs. Living in marshes and other wet areas, they feed on fish, amphibians, and other animals, which are caught in various ways depending on the structure of the bill. Some of the storks are scavengers. Most species are colonial, nest in mixed, often huge, colonies in trees or on the ground, as well as a few on cliff ledge. All are strong fliers, usually having to cover a long distance between the nesting site and feeding areas; some are excellent soarers.

The New World vultures are placed in a separate suborder to emphasize their specialization as scavengers. These vultures are large soaring birds; the Andean condor has the largest wing span of any living land bird. Vultures locate their food from the air either by sight or by smell.
