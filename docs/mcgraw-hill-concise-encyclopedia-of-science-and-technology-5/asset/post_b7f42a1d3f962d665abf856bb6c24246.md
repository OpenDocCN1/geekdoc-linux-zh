
## 1520 Nuclide

observed abundances of some carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and fluorine nuclei are attributed to hydrogen burning in the CNO cycles. See CARBON-NITROGEN-OXYGEN CYCLES, NUCLEAR FUSION, PROTON-PROTON CHAIN.

When the hydrogen fuel is exhausted in the central region of the star, the core contracts and its temperature and density increase. Helium, the ash of hydrogen burning, cannot be burned immediately due to the larger nuclear charge of helium ($Z = 2$) requiring a much higher temperature before helium fusion can begin. When the temperature eventually exceeds about $10^{8}$ K, helium becomes the fuel for further energy generation and nucleosynthesis. The basic reaction in this thermonuclear phase is the triple-alpha process in which three ${}^{4}$He nuclei (three alpha particles) fuse to form ${}^{12}$C, a carbon nucleus of mass 12 (atomic mass units). Capture of an alpha particle by ${}^{12}$C then forms ${}^{16}$O, symbiotically written as ${}^{4}\mathrm{He}+{}^{4}\mathrm{He}\to{}^{8}\mathrm{Be}+\gamma$ or simply ${}^{12}\mathrm{C}(\alpha, \gamma){}^{16}\mathrm{O}$, where $\gamma$ represents energy released in the form of electromagnetic radiation. Other reactions that are included in helium burning are ${}^{20}\mathrm{Ne}(\alpha, \gamma){}^{24}\mathrm{Mg}$, ${}^{28}\mathrm{Si}(\alpha, \gamma){}^{32}\mathrm{S}$, ${}^{12}\mathrm{C}({}^{4}\mathrm{He}, \gamma){}^{16}\mathrm{O}$, and ${}^{28}\mathrm{Si}(\alpha, \gamma){}^{32}\mathrm{S}$. Neon-18, produced when ${}^{14}\mathrm{N}$ captures an alpha particle, is unstable and decays by emitting a positron ($e^{+}$) and a neutrino ($\nu$) to form ${}^{18}\mathrm{O}$ in short, ${}^{14}\mathrm{N}(\alpha, p){}^{18}\mathrm{O}$.

Because there is likely to be the stellar core at hydrogen burning (preceded by the carbon-nitrogen-oxygen cycles), the neutron-producing reaction ${}^{13}\mathrm{C}(\alpha, n){}^{16}\mathrm{O}$ should also be included with the helium-burning reactions. Helium burning is probably responsible for much of the ${}^{12}$C observed in the cosmic abundances, although in more massive stars the later burning stages will consume the ${}^{12}$C produced earlier by helium burning. See NUCLEAR REACTION.

Upon exhaustion of the helium supply, if the star has an initial mass of at least 8 solar masses, gravitational contraction of the stellar core can lead to a temperature exceeding $5 \times 10^{8}$ K, where it becomes possible for two ${}^{12}$C nuclei to overcome their high mutual Coulomb-repulsion barrier and fuse to form ${}^{24}\mathrm{Mg}$, ${}^{28}\mathrm{Ne}$, and ${}^{26}\mathrm{Mg}$ through reactions such as ${}^{12}\mathrm{C}({}^{12}\mathrm{C}, \gamma){}^{24}\mathrm{Mg}$ and ${}^{24}\mathrm{Mg}({}^{4}\mathrm{He}, \gamma){}^{28}\mathrm{Ne}$.

Carbon burning is followed by a short-duration stage, sometimes referred to as neon burning, in which ${}^{20}\mathrm{Ne}$ disintegrates by the reaction ${}^{20}\mathrm{Ne}(\gamma, \alpha){}^{16}\mathrm{O}$. The eventual result is that most of the carbon from helium burning becomes oxygen, which supplements the original oxygen made from helium burning. This stage is followed by the fusion of oxygen nuclei at much higher temperatures. (Temperatures greater than $10^{9}$ K are required for ${}^{16}\mathrm{O}$ nuclei to overcome their mutual Coulomb barrier). More relevant reactions for oxygen burning are ${}^{16}\mathrm{O}({}^{16}\mathrm{O}, \gamma){}^{28}\mathrm{Si}$, ${}^{16}\mathrm{O}({}^{16}\mathrm{O}, p){}^{31}\mathrm{P}$, and ${}^{16}\mathrm{O}({}^{16}\mathrm{O}, \alpha){}^{28}\mathrm{Si}$. Nuclei of mass up to $A \approx 56$ can be produced in this phase through proton, neutron, and alpha-particle captures.

Silicon burning commences when the temperature exceeds about $3 \times 10^{9}$ K. In this phase, photodisintegration of ${}^{28}\mathrm{Si}$ and other intermediate-mass nuclei around $A = 56$ produces copious supplies of protons, neutrons, and alpha particles. These particles capture on the seed nuclei left from previous burning stages and thus produce new isotopes up to mass 60, resulting in the buildup of the abundancy peak near $A = 56$.

Because neutrons are neutral particles, their capture is not affected by the Coulomb barrier that inhibits charged-particle reactions. If the number of neutrons per seed nucleus is small, so that time intervals between neutron captures are long compared to the beta-decay lifetimes of unstable nuclei that are formed, the $s$-process (slow process) takes place. The seed nuclei are proton-rich in the iron peak, and the abundances of the final nuclei are also affected by neutron-capture processing. In the $r$-process, if neutron capture produces a nucleus that is unstable (due to an excess number of neutrons), the newly formed nucleus undergoes beta decay to a stable isobar by emitting an electron and an antineutrino. A resulting nucleus eventually captures another neutron and the capture-decay step repeats. The presence of free neutrons leads to a chain of capture-decay steps that drives the abundances along a unique $s$-process path that zigzags along a single line in the nuclear $Z$-N diagram near the low of beta-stable nuclei (valley of beta stability). Given enough neutrons, the $s$-process synthesizes nuclei of mass up to 209, when alpha decay becomes a deterrent to further buildup by neutron capture. See RADIONUCIDE.

The rapid neutron capture process occurs when large numbers of neutrons are available. This process produces a large amount of heavy nuclei by neutron capture. The $r$-process occurs at temperatures exceeding $10^{9}$ K, where neutron capture times are very short (1-100 s), and the abundance of free neutrons is very large. These conditions are found deep inside the interiors of exploding massive stars, supernovae. The $r$-process can synthesize nuclei all the way into the transuranic elements.

The $p$-process produces heavier elements on the proton-rich side of the beta valley. The $p$-nuclei (about 35 are known) are blocked or formation by stable nuclei produced by either the $s$- or $r$-process. The major task for $p$-process theory is thus to find ways, other than beta decay, to process the more abundant $n$- and $s$-nuclei into the less-abundant $p$-nuclei. Two possible mechanisms are: radiative proton capture, and gamma-induced neutron, proton, or alpha-particle removal reactions. In both cases the temperature should be in excess of $2-3 \times 10^{8}$ K.

The neutrino ($\nu$) flux emitted from a cooling proto neutron star alters the (a) of explosive nucleosynthesis from type II supernovae. Inelastic scattering of neutrinos (all flavors) off abundant nuclei can decay via single or multiple nucleon emission. The $\nu$-process is probably responsible for significant contributions to the synthesis in nature of about a dozen isotopes. While the neutrino interaction cross section with matter is extremely small (about $10^{-41}\ \mathrm{cm}^{2}$), the high neutrino energies and the large neutrino flux close to the collapsing iron core of a massive star lead to significant amounts of nuclei, either directly or indirectly. See NEUTRINO.

The bulk of the light elements lithium, beryllium, and boron found in the cosmic abundance curve cannot have survived processing in stellar interiors because they are readily destroyed by proton capture. Although some ${}^{7}\mathrm{Li}$ originated in the big bang, primordial nucleosynthesis cannot be responsible for the bulk of the ${}^{7}\mathrm{Li}$ in existence. Spallation of more abundant nuclei such as carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen by high-energy cosmic rays and the $^{3}\mathrm{He}$, $^{4}\mathrm{He}$, $^{7}\mathrm{Li}$, $^{11}\mathrm{B}$, and some ${}^{7}\mathrm{Li}$. The canonical process is spallation of carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen nuclei in the interstellar medium by fast light particles, such as alpha particles and protons that are abundant in the gas between the stars. These high-energy cosmic rays are referred to as cosmic rays, leading to the term "cosmic-ray spallation" (CRS) process. See COSMIC RAYS.

## Nucleotide

A cellular constituent that is one of the building blocks of ribonucleic acids (RNA) and deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA). In biological systems, nucleotides are linked by enzymes in order to make long, chainlike polymolecules of defined sequences. The order or sequence of the nucleotide units along a polynucleotide chain plays an important role in the storage and transfer of genetic information. Many nucleotides also perform other important functions in biological systems. Some, such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP), serve as energy sources that are used to fuel important biological reactions. Others, such as nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) and coenzyme A (CoA), are important cofactors that are needed to complete a variety of enzymatic reactions. Cyclic nucleotides such as cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) are often used to regulate complex metabolic systems.
