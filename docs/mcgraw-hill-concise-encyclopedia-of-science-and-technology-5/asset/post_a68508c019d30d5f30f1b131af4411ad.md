The determination of the chemical composition of an archaeological find is not an end in itself, but provides the archaeologist with factual evidence not otherwise available. Chemical dating on many aspects of early human life. The changing elemental composition of coins detects progressive debasement and reveals economic history and fiscal policy. The metals added to copper to make bronze and brass outline the history and spread of technology. The foodstuffs consumed are indicators of the advent and progress of agriculture and animal husbandry. Together, all these paint a picture of prehistoric social, cultural, and economic stratification. The composition of an object also often clues to its geographic origin, which may be far from the excavation site. This provides evidence of trade and exchange in commodities and raw materials.

While the most widely used methods for dating archaeological material—radioactive decay, thermoluminescence, and archaeomagnetism—deal with physical processes, three depend on the progress of conventional chemical reactions.
- (1) Amino acid dating uses the rate of racemization of optically active organic molecules.
- (2) Hydration dating measures the thickness of the weathering layer produced by the action of water on natural and artificial glass, including obsidian and flint.
- (3) NPU dating of bone relies on the loss of nitrogen (N) from the organic collagen compound and on the gain of fluoride (F) and uranium (U) by the inorganic hydroxyapatite component.

Like all nonnuclear chemical reactions, these changes are a function not only of time but also of temperature, of acidity and, in the case of fluoride and uranium uptake, of the concentrations of these elements in the surrounding soil. Chemical methods cannot produce absolute dates unless these various variables are known or can be estimated reasonably closely. They are, however, useful in establishing relative ages of finds within a single site in which the depositional characteristics are likely to have been uniform.

## Archeological chronology

The establishment of the temporal sequence of human cultures. Prior to the discovery of nuclear and chemical dating methods, which provide an absolute time scale, archaeologists used stratigraphy, lithic and ceramic typology, seriation, index fossils, and a limited range of chemical techniques to establish relative chronologies for cultural remains.

The major chemical techniques used in dating of bones in relative sequence have been labeled FU-N (for fluorine-uranium-nitrogen). In a single site or environment, bones of the same age usually absorb the same amount of fluoride and uranium while losing the same amount of nitrogen.

Since the mid-twentieth century numerous absolute (sometimes called chronometric) dating techniques have been devised by natural scientists. Some of these techniques can give results in calendar years, whereas others yield dates that are expressed in years but which cannot always be correlated precisely with the calendar. The major methods for absolute dating of archaeological materials used include radiocarbon dating, dendrochronology, thermoluminescence dating, hydration dating, racemization, potassium-argon dating, lead-210 dating, and archaeomagnetism. Many other techniques, for example, fission track dating, have been used to establish archaeological chronology, and a host of physical and, to a lesser degree, chemical dating methods have been investigated.

## Archeology

The scientific study of past material culture. The initial objective of archaeology is to construct cultural chronologies, attempting to order past material culture into meaningful temporal segments. The intermediate objective is to breathe life into these chronologies by reconstructing past lifeways. The ultimate objective of contemporary archaeology is to determine the cultural processes that underlie human behavior, both past and present.

The material culture of the past is of infinite variety. The scientific study of this evidence is such a broad task that there is no such thing as any single archaeological method, although over the past century archaeologists have evolved what can be termed an overall archaeological approach. By constant confirmation, the archaeologist often attempts to establish synchronism with what has already been established historically.

Archaeologists use a normative order of types in order to categorize similar artifacts. Most common is the temporal type, a principle similar to the index fossil concept used by the geologist. A temporal type can be any kind of archaeological artifact or feature, but ideally it is some object of common use in which the form is subject to change, due to either the whim of fashion or technological improvement. One example is the simple flint arrowhead with side notches on the central part. It is typical of the British Bronze Age and was not in fashion earlier or later. Ceramic types have been established by archaeologists working around the world, and a thoroughly tested ceramic chronology is invaluable as a temporal ordering device, no matter where the archaeologist is working.

The nature of the artifact employed as a temporal type is irrelevant, and its use may or may not be known. Archaeologists also establish other kinds of types: functional types (in this case on the basis of known or presumed functions). Technological types, divisions which reflect the mode of manufacture, are particularly helpful when studying stone tool manufacture.

The concept of culture is used in two different ways by contemporary archaeologists. When dealing with cultural chronologies, the archaeologist most commonly uses a modal or shared view of culture. It is this normative collection of shared ideas which causes artifacts to change in systematic ways through time, and temporal types can be established on the basis of this shared culture. When attempting to reconstruct lifeways, however, the archaeologist can no longer rely on the shared aspects of culture. When transcending temporal associations, contemporary archaeologists tend to view culture systematically, as people's ex-ratemodel (that is, learned) method of dealing with the social and cultural environment.

The principles of stratigraphy are applied to archaeology in terms of the law of superposition, which states that, all else being equal, older deposits will tend to be buried beneath younger ones. More stratigraphic equivalence, however, does not necessarily indicate contemporaneity, as there can be mixing and mixtures of successive occupational debris on one surface. Archaeologists must therefore study the processes of cultural deposition in order to recognize the difference between intact and disturbed strata.

Contemporary excavation must be conducted with a plan, a firm research design that attempts to provide answers to definite questions. Archaeology is one of the few sciences which destroys its own data in the process of generating them. Archaeologists must therefore be extremely careful to make the appropriate observations at the time of excavation.

The task of deciphering meaning from past material culture is so complex that the archaeologist is often required to borrow from allied disciplines in the physical and natural sciences, including geology, climatology, paleontology, mineralogy, physics, chemistry, and anthropology. The archaeologist must have some understanding of all these sciences to extract from sites and materials every possible piece of information which may lead to a better understanding of prehistory.
