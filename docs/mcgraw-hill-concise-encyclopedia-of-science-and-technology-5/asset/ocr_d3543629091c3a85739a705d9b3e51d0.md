In the superfamily Nepidoidea, the Nepidae, or water scorpions, have a long breathing tube at the tip of the abdomen, through which they obtain air directly from the surface. The beak is short and stout to suck the juices of other insects on which they prey. Belostomatidae, or electric-light bugs, have short, strap-like respiratory appendages at the tip of the abdomen. Giant water bugs of the genus *Lethocerus* are pests in fish ponds where they attack fry. They can inflict a painful bite when handled carelessly.

The superfamily Notonectioidea includes the Notonectidae or backswimmers: they swim ventral side upmost, the hindlegs serving as oars. Breathing is facilitated by an air bubble, obtained by touching the tip of the abdomen to the surface.

The minute bugs of the families Pleidae and Heteroptidae (superfamily Pleyoidea) are suboval, with legs not fitted for rowing.

The creeping water bugs (superfamily Naucoroidae) are sometimes separated from the Naucoridae and Aphelocheiridae. They are suboval in body form. Respiration is either by an air bubble or by means of a glans. In the Old World genus *Aphelocheirus*, the prosternum consists of an ultramicroscopic hair pile which acts as a physical gill; the bug is thus able to remain submerged permanently.
