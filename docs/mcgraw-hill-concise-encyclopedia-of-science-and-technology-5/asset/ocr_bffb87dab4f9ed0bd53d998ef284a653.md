Smog is an episodic phenomenon because specific meteorological conditions are required for it to accumulate near the ground. These conditions include calm or stagnant winds which limit the horizontal transport of the pollutants from their sources, and a temperature inversion which prevents vertical mixing of the pollutants from the boundary layer into the free troposphere.

Smog can be classified into three types: London smog, photochemical smog, and smog from biomass burning.

London smog arises from the by-products of coal burning. These by-products include soot particles and sulfur oxides. During coal damp periods (often in the winter), the soot and sulfur oxides can combine with fog droplets to form a dark acidic fog. As nations switch from coal to cleaner-burning fossil fuels such as oil and gas as well as alternative energy sources such as hy-droelectric and nuclear, London smogs cease. See *ACID RAIN*; *COAL*.

Photochemical smog is a more of a haze than a fog and is produced by chemical reactions in the atmosphere that are triggered by sunlight. A. J. Hagen-Smit first unraveled the chemical mechanism that produces photochemical smog. He irradiated mixtures of volatile organic compounds (VOC) and nitrogen oxides (NO<sub>x</sub>) in a reaction chamber. After a few hours, Hagen-Smit observed the appearance of cracks in rubber bands stretched across the chamber. Knowing that ozone (O<sub>3</sub>) can harden and crack rubber, Hagen-Smit correctly reasoned that photochemical smog was caused by photochemical reactions involving VOC and NO<sub>x</sub>, and that one of the major oxidants produced in this smog was O<sub>3</sub>. See *ATMOSPHERIC CHEMISTRY*; *OZONE*; *PHOTO-CHEMISTRY*.

While generally not as dangerous as London smog, photochemical smog contains a number of noxious constituents. Ozone, a strong oxidant that can react with living tissue, is one of these noxious compounds. Another is peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN), an eye irritant that is produced by reactions between NO<sub>2</sub> and the breakdown products of carbonyls. Particulate matter having diameters of about 10 micrometers or less are of concern because they can penetrate to the human respiratory tract during breathing and have been implicated in a variety of respiratory ailments.

Probably the oldest type of smog known to humankind is produced from the burning of biomass or wood. It combines aspects of both London smog and photochemical smog since the burning of biomass can produce copious quantities of smoke as well as VOC and NO<sub>x</sub>.   [W.L.Ch.]

# ## Smoke

Fumes and smoke are dispersions of finely divided solids or liquids in a gaseous medium. The particle-size range is 0.01–5.0 micrometers. Typical dispersions are smokes from incomplete combustion of organic matter such as tobacco, wood, and coal; soot or carbon black; oil-vapor mists; chemical fumes such as sulfur trioxide (SO<sub>3</sub>) and phosphorus pentoxide (P<sub>2</sub>O<sub>5</sub>) mists, ammonium chloride (NH<sub>4</sub>Cl), and metal oxides; and the products of hydrolysis of metal chlorides by moist air. Oil-vapor and P<sub>2</sub>O<sub>5</sub> mists (formed by burning phosphorus in moist air) have been extensively used in military polling operations to produce screening smoke. See *AIR POLLUTION*.   [H.H.S., A.J.]

# ## Smut (microbiology)

The dark powdery masses of “smut spores” (teliospores) that develop in living plant tissues infected by species of *Ustilago*, *Tilletia*, and similar plant parasitic fungi. Molecular and ultrastuctural data show that smut fungi comprise two phylogenetically distinct taxa within the Basidiomycota; recent classification places them in two different classes, Ustilaginomycetes and Urediniomycetes. Ustilagino-mycetes contains most of the smut fungi, and several groups of morphologically distinct, nonsmut plant parasites, including *Exobasidium*, *Graphiola*, and *Microstroma*. Smut fungi belong-

ing to the genus *Microbryum*, best known as anthner smuts of Caryophyllaceae, are now placed within the Urediniomycetes with rust fungi and allied taxa. Ustilaginomycetes and Tilo-diniomycetes also contain a number of saprotrophic, yeastlike fungi that are related to the plant parasitic smuts. Yeastlike saprotrophs that produce teliospores include *Tilletaria* in the Ustilaginomycetes, and *Leucosporidium*, *Rhodosporidium*, and *Sporobolomyces* in the Urediniomycetes. Yeastlike saprotrophs in the Ustilaginomycetes that do not form teliospores but reproduce in an another manner similar to *Ustilago* and *Tilletia* include *Pseudomyces* and *Tilletopsis*, respectively. See *FUNGI*.

Teliospores of plant parasitic smut fungi form in a fruiting structure called a sorus. Sorii are commonly produced in the inflo-rescence, leaves, or stems of the host, although the root is the site of sorus formation in smuts belonging to the genus *Entirhiza*. Teliospores develop within the sorus by conversion of dikaryotic mycelial cells to thick-walled resistant spores within which paired nuclei fuse. Meiosis also occurs in teliospores of some smut fungi, but meiotic division is more characteristic of the tubular basidium that develops at germination.

There are 1200 species and 50 different genera of known smut fungi that infect over 4000 species of angiosperms. Smut fungi occur on both monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous hosts but are most economically important as pathogens of barley, corn, oats, onions, rice, sorghum, sugarcane, and wheat. Control of smut diseases varies with species and includes fungicial seed treatments and use of resistant crop varieties. See *BASIDIO-COTA*; *PLANT PATHOLOGY*.   [J.Ba., L.M.Ca.]

# ## Snail

Any of the approximately 74,000 species in the class Gastropoda of the phylum Mollusca or, alternatively, any of the 12 or so species of land pulmonate gastropods used as human food.
