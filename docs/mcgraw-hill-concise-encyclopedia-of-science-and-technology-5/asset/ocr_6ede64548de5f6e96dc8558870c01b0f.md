The second way in which inorganic nitrogen is made available to biological agents is by nitrogen fixation (this term is maintained even though $\mathrm{N}_2$ is now called dinitrogen), a process in which $\mathrm{N}_2$ is reduced to $\mathrm{NH}_3$. Since the major majority of nitrogen is in the form of $\mathrm{N}_2$, nitrogen fixation obviously is essential to life.

The fixing process is confined to prokaryotes (certain photosynthetic and nonphotosynthetic bacteria). The major nitrogen fixers (called diazotrophs) are members of the genus *Rhizobium*, bacteria that are found in root nodules of leguminous plants. And the cyanobacteria (originally called blue-green algae). See **NITROGEN FIXATION**.

+   1. **Nitrogen fixation** The chemical or biological conversion of atmospheric nitrogen ($\mathrm{N}_2$) into compounds that can be used by plants, and thus become available to animals and humans.
+   2. In the 1990s, chemical and biological processes together contributed about 260 million tons (230 million metric tons) of fixed nitrogen per year globally. Industrial production of nitrogen fertilizer accounted for about 85 million tons (80 million metric tons) of nitrogen per year, while spontaneous chemical processes, such as lightning, ultraviolet irradiation, and combustion, leading to the synthesis of nitric oxide from $\mathrm{O}_2$ and $\mathrm{N}_2$, may have accounted for 44 million tons (40 million metric tons) per year. The remainder, roughly half of the global input of newly fixed nitrogen, arose from biological processes. World agriculture, which is very dependent on nitrogen fixation, is increasingly reliant on chemical nitrogen sources. See **NITROGEN**.
+   3. **Chemical fixation** Three chemical processes for fixing atmospheric nitrogen have been developed. All require accessible thermal or electrical energy and yield different products.
+   4. In processes, which are now rarely used, it is passed through an electric arc and about 1% nitric oxide is formed, which can be chemically converted to nitrate. In the cyanamide process, which is now obsolete, heated calcium carbide in nitrogen generates calcium cyanamide, which when moistened hydrolyzes to urea and ammonia. In the widely used Haber process, hydrogen (generated by heating natural gas) is mixed with nitrogen (from air), and turned to yield a nitrogen-hydrogen mixture. The nitrogen-hydrogen mixture is compressed (10–80 megapascals) and heated ($200-700^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to $390-1300^\circ\mathrm{F}$) in the presence of a metal oxide catalyst to give ammonia. The Haber process is the major source of ammonia used for fertilizers. See **AMMONIA; CYANAMIDE; ELECTROCHEMICAL PROCESS; FERTILIZER; HIGH-PRESSURE PROCESSES**.
+   5. **Biological fixation** Only prokaryotes—bacteria, archaea, and cyanobacteria (earlier called blue-green algae)—fix nitrogen. Nitrogen-fixing microbes, called diazotrophs, fall into two main groups, free-living and symbiotic. See **ARCHAEAEBACTERIA; CYANOBACTERIA; PHOVATE**.
+   6. The free-living diazotrophs are subaerial. Aerobic diazotrophs, of which there are over 50 genera, including *Azotobacter*, methane-oxidizing bacteria, and cyanobacteria, require oxygen for growth and fix nitrogen when oxygen is present. Azotobacter and related bacteria, and some cyanobacteria fix nitrogen in ordinary air, but most members of this group fix nitrogen only when the oxygen concentration is low. Free-living diazotrophs, which fix nitrogen only when oxygen is absent or vanishingly low, are widespread. The genera *Bacillus* and *Klebsiella* include many strains of this type, and representatives of symbiotic diazotrophs behave in this way as well. See **ALGAE; BACTERIAL PHYSIOLOGY AND METABOLISM**.
+   7. The best-known symbiotic bacteria belong to the genus *Rhizobium*. Species of *Rhizobium*, or related genera, such as *Bradyrhizobium* and *Sinorhizobium*, colonize the roots of leguminous plants and stimulate the formation of root nodules within which they fix nitrogen microaerobically. Both nodules and bacteria show specificity; for example, certain types of plants require special strains of *rhizobium*. Some types of *rhizobium*, such as *Bradyrhizobium*, can fix nitrogen in the absence of plant tissue, but require low oxygen, though most *rhizobia* fix nitrogen only within the nodules. See **SOIL MICROBIOLOGY**.

The enzymes responsible for nitrogen fixation are called nitrogenases. The most common nitrogenase consists of two proteins, one large containing molybdenum, iron, and inorganic sulfur (the MoFe-protein or dinitrogenase), the other smaller containing iron and inorganic sulfur (the Fe-protein or dinitrogenase reductase). Nitrogenase reduces one molecule of $\mathrm{N}_2$ to two of ammonia ($\mathrm{NH}_3$), a reaction which is accompanied by the conversion of 16 molecules of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and the release of one molecule of $\mathrm{H}_2$ as a by-product. Nitrogenase is irreversibly destroyed by air, so all aerobic diazotrophs have developed means of restricting access of oxygen to the active enzyme.

+   1. **Nitrogen oxides** Chemical compounds of nitrogen and oxygen. Nitrogen and oxygen do not combine when mixed directly (as in air), but they do combine during chemical reactions of compounds containing them. A number of nitrogen oxides can be isolated which differ from one another in the numbers of nitrogen and oxygen atoms present in each molecule. The table gives data for the five nitrogen oxides which are well established.
+   2. **Nitrous oxide and nitric oxide**. When inhaled, nitrous oxide has anesthetic effects; in small amounts it produces mild hysteria, and hence is sometimes called laughing gas. It is colorless, the least reactive of the oxides, and dissolves in water without chemical reaction. Some nitric oxide is formed in an electric arc, as in the technical production of nitric acid.
+   3. **NITROGEN OXIDE**.
+   4. **Nitric oxide** is colorless and soluble in water without reaction. It is an important messenger molecule in animals. It is one of the few "odd" molecules which contain an odd number of electrons. As an odd molecule, it has the ability to lose or gain one electron, thus giving the electrically charged ions $\mathrm{NO}^{+}$ and $\mathrm{NO}^{-}$. The important nitrosyl compounds contain these ions.
+   5. **Trioxides**.
+   6. **Dinitrogen trioxide** exists purely in the solid state. It is the anhydride of nitrous acid, which when the oxide is dissolved in an alkaline solution, nitrite ion is produced.
+   7. **Dioxide and tetraoxide**. The position of the equilibrium between nitrogen dioxide and dinitrogen tetraoxide depends upon temperature and physical state. Dinitrogen tetraoxide reacts readily with water to give an equilibrium mixture of nitrous and nitric acids. As temperature is raised, the nitrous acid decomposes to nitric acid and nitric oxide. These reactions are important in the technical production of nitric acid by catalytic oxidation of ammonia. Dinitrogen tetraoxide is an oxidizing agent comparable in strength to bromine, and is employed as such in the lead-chamber process for sulfuric acid. In organic chemistry the tetraoxide finds use as a special oxidizing agent (for example, in the production of sulfonides and phosphine oxides), and as a nitrating agent.
