# Appendix 2471

Ecclês, John Carew (1903–1997). Australian physiologist. Elucidated the action of nerve impulses across zones of close contact between nerve cells. Nobel Prize, 1963.

Eddington, Arthur Stanley (1882–1944). English astronomer and writer. Theoretical research on stellar movements and internal makeup of stars. Wrote on theory of relativity.

Edelman, Gerald Maurice (1929– ), American biochemist. Worked to determine chemical structure of immunoglobulins. Nobel Prize, 1972.

Edison, Thomas Alva (1847–1931). American electrician and inventor. Invented the gramophone, carbon transmitter for the telephone, in incandescent electric lamp, modern motion pictures, and the duplex system of telegraphy.

Ehrenfest, Paul (1880–1933). Austrian-born Dutch theoretical physicist. Made fundamental contributions to physics. Developed mechanics and quantum mechanics; developed Ehrenfest’s principle; proved Ehrenfest’s theorem.

Ehrlich, Paul (1854–1915). German bacteriologist. Research in chemotherapy, notably the discovery of Salvarsan for treatment of syphilis. Pioneer in the study of hematology and immunity. Nobel Prize, 1908.

Egen, Manfred (1927– ). German chemist. Developed chemical reaction techniques to study high-speed structural changes. Nobel Prize, 1967.

Eijkman, Christiaan (1858–1930). Dutch physician. Studied dietary deficiency diseases, in particular beriberi. Nobel Prize, 1929.

Einstein, Albert (1879–1955). German-born American physicist. Developed theory of relativity; extended the application of quantum theory. Nobel Prize, 1921.

Einthoven, Willem (1860–1927). Dutch physiologist born in Java. Used the string galvanometer to record electrical activity of the heart, thereby inventing the electrocardiograph. Nobel Prize, 1924.

Elion, Gertrude Belle (1918–1999). American biochemist. With G. H. Hitchings, pioneered research on the development of drugs for the treatment of leukaemia, malaria, gout, herpes, bacterial and fungal infections, and autoimmune diseases and organ-transplant rejection. Nobel Prize, 1988.

Elasser, Walter Maurice (1904–1991). German-American geophysicist. Formulated the dynamic theory of the Earth’s permanent terrestrial magnetic field.

Elsater, Johann Philip Ludwig Julius (1854–1920). German astrophysicist. Wrote on meteorology; studied atmospheric electricity, radioactivity, and electrophotography; invented the photoelectric cell.

Emmett, Paul Hugh (1900–1985). American chemist. Worked on catalysts for ammonia synthesis and the water-gas conversion reaction. With R. Brunner and R. Teller, formulated Brunauer-Emmett-Teller equation for surface area determination.

Encke, Johann Franz (1791–1865). German astronomer. Determined period of revolution of Encke’s comet; discovered by J. L. Pons, measured the distance of the Sun from the Earth.

Enders, John Franklin (1897–1985). American microbiologist. With F. C. Robbins and T. H. Weller, discovered the capacity of poliomyelitis virus to grow in various tissue cultures. Nobel Prize, 1954.

Ensōkō, David (1884–1947). Swedish physicist. With S. Chapmann, developed the Chapman-Enskog theory for solving the Boltzmann transport equation.

Eötvös, Roland, Baron (1848–1919). Hungarian physicist. Research on gravitation and terrestrial magnetism; formulated a law which relates surface tension to temperature. Made observations of the Eötvös torsion balance.

Erastothenes (3rd century b.c.). Greek physician and anatomist. Founder of the medical school of Alexandria. Discovered between the cerebrum and cerebellum, and sensory and motor nerves.

Erathostenes (3d century b.c.). Greek astronomer. Suggested an extra day in the calendar every fourth year; made a determination of the size of the Earth; measured obliquity of the ecliptic.

Erdös, Paul (1913–1996). Hungarian mathematician. Did wide-ranging work in algebra, analysis, combinatorial theory, geometry, topology, number theory, and graph theory. With A. Selberg, gave an elementary proof of the prime number theorem.

Erlander, Joseph (1874–1965). American physiologist. With H. S. Gasser, created new methods for amplifying and recording electrical impulses in nerves. Research on the function of the sympathetic nerves. Nobel Prize, 1944.

Erlenmeyer, Richard August Carl Emil (1825–1909). German organic chemist. Research on synthesis and constitution of aliphatic compounds; studied modern organic chemistry; invented Erlenmeyer flask.

Ernst, Richard F. (1933– ). Swiss chemist. Developed soft desorption ionization method for mass spectrometry, a new method for studying proteins. Invented NMR spectroscopy from a tool with a narrow application to a key analytical technique in chemistry as well as many other fields. Nobel Prize, 1991.

Esaki, Leo (1925– ). Japanese physicist. Discovered a new negative-resistance characteristic in semiconductor pn junctions, leading to the discovery of the tunnel, or Esaki, diode. Nobel Prize, 1973.

Euclid (ca. 330–ca. 275 b.c.). Greek mathematician. Wrote geometry texts; Euclidean geometry is named after him.

Eudoxus of Cnidus (ca. 408-ca. 355 b.c.). Greek astronomer and mathematician. Assumed the theory of motion of planets based on homocentric, confocal spheres. Developed theory of proportions and methods of measuring areas and volumes of geometric figures.

Euler, Leonhard (1707–1783). Swiss mathematician. Contributed to algebraic series and differential and integral calculus; realized the significance of cosinus. (Euler numbers) of certain trigonometrical expansions.

Euler–Chelpin, Hans Karl August Simon von (1875–1964). German-Swedish chemist. Research on enzyme action and fermentation of sugars. Nobel Prize, 1929.

Ewald, Paul Peter (1886–1955). German-born American physicist. Developed dynamic theory of X-ray interference in crystals.

Ewing, William Maurice (1906–1974). American geophysicist. Made fundamental contributions to seismology, geodesy, oceanography, and submergence.

Eyting, Henry (1901–1981). Mexican-born American chemist. Pioneered in the application of quantum mechanicals to chemistry; conceived the theory of absolute reaction rates and the significant structures theory of liquids.

Fabricius, Hieronymus, or Girolamo Fabrizio (ca. 1533–1619). Italian anatomist. Made painstaking descriptions of valves in veins; did comparative anatomy in animal embryology.

Fabry, Charles (1867–1945). French physicist. With A. Perot, invented Fabry-Pérot interferometer. Developed improved Doppler broadening and Doppler effect.

Fahrenheint, Gabriel Daniel (1686–1736). German physician. Constructed thermometers; invented Fahrenheit temperature scale.

Faloppio, Gabriele (1523–1562). Italian physician. Discovered Fallopian tubes; gave first clear description of organs of inner and middle ear.

Fattings, Gerde (1954– ). German mathematician. United methods of analytic algebraic geometry to prove the Gordan conjecture, which states that there are only finitely many rational points on curves whose genus is greater than 1; this was a step toward the later proof of Fermat’s last theorem by A. Wiles. Fields Medal, 1996.

Faraday, John Thomas (1837–1911). American civil engineer. Designed water works and water supply systems.

Faraday, Michael (1791–1867). English chemist and physicist. Discovered electrochemical induction; formulated two laws of electrolysis; invented the dynamo.

Fechner, Gustav Theodor (1801–1887). German psychologist. Formulated Fechner’s law; developed the Fechner law concerning the invariance of sensation produced by a stimulus.

Fermi, Charles Eugene (1904– ). American mathematician. Worked in Fourier analysis, partial differential equations, and the theory of functions of several complex variables; discovered the ideal form of the Hardy space H²; Fields Medal, 1978.

Feit, Walter (1930–2004). Austrian-born American mathematician. Worked in group theory. With J. G. Thompson, proved one of the simple groups classification theorem in terms of simple finite groups having the prime order groups.

Fern, John B. (1817– ). American chemist. Developed soft desorption ionization method for mass spectrometric analysis of biological molecules; worked on the chemistry of proteins and carbohydrates. Nobel Prize, 2002.

Fermi, Enrico (1901–1954). Italian-born American physicist. Pioneer in physics. Developed nuclear fission; discovered the inverse beta process in a neutron source; studied the neutron capture of the slow neutrons; led to the discovery of the first artificial gold (1938). Nobel Prize, 1938.

Feynman, Richard Phillips (1918–1988). American physicist. Worked on the application of group theory to problems of physics and mathematics. Nobel Prize, 1965.

Fibiger, Johannes Andreas (1867–1928). Danish pathologist. First to produce cancer experimentally. With N. Ryberg (1860–1904), Danish physiologist, developed the first light for certain diseases. Nobel Prize, 1912.

Fischer, Erhard H. (1920– ). American biochemist. With E. G. Krebs, discovered the phosphofructokinase, a class of enzyme that transfer phosphate groups from one molecule to another. Nobel Prize, 1992.

Fischer, Emil Hermann (1852–1919). German chemist. With E. Fischer, studied natural substances. With O. Fischer, studied the nucleotide of natural products. Nobel Prize, 1903.

Fischer, Ernst Otto (1918– ). German chemist. With E. Krebs, discovered the phosphofructokinase. Nobel Prize, 1992.

Fischer, Hans (1881–1945). German organic chemist. Investigated and synthesized pyrethroid compounds. With G. Stedman, discovered the principle of organic. Nobel Prize, 1930.

Fisher, Ronald Aylmer (1890–1962). English geneticist and statistician. Developed statistical methods for analysis of variance. With G. A. Fisher, developed the theory of dominance.

Fitch, Val Logson (1932– ). American physiologist. Developed a method to determine the structure of a DNA sequence. The work was done on the evolution of dominance. Nobel Prize, 1980.

Fitzgerald, George Francis (1851–1901). Irish physicist. Worked on the Fitzgerald-Lorentz contraction, which is a key principle in special relativity. Nobel Prize, 1901.

Fizeau, Armand Hippolyte Louis (1819–1896). French physicist. Studied to accurately measure the velocity of light. Conducted experiments on the velocity of electricity; used light navigation to measure the length of the Earth’s circumference. Nobel Prize, 1851.

Florence, John (1821–1719). English anatomist. Made a truthful catalog of stars; invented conical projection in mapmaking.

Florey, Alexander (1898–1955). British bacteriologist. Discovered the properties of penicillin; Nobel Prize, 1945.

Flux, John Ambrose (1849–1945). English electrical engineer. Invented the thermionic valve; contributed to wide application of electric lighting and heating.
