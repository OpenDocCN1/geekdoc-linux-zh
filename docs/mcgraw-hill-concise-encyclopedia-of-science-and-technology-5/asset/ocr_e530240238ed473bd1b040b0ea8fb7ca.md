# Siderophores Low-molecular-mass molecules that have a high specificity for chelating or binding iron. Siderophores are produced by many microorganisms, including bacteria, yeast, and fungi, to obtain iron from the environment. More than 500 different siderophores have been identified from microorganisms. Some bacteria produce more than one type of siderophore. See BACTERIA; BIOORGANIC CHEMISTRY; CHELATION; FUNGI; IRON; YEAST.

Iron is required by aerobic bacteria and other living organisms for a variety of biochemical reactions in the cell. Although iron is the fourth most abundant element in the Earth’s crust, it is not readily available to bacteria. Iron is found in nature mostly as insoluble precipitates that are part of hydroxide polymers. Bacteria living in the soil or water must have a mechanism to solubilize iron from these precipitates in order to assimilate iron from the environment. Iron is also not freely available in humans and other mammals. Most iron is found intracellularly in heme proteins and ferritin, an iron storage compound. Iron in blood cells is tightly bound to proteins. If siderophores in the environment grow in human tissue, such as the blood or gut, they can interfere with the host’s iron metabolism. For this reason, some siderophores have the ability to sequester iron from the host, which can be toxic to the host. This is known as the “iron robbery” hypothesis. The ability of siderophores to steal iron from the host can be a virulence factor in some pathogens. For example, Yersinia enterocolitica produces the siderophore yersiniabactin, which can inhibit the growth of other bacteria by depriving them of iron. Similarly, Staphylococcus aureus produces staphylokinase, which can sequester iron from the host and inhibit the growth of competing bacteria. In addition, siderophores can also serve as signals for bacterial communication and can modulate the expression of other virulence factors. For example, the siderophore aerobactin in Aeromonas hydrophila can activate the two-component system GacS/GacA, which regulates the expression of several virulence factors, including the adhesive fimbriae and the protease Aerolysin. Thus, siderophores are not only important for iron acquisition, but also play a role in bacterial pathogenesis and regulation.

The many different types of siderophores can generally be classified into two structural classes, hydroxamate-type and carboxylate-type siderophores. In addition, there are a few siderophores that contain both hydroxamate and carboxylate groups. The siderophores that contain carboxylate groups are usually larger and more complex than the hydroxamate-type siderophores. The carboxylate-type siderophores are mainly produced by fungi, while the hydroxamate-type siderophores are produced by both bacteria and fungi. The most common siderophore is enterobactin, a triscatecholate siderophore produced by E. coli and other Enterobacteriaceae. Enterobactin has a very high affinity for iron, with a dissociation constant of about $10^{-35}$ M. Another important siderophore is ferrichrome, a trihydroxamate siderophore produced by many fungi, including Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Fusarium. Ferrichrome has a dissociation constant of about $10^{-25}$ M. Siderophores are usually small molecules with a molecular weight of less than 1000 Da. They are highly specific for iron(III) and have a high affinity for this ion. The iron(III)-siderophore complex is usually stable in solution, which allows the siderophore to transport iron across cell membranes. Once inside the cell, the iron is released from the siderophore and used in various biochemical reactions. The siderophore is then recycled and can be used again to transport iron into the cell.

Siderophores have potential applications in the treatment of some human diseases and infections. Some siderophores are used therapeutically to treat chronic or acute iron overload conditions in order to prevent iron toxicity in humans. Individuals who have defects in blood cell production or who receive multiple transfusions can sometimes have too much free iron in the body. However, in order to prevent infection during treatment for iron overload, it is important to use siderophores that cannot be used by bacterial pathogens. A second clinical application of siderophores is in antibiotic delivery to bacteria. Some gram-negative bacteria are resistant to antibiotics because they are too big to diffuse through the outer-membrane porins. However, siderophone-antibiotic combination compounds have been synthesized that can be transported into the cell using the siderophone receptor. See ANTI-OTIC; DRUG RESISTANCE.

# Sigma-delta converter A class of electronic systems containing both analog and digital subsystems whose most common application is to the conversion of analog signals to digital form and vice versa. The device is also known as a delta-sigma converter. The main advantage of the sigma-delta approach to signal conversion is its minimal reliance on the quality of the analog components required. To achieve this end, the system uses pulse density modulation to create a high-rate stream of single-amplitude pulses. For analog-to-digital conversion, the rate at which the pulses are generated depends on the amplitude of the analog voltage being sensed. For digital-to-analog conversion, the pulse density depends on the numeric digital quantity applied at the converter input. See PULSE MODULATION.

The digital circuitry can be implemented using logic gates or microprocessors. The entire system can be implemented on a single chip or on a board with separate analog and digital components.

Conversely, in a digital-to-analog converter, a digital circuit is used to convert numeric values from a digital processor to a pulse stream, and these pulses are then low-pass filtered by a relatively simple analog system to produce an analog waveform. In each case, analog information is contained not in the pulse amplitude but in the number of pulses that occur during the conversion interval. This distribution of the analog information makes the conversion process essentially independent of the amplitude of the pulses and greatly simplifies the design and fabrication of the analog portion of the converter. # Signal detection theory A theory in psychology that characterizes not only the acuity of an individual’s discrimination but also the psychological factors that bias the individual’s judgments. Failure to separate these two aspects of discrimination has tempered the success of theories based upon the classical concept of a sensory threshold. The theory provides a modern and more complete account of the process whereby an individual makes fine discriminations.

The theory of signal detection has two parts of quite different origins. The first comes from mathematical statistics and is a translation of the theory of statistical decisions. The major contribution of this part of the theory is that it permits a determination of the individual’s discriminative capacity, or sensitivity, that is independent of the judgmental bias or decision criterion the individual may have used when the discrimination was made. The second part of the theory comes from the study of electronic communications. It provides a means of calculating for simple signals, such as tones and lights, the best discrimination that can be attained. The prediction is based upon physical measurement of the signals and their interfering noise.

This opportunity to compare the sensitivity of human observers with the sensitivity of an “ideal observer” for a variety of signals is of considerable usefulness, and of growing interest, in sensory psychophysiology. Signal detection theory has been applied to several topics in experimental psychology in which separation of intrinsic discriminability from decision factors is desirable. Included are attention, imagery, learning, conceptual judgment, personality, reaction time, manual control, and speech. The analytical apparatus of the theory has been of value in the evaluation of the performance of systems that make decisions based on uncertain information. Such systems may involve only people, or people and machines together, or only machines. Examples come from medical diagnosis, where clinicians may base diagnostic decisions on a physical examination, or on a ray image, or where machines make diagnoses, perhaps by counting blood cells of various types.
