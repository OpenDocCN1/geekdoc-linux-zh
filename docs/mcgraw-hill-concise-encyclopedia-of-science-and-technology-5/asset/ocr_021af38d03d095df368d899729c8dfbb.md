The green algae exhibit great morphological diversity while sharing fundamental biochemical and ultrastructural features. Their photosynthetic pigments are similar to those in higher plants and include chlorophyll a and b, α-, β-, and γ-carotene, and various xanthophylls. Chloroplasts vary in number and shape, but always have two membranes, two to five thylakoids per lamella, and usually one or more pyrenoids. A cell wall, which may be calcified, is usually present, often containing cellulose, hydroxyllysine glycosides, xylan, and mannann. The chief food reserve is starch, stored in the chloroplast as granules which often sheathe a pyrenoid. Motile cells usually have two or four apically inserted smooth flagella of approximately equal length. See CELL PLASTIDS; CHLOROPHYLL; PHOTOSYNTHESIS.

Almost all somatic cell types known for algae occur among the Chloryrophyta, the exceptions being rhizopodial unicells and complex multicellular thalli differentiated into macroscopic organs. Ultrastructural studies have revealed differences in details of nuclear and cell division, cell coverings, plasmadema, pyrenoids, and flagellar structure that are correlated among themselves and with certain traditional characters to a degree that strongly suggests that the ulothricine line is polyphyletic, with desmoseioid having evolved at least four times. Moreover, these studies suggest that the ultimate ancestral green flagellate was prasinospicacean (asymmetric and covered with scales) rather than Chlamydonomas-like. Integration of these studies and inferences suggests four phyletic lines: chloropycean, ulvopycean, chlorophycean, and prasinosphecean.

In addition to the four phyletic lines, each of which includes monads as well as multicellular algae, there is a residual group of primitive green flagellates for which the name Micromonadopylae has been proposed. These monads are scaly or secondarily naked and have one to four flagella arising from an apical pit or lateral depression. The interzonal motile spindle persists during cytokinesis. This group corresponds to the class Prasinosphecae in the present classification. For the sake of consistency, however, Prasinosphecae and Charophyceae are treated elsewhere in this work as separate classes. See ALGAE; CHLOROPYCEAE; PRASINOPHYCEAE. [PC.Si; R.L.Mo€]

# Chloropyta  A division of the plant kingdom (also known as Chloropyta or Chloropyrophyta) comprising all algae that have chlorophyll a and b except the Eugleno-*phyteae, and that in all other respects are so different as to suggest a separate origin of their photosynthetic pigments. Three classes, Chloropyceae [charophytes], Chlorophyceae [green algae], and Prasinophyceae, are recognized. See ALGAE; CARO-PHYCEAE; CHLOROPHYCEAE; PRASINOPHYCEAE. [PC.Si; R.L.Mo€]

# Chloropyll  The generic name for the intensely colored green pigments which are the photoreceptors of light energy in photosynthesis. These pigments belong to the tetrapyroryle family of organic compounds.

Five closely related chloropylls, designated a through e, occur in higher plants and algae. The principal chloropyll (Chl) is Chl a (both in all oxygen-evolving organisms; photosynthetic bacteria, which do not evolve O2, contain instead bacteriopro-pyill (Bchl). Higher plants and green algae contain Chl b, the ratio of Chl b to a being 1:3. Chloropyll c (of two or more types) is present in diatoms and brown algae. Chloropyll d, isolated from marine red algae, has not been shown to be present in the living cell in large enough quantities to be observed in the absorption spectrum of these algae. Chloropyll e has been isolated from cultures of two algae, Tribonemena boninense and Vaucheria hamata. In higher plants the chloropylls and the above-mentioned pigments are contained in lipid-protein bodies and plastids. See CAROTENOID; CELL PLASTIDS; PHOTOSYNTHESIS.

Chloropyll molecules have three functions: They serve as antennae to absorb light quanta; they transmit this energy from one chloropyll to another by a process of "resonance transfer;" and finally, this chloropyll molecule, in close association with enzymes, undergoes a chemical oxidation (that is, an electron of high potential is ejected from the molecule and can then be used to reduce another compound). In this way the energy of light quanta is converted into chemical energy.

The chloropylls are cyclic tetrapyrroles in which four 5-membered pyrrole rings join to form a giant macrocycle. Chlo-rpylls are members of the porphyrine family, which plays important roles in respiratory pigments, electron transport carriers, and oxidative enzymes. See PORPHYRIN.

The two major pigments of protoplasms, green chloropyll and red chyme, are synthesized from ALA (δ-aminolevulinic acid) along the same biosynthetic pathway to idenpyll. ALA is converted in a series of enzymic steps, identical in plants and animals, to protoporphyrin. Here the pathway branches to form (1) a series of porpyrins chelated with iron, as heme and related cytochrome pigments; and (2) a series of porpyrins chelated with magnesium which are precursors of chloropyll. See HEMOGLOBIN.

Chloropylls reemit a fraction of the light energy they absorb as fluorescence. Irrespective of the wavelength of the absorbed light, the emitted fluorescence is always on the long-wavelength side of the lowest energy absorption band, in the red or infrared region of the spectrum.

The fluorescent properties of a particular chloropyll are functions of the structure of the molecule and its immediate environing. Thus, the fluorescence spectrum of chloropyll in the living plant is always shifted to longer wavelengths relative to the fluorescence spectrum of a solution of the same pigment. This red shift is characteristic of aggregated chloropyll. [G.S.G; G.P]
