# Chapter 10 · NetNews

The history of Usenet begins back in 1979 when two graduate students, Tom Truscott and Jim Ellis, thought of using the UNIX Copy soft-ware (UUCP), to connect machines for the purpose of information exchange among users. At first, traffic was handled by a number of shell scripts, but the first public software was Anews. Anews was small and not designed to handle more than a few articles per group day. When the volume of readers grew, Anews was rewritten and released as Bnews in 1982 and then rewritten again as Cnews in 1987. RFC 850 and 1036, titled Standard for Interchange of USENET Messages detailed the format for the interchange of network News messages among USENET hosts, defining the format of news messages, transmission methods, and propagation methods.

All of the news server releases up until Cnews were targeted primarily by UUCP networks, though they could be used over different protocols as well.

With the growing popularity of other networks as TCP/IP and DEC-Net, more efficient methods of news transfer had to be developed, and so the Network News Transfer Protocol (NNTP), was born in 1986. NNTP was designed as a “store and forward” technology, meaning that news articles are stored in a central database from which readers pull individual articles. Further technical details of RFC977, A Proposed Standard for the Stream-Based Transmission of News.

At first, NNTP was available only as an add-on package to Cnews, but then the Internet News program, more commonly known as ANN, was developed. ANN combined the NNTP section with the news server itself, making installation and configuration easier than before. Originally, the ANN software was written and maintained by Rich Salz up until ANN1.4sec, released in the middle of 1993. At that point, Rich not had the time to maintain the distribution, and Dave Barr uno stepped in and took over maintenance and wrote the 1.4off versions of the software. In 1996, Rich turned over the ANN distribution to the Internet Software Consortium (ISC) with the understanding that they would take over maintenance and development of future ANN releases. The ISC, mainly James Brister, merged the changes that had occurred in the unofficial source off and then added more features and bug fixes and produced version 1.5 and above.

Today, most sites on the Internet use ANN as their news server, but there are various newly developed servers such as Cyclone, Dnews, Diablo, and others. This chapter deals primarily with the ANN software and how to install, configure, and troubleshoot it on a Linux system.
