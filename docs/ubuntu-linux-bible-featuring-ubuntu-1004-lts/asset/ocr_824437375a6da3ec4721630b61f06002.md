# Part II: Ubuntu for Desktop Users

# Adding SATA Drives

SATA drives are much easier to attach and use than EIDE/ATA or SCSI drives, primarily because no jumping is required. SATA drives are automatically identified by your system based on the number of the SATA port that they are attached to. Most motherboards with SATA support have two or four SATA ports that feature the L-shaped SATA connector in their center. Stand-alone SATA controllers are also available that typically provide four SATA ports but can provide many more.

SATA has many advantages over traditional EIDE/ATA drives (which I'll call PATA drives in the rest of this section). First and foremost, each SATA drive has a separate cable connecting to the controller, reducing the shared-cable, master/slave model used by PATA drives. Although performance is approximately the same between SATA II and PATA drives, the dedicated connection and controller for each SATA drive can provide higher throughput in disk-intensive environments. SATA cables can be up to 39 inches long, while PATA cables are supposed to be limited to 19 inches. The fact that SATA cables are much smaller than PATA ribbon makes them them to route aesthetically within the tight confines of many computer cases. And finally, the biggest advantage of SATA over PATA drives is that SATA drives can be hot-swapped, making them entirely suitable for RAID or other environments with 24 × 7 availability requirements.

To add another SATA drive to your system, simply mount it in an available drive bay, connect it to an available SATA port, and power the drive by connecting either a traditional power connector or a special SATA power connector, depending on the drive you're using. At this point, you can power up your system, boot into Linux, and then follow the instructions in the section later entitled "Locating, Partitioning, and Formatting New Drives." If you have problems booting your system after adding a new drive, see the section, "Troubleshooting Boot Problems After Adding New Drives," later in this chapter.

# Tip

Some motherboards provide two different sets of SATA ports, one for hardware RAID support and another of normal SATA ports. Do not use the hardware RAID ports unless you're sure that the hardware controller is well supported under Linux and you're sure that you want to use hardware RAID. I usually prefer Linux software to Linux hardware RAID because controller-specific. Using hardware RAID often means that if your motherboard fails, you will need to replace it with an identical motherboard or one with the same RAID controller. In most cases, you will not be able to use your existing drive(s) with a different hardware RAID controller.

# Adding SCSI Drives

As for SCSI, I knew them well. SCSI drives have long been the traditional drive of choice for RAID boxes or in systems with 24 × 7 availability requirements. The primary reasons for this are high performance (SCSI drives are still faster than SATA II drives), SCSI's traditional support for hot-swapping, and higher quality control (some manufacturers even offer longer warranties for SCSI drives for this reason).
