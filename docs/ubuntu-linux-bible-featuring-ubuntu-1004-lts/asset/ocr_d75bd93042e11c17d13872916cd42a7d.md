# Using Command-Line Tools to Identify Drives and Partitions

The df command provides an easy way to examine all the disk partitions and associated file-systems that are currently mounted and in use on your system. Unfortunately, commands like df don't provide information about devices that are newly attached to your system (and that may not contain partitions yet) or devices that are attached to your system whose filesystems are not mounted. Luckily, Linux provides some easy ways of finding all of the storage devices on your system, regardless of whether they're partitioned, mounted, or not. You'll have to put up with a small bit of cryptic command-line magic, but you're at least an apprentice Linux wizard, right? After all, you just press a new drive in your system!

The easiest way to determine device nodes associated with the storage devices in your system is to look through the kernel messages for patterns that match the strings associated with those devices. You can quickly look through the in-memory portion of these messages using a command called dmesg. Because you know IDE devices traditionally had names that began with hd and are followed by some other letter, that SATA and SCSI devices on newer kernels have the same naming convention we use a pattern such as `[sdX][a-z]` to see what exactly match these device names. If you don't speak Linux regular expressions, the string `[sdX][a-z]` means, any sequence of characters beginning with an h or s, followed by the letter d, and any character between a and z (inclusive), followed by a colon, followed by a space. That was easy enough?

The result of searching for this string in the dmesg command on a sample system is precisely exactly what you'd hope for, for in the following examples:
