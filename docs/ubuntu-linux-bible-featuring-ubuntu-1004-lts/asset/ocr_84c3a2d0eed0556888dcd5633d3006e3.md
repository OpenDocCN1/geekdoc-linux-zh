# Chapter 20: Adding Hardware and Attaching Peripherals

After you jumper your new drive, mount it in a drive bay, attach it to the selected IDE cable in your system, and provide power to the drive by connecting it to one of your system's power feeds. At this point, you can power up the system, boot into Linux, and then follow the instructions in the section later in this chapter entitled "Locating, Partitioning, and Formatting New Drives." Some common problems at this point are:

+   - If you have trouble booting your system after adding a new drive, see the section, "Troubleshooting Boot Problems after Adding New Drive," later in this chapter.
- The most common source of problems when adding a new EIDE/ATA drive to an existing system is jumping it incorrectly. If a new drive is jumped to the same (master or slave) as an existing drive, your system will not be able to see either drive. You can verify that the new drive is jumped, attached, and detected correctly by entering the BIOS immediately after rebooting the PC and examining the primary and secondary IDE interfaces.
- If you find problems jumping the new drive after booting, reboot your system and press a key such as the F2 or Delete key to enter your system's BIOS setup screens. (A message that identifies the key that is required to access your system's BIOS is usually displayed when you boot your computer.) The Main or System section of these screens should enable you to probe your IDE or SATA drives to verify that your system can actually find them. If your system does not see the drive(s) at all, check the power and IDE/SATA connections to the drive.

You will not be able to use any new drive on your system until you have partitioned and formatted the drive. Some drives come pre-formatted with an NTFS filesystem, which is probably not a filesystem that you want to use on an internal drive on a Linux system. See the section later in this chapter entitled "Locating, Partitioning, and Formatting New Drives" for detailed information about partitioning and formatting your new drives.

# Hard Drive Names on Linux Systems.

The traditional IDE subsystem on Linux systems recognized EIDE/ATA hard drives as devices with names of the form /dev/hdX, where X was a letter that reflected the sequence in which drives were located on your system's IDE interfaces. SCSI hard drives used their driver subsystem, using names of the form /dev/sdX. SATA also used the SCSI subsystem and, therefore, followed the same naming convention as removable Flash and USB. However, in 2007, the existing IDE subsystem in the kernel was replaced by a new subsystem using /dev/sdX naming convention. This is a superior subsystem, although it certainly confused many people when drives formerly known as /dev/hdX started up with names in the form /dev/sdX after a kernel upgrade, especially if your GRUB configuration file wasn't updated at the same time. This should all be under the bridge now, but it's worth explaining because hard-drive naming conventions often confuse people in the first place, let alone after drives "rename themselves."
