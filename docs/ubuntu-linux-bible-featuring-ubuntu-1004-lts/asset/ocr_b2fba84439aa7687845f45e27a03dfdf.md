# Part II: Ubuntu for Desktop Users

Broadband Ethernet, even cheaper wired hardware, and the explosive growth of wireless networking made a true reality for many home computer users. Home computer systems may now have real IP addresses and functional connection speeds to the Internet and are also commonly members of home computer networks that share those connections to the Internet using mechanisms such as Native Address Translation (NAT). If you're new to networking, the first section of this chapter provides an overview of basic networking concepts and terminology.

Better networking and network access come at a price. Ubiquitous networking gives thousands of “randoms” access to your computer system through a real IP server or other network processes. Most of them could care less, some are simply curious, and others are downright malicious. The last set gives everyone a bad name by actively trying to break into computers in some fashion. I have no problem with hackers who are simply curious about what's out there—exploration has always been a fundamental part of the human condition. Real hackers are the electronic equivalent of the National Geographic Society or Star Trek, where nobody has gone before. Who break into systems to damage or exploit them, however, are crackers, who give everyone a bad name.

Unfortunately, there are plenty of unscrupulous hackers who would like to break into your machine and damage it or turn it into some sort of zombie system, either to supposedly demonstrate their cleverness or to somehow make a buck. Ubiquitous networking gives the availability of tools that this enable sort of thing. The people that use them are often-called script kiddies, who use using tools to demonstrate cleverness in the same way as giving a child an Uzi access to marksmanship.

The bottom line of ubiquitous networking is that security becomes everyone’s job. If you live in a small town that considers two newspapers from the box on the corner a serious crime, locking your door at night may seem very silly. Unfortunately, when you use a personal computer with network access, you are part of the big city known as the Internet. The administrators of enterprise and academic systems that require continuous access to the Internet have this for a long time. Sadly enough, nowadays, your parents, and your grandmother have to worry about it, too. Security is more of a concern today than it has ever been before, and tomorrow will just be worse.

This chapter gives a basic introduction to networking, explains the tools that Ubuntu Linux provides to manually and graphically configure and test your network, and (most importantly) provides some general guidelines on how to secure your system to protect it as well as any can. There’s an old saying in the IT business that the only truly secure system is one that isn’t connected to anything. Although this is true, it’s also impractical. There are easy rules to follow minimize the chances that your system will be broken into. You’re already running Ubuntu, which puts you miles ahead of the millions of vulnerable Microsoft Windows users out there.
