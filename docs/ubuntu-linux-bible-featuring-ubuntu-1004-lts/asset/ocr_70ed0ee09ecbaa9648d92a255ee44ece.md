# Chapter 7: Managing E-Mail and Personal Information with Evolution

# A Quick Guide to POP versus IMAP

The key difference between POP and IMAP protocols lies in where your e-mail is actually stored. POP clients typically download your e-mail from the POP server and store it on the machine where your mail client is running. This is extremely convenient for the operators of the mail server— they don't have to provide enough storage for e-mail because the storage requirements for POP mail are transient. As people read their mail, it is downloaded to the client and usually deleted from the server. IMAP servers, on the other hand, permanently store your mail on the server and only download the mail headers to your client when you first check your mail. These headers include information about the sender, the date on which the message was sent, and so on. When you select a message to read from an IMAP server, the mail is copied to your local, but the master copy of the message remains on the IMAP server. The storage requirements for IMAP mailers therefore tend to increase over time, as more and more people accumulate collections of saved e-mail.

Each of these has advantages and disadvantages. POP keeps all of your mail on your local machine, which is handy if you want to review your existing mail when you're not connected to the NET. The downside, of course, is that all of your mail lives on a single machine, and it's tricky to read old mail from a mailer other than the one where it is actually stored. IMAP mailers make it easy to access your mail from any machine, anywhere, but require a network connection to make that in order to do so. Most IMAP clients enable you to synchronize your mailboxes with those stored on the IMAP server, so you can easily access your mail while traveling without requiring a network connection. However, most IMAP people like me, who have mail archives going back 10 years or so require significant storage on an IMAP server.

Most POP clients, including Evolution's POP, provide a special option "Leave mail on server." This enables you to configure multiple clients to read your POP mail, but only one of them (typically your home machine) downloads the mail and deletes it from the server. All POP clients remember the last message that they read, so they don't re-download all of your mail each time you check your mail—only that they recognize as new. This provides a nice compromise between the flexibility of IMAP and the local permanence of POP. For example, when I'm on the road, the copy of Evolution on my laptop lets me read my mail but doesn't delete it from the server—this happens when I get back home and check mail from my primary machine. The downside of POP while traveling, of course, is that I don't have access to all of my mail when I'm on the road, but if I'm really desperate, I can always connect to my home again and manually look through my mailboxes or run Evolution from that over the NET.
