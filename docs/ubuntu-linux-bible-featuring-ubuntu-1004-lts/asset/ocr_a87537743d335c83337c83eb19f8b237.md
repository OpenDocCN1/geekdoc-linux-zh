A final issue to consider when deciding backups and restoring is when to do them, and what privileges are required. It's generally fastest to do backups during off-peak hours when system usage is at a minimum, so that the backups can complete as quickly as possible, and when people are less likely to be modifying the files that you're backing up. In an enterprise environment, this might mean you'll want to have a graveyard shift of operators. In this case, you'll need to think about how to make sure that operators have the right set of privileges. Being able to back up user files that may be heavily protected or using a backup system that accesses the filesystem generally requires root privileges. Many people use such as sudo (which is already frequent on Ubuntu systems) or set-bits on privileged binaries such as backup and restore programs so they don't have to give the administrative password to the operators or part-time staff that generally do backups at off-peak hours. See the section of Chapter 27, "Managing Users, Groups, and Authentication," entitled "Customizing the sudo Command" for some suggestions on how to assign privileges to different groups of individuals on your Ubuntu system.

# Verifying and Testing Backups

Just doing backups isn't a guarantee that you're safe from problems, unless you're also sure that the backups you're making are readable and that files can be easily restored from them. Although its common today, there's always the chance that the heads in a tape drive may be out of alignment. This might mean that you can only read the tapes back on the same tape drive you wrote them on, or that they can't be read at all. You should always verify that you back and restore files from backups using another device than the one on which they were made. You don't have to check backup every day, but random spot checks are important for peace of mind and job security. Similarly, tapes can stretch or wear out from use—be prepared to replace the media you use for various types of incremental backups after some amount of time. Nobody appreciates WORN backup media—"once write, never read"—even though its storage capacity is infinite.

One of the problems inherent in backups is that every type of computer media has a shelf life of some period of time, depending on the type of media, the environment in it is stored, and how lucky you are. No backup media has infinite shelf life. For example, backups can last for years, but they can also become unreachable after a shorter period of time. Long-lived media such as write-CD-Rs and DVDs are一来 attractive because of their supposed longevity, but they have other problems, as mentioned earlier in the sidebar entitled "A Few Words about Backup Media." Media as these may only be suited for certain types of backups, depending on whether your backup software writes to the backup filesystem or as a raw storage device. Also, no one knows exactly how long those types of media will last, but they certainly take up less room than any kind of hard drives. In addition to spot-checking the backup media that you are currently using, you should always make a point to spot-check old archives every few years to make sure they're still useful.
