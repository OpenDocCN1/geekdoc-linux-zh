## Working with Multimedia

MP3, WMA, OGG, FLAC, AAC, APE—this may look like a blast from a shotgun far loaded with three-letter acronyms (TLAs), but it's really a list of the most popular formats for digital audio nowadays. The same is true of digital video, where acronyms such as MPG, MPEG, and VOB are king. There are also really ugly acronyms such as MPAA and RIAA, which stand for groups of self-interested thugs trying to deny the rights of the consumer, but we won't go into that.

The great thing about digital audio and video is that you no longer need specialized devices to play your music or watch a movie. You can listen to a music CD or play a video CD or DVD on your computer. Once you buy a CD or DVD, it should be completely legal for you to transfer its contents to your computer and listen to it or watch it. This eliminates the need to carry around the original media, and you can safely leave them at home as backups if your computer crashes. Digital Rights Management (DRM, also known as “ripping off the consumer”) may prevent you from doing that, but the possibility exists unless it is actively subscribed by the manufacturer of your CD or DVD.

This chapter helps you make some sense out of the maze of acronyms, general terminology, and specialized jargon that aficionados use to describe digital audio and video; and explains how to use the software available for Linux to listen to music, watch movies, and create your own CDs and DVDs. I love being able to check into a hotel room and relax while listening to my favorite tunes because I've brought them on my favorite multimedia device—a computer running Ubuntu Linux.

## Overview of Digital Audio and Video

As mentioned in the introduction to this chapter, digital audio and video is an area in which the computer industry's tendency toward using jargon and three-letter acronyms makes many conversations and reference almost incomprehensible to the novice. I've been a record collector and musician for years, I still encounter terms that I have to look up or decrypt, and there is no single source of this sort of terminology encompassing sites like Wikipedia. This section, therefore, provides a basic glossary for the most common audio, video, and digital multimedia terms that will be used.

- **AAC (Advanced Audio Coding):** A lossy codec (a codec that sacrifices quality for file size) originally developed by Dolby Laboratories that is similar to MP3 files in size but provides slightly higher quality. AAC is now often, incorrectly referred to as Apple's format to impose DRM on its customers.

- **ABR (average bit rate):** A type of encoding in which the user specifies the target bit rate for sampling, and the encoder calculates the appropriate VBR (variable bit rate) values to achieve.

- **AIFF (Audio Interchange File Format):** A lossless audio codec that preserves sound quality by using larger file sizes originally developed by Apple, and therefore commonly used on Macintosh systems.

- **ALAC (Apple Lossless Audio Codec):** A lossless audio codec developed by Apple. This format is frequently confused with M4A, which is a container that can deliver files in ALAC and other formats.

- **APE:** The file extension for audio files encoded using a proprietary lossless audio codec. This provides slightly better compression (i.e., smaller file sizes) than FLAC, but at the cost of slower encoding/decoding. APE files are primarily used on Linux platforms. For more information about the APE codec, see www.monkeyaudio.com.

- **bit rate:** The number of bits per interval to make audio signal, often measured in Kbps (thousands of bits per second). This is overkill for digital.

- **BitTorrent:** A protocol for downloading and uploading digital data, including audio and video files. BitTorrent is not a multimedia term, provides a mechanism for sharing multimedia files. See the section of Chapter 26, "File Transfer and Sharing," for more information on using BitTorrent.

- **Burn:** The term for writing data to recordable or rewritable CD or DVD media. The term comes from the fact that data is written to CDs and DVDs using a laser that inscribes pits on their surfaces to represent digital information.

- **CBR (constant bit rate):** A codec that uses a fixed number of bits per second as opposed to ABR or VBR.

- **CD (compact disc):** A storage device capable of holding approximately 700 MB of digital information.

- **CD-R (compact disc/recordable):** A storage device capable of holding approximately 700 MB of digital information; CD-R disc can be written once by home computers with compatible drives. Each session writes a different portion of the disc.

- **CD-RW (compact disc/rewritable):** A storage device capable of holding approximately 700 MB of digital information, which can be written multiple times by home computers with compatible drives. Only CD-RW drives can read.

- **CDDA (Compact Disc Audio):** The format used for unprotected audio CDs; sometimes referred to as CDA because of Windows' inability to use.

- **CDDB (CD Database):** A commercial, online database that provides identification and track listings for commercial CDs.

- **codec (compressor/decompressor):** An algorithm by which digital signals are pressed to a minimum amount of quality and decompressed for playback.

- **DAT (digital audio tape):** A digital recording medium used in recording studios and by audio professionals.

- **DAE (digital audio extraction):** The process of cloning media by directly copying it rather than converting the analog audio output and saving information to a digital storage device.

- **disc-at-once:** A mechanism that writes the entire surface of the CD or DVD disc rather than the amount of storage required to hold the information.

- **DMCA (Digital Millennium Copyright Act):** Legislation passed in the United States in which the goal was to preserve copyrights for digital media that has been used for reverse engineering, eliminate encryption technology, and free reuse of legitimately purchased audio and video files.

- **DRM (Digital Rights Management):** A technique for denying legitimate users of audio and video files the ability to freely copy and use them.

- **DSP (digital signal processing):** Using various algorithms to convert or modify audio and video signals. DSP can be done in hardware, software, or a combination of both.

- **DVD (digital video disc):** A storage device capable of holding approximately 4.7 GB of digital information.

- **EQ (equalize):** A recording effect that increases or decreases the volume of frequency ranges in an audio signal. Equalizing frequency ranges smooths the audio signal by preventing extreme differences in volume across frequency ranges.

- **FFP (FLAC footprint):** Checksum files that are typically distributed with FLAC-encoded files to verify their integrity. See the entry for MD5 in this glossary for more information about checksums.

- **FLAC (Free Lossless Audio Codec):** A lossless audio codec that typically achieves compression rates of 30 to 50 percent without any reduction in quality from the original recordings.

- **FreeDB (Free DB):** An open source alternative to CDDB that enables users to contribute information about CDs.

- **ID3 tags:** 128-byte header information in MP3 files that is used to provide information about the audio file. ID3v1 tags provide audio, artist, general comments, genre, and title information. ID3v2 tags provide this information and more in a more free-form fashion, permitting larger, user-defined tags that support all ID3v1 tags but can also include information such as lyrics and images.

- **ISO (International Standards Organization):** In the context of CDs and DVDs, ISO refers to the three-letter file extension added to CD and DVD images to show the contents of the file conform to the ISO9660 specification, which defines CD and DVD formats. People often refer to image files that contain complete CD or DVD images as ISOs.

- **Lossless formats:** File formats that are not lossy, file formats that provide all of the information that was present in the audio, graphics, or video information as it was originally recorded, drawn, or captured.

- **Lossy formats:** File formats that sacrifice quality to reduce file size. The term lossy refers to the data compression algorithm used to reduce file size, some of the information that was present in the original file is actually eliminated to produce the smaller, compressed, and therefore the original file can never be reconstructed from the compressed version. In the case of audio files, lossy images typically reduce both the bit rate and the frequency ranges at which audio is recorded.

- **M4A:** An audio file format that can contain AAC or (more commonly) ALAC files. Protected versions of M4A files that have been subjected to Digital Rights Management are delivered as M4P files.
