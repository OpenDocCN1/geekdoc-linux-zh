
## Chapter 20: Adding Hardware and Attaching Peripherals

### Adding Internal Disks and CD/DVD Drives

Portable USB and FireWire drives are common today, as are USB flash/thumb drives (which look like small hard drives to your system). These portable, removable storage devices simplify backups, transporting large amounts of data and transferring large amounts of data from one system to another. For the most part, these removable devices are correctly identified by Ubuntu systems, which either mount new devices or prompt you for the action to take, as described in the portions of Chapter 18 on digital detection.

While portable, external devices are convenient, you may eventually want to add more storage to your system permanently, which means adding new drives to free connections that are available on your system. These interfaces are faster than external storage and also keep the number of cables running and from the exterior of your system to a minimum.

This section explains how to add internal drives to your systems. The most common types of hard drives found in desktop and laptop IDE/ATA (Enhanced Integrated Drive/Advanced Technology Attachment) and SATA (Serial Advanced Technology) drives. Because of the popularity of SATA drives, IDE/ATA are also known as PATA (Parallel Adaptive Technology Attachment) drives, which highlights the basic difference between these two types of drives (parallel versus serial) and also delivers a nicely paired acronym at the same time. SCSI (Small Computer System Interface) hard drives are another possibility, but are much less common in desktop systems. Most CD and DVD drives are IDE/ATA drives, using a standard referred to as ATAPI, although SCSI and SATA CD and DVD drives are also available.

To add any of these internally to your system, you will have to shut down the system and remove its side cover to give you access to the motherboard and to the partitions of the case where hard drives can be mounted, which are usually referred to as drive bays*.

The next few sections explain how to add extra drives of these various types to your system, describe how to identify the device nodes associated with those drives when you reboot your system, and provide an overview of partitioning and creating filesystems on these devices.

#### Tip

Before adding new internal disks to your system, you should modify the GRUB bootloader's configuration so it gives you a chance to choose boot options if something goes wrong. To do this, edit the /etc/default/grub and comment out the following lines (putting a # character at the beginning of each line):

```
GRUB_HIDDEN_TIMEOUT=0
GRUB_HIDDEN_TIMEOUT_QUIET=true
```

Commenting these lines causes GRUB to display a boot menu before showing its splash screen. If problems occur, you can edit the commands in menu to boot your system, and update your GRUB configuration to make your changes permanent.

After making these changes and saving the file, run the update-grub command configuration files on your system. Once you've added the new drives and verified that your system boots correctly, you can un-comment these entries and rerun update-grub to hide the boot menus—until the next time you need them, of course. ■

## Part II: Ubuntu for Desktop Users

### Adding EIDE/IDE PATA Drives

Once your system is turned off and you have removed one of its side panels so that you have access to the IDE interfaces that are attached to the EIDE interfaces on your motherboard (or, in rare cases, an IDE or EIDE controller card). IDE cables are flat, 40-pin ribbon-bonbons with two flat, notched connectors near one end and a similar connector at the other end. Most PCs have two IDE interfaces, known as primary (IDE-1) and secondary (IDE-2). The cable from each IDE interface can be attached to a maximum of two hard or CD/DVD drives. If two drives are attached to a single cable, they must be configured as master and slave drives by connecting pins as jumpers that are located on the back or bottom of the hard or CD-ROM drive. Master is the term used for the first drive on an IDE interface; slave is the term used for the second drive on the IDE interface.

To add a new drive to your system, you must set the jumper (or jumpers) on the new drive so it does not conflict with any existing drive on a given IDE interface in your system. To do so, you must know how the current drives are connected. You can determine how the existing hard drive(s) and CD/DVD drive(s) are connected in one of two ways: automatically (by checking the BIOS settings on your system) or manually (by simply checking the connections and jumpers on the existing drives). Because you've (hopefully) turned off the PC and all the ways-collecting information from a PC BIOS differ depending on WHICH your system uses, we'll focus on looking at the cases and jumpers.

By tracing cables and examining jumpers, you can manually identify how the drives are connected. To do so, follow each of the IDE cables from your motherboard back to any hard or CD/DVD drives connected to them. The hard drive that your PC boots from is known as the boot drive, and if CD/DVD drive is connected to the same IDE cables as your hard drive, it is probably jumpered as the slave on the primary IDE interface (IDE-1). If your CD-ROM drive is connected to your other IDE cable (IDE-2), it is probably jumpered as the master on that interface.

#### Tip

If there are two devices attached to an IDE interface and your PC was working correctly before, skip that IDE interface—it's already fully populated and correctly configured. Once you know how your current drives are connected, you can proceed to jumper your new drive so that it does not conflict with existing drives in your system. A guide to the jumpers on your hard drive that will cause the drive to be recognized as a master or slave drive is usually printed on the top of the hard drive.

#### Caution

Be careful when reading the jumper information; although it is usually written on the hard drive some jumper settings are located on the left or right.

After you jumper your new drive, mount it in your bay, attach it to the selected IDE cable and provide power to the drive. At this point, you can power up the system, boot into Linux, and then follow the instructions in the section "Adding New Drives." Some common problems at this point are:

- If you have trouble booting your system after adding a new drive, see the section, "Troubleshooting Boot after Adding New Drive."
- The most common source of problems when adding new drive to an existing system is jumping it incorrectly. If a new drive is jumped to the same (master or slave) as an existing drive, your system will not be able to see either drive.
- If you find problems jumping the new drive after booting, reboot your system and press a key such as F2 or Delete to enter your system's BIOS setup screens. (A message that identifies the key that is required to access your system's BIOS is usually displayed when you boot your computer.) The Main or System section of these screens should enable you to probe your IDE or SATA drives to verify that your system can actually find them. If your system does not see the drive(s) at all, check the power and IDE/SATA connections to the drive.

You will not be able to use any new drive on your system until you have partitioned and formatted the drive. Some drives come pre-formatted with an NTFS filesystem, which is probably not a filesystem you want to use on an internal drive on a Linux system. See the section later in this chapter entitled "Locating, Partitioning, and Formatting New Drives" for detailed information about partitioning and formatting your new drives.

### Hard Drive Names on Linux Systems

The traditional IDE subsystem on Linux systems recognized EIDE/ATA hard drives as devices with names of the form /dev/hdX, where X was a letter that reflected the sequence in which drives were located on your system's IDE interfaces. SCSI hard drives used their driver subsystem, using names of the form /dev/sdX. SATA also used the SCSI subsystem and, therefore, followed the same naming convention as removable Flash and USB. However, in 2007, the existing IDE subsystem in the kernel was replaced by a new subsystem using /dev/sdX naming convention. This is a superior subsystem, although it certainly confused many people when drives formerly known as /dev/hdX started up with names in the form /dev/sdX after a kernel upgrade, especially if your GRUB configuration file wasn't updated at the same time.
