# # Part I: Getting Started with Ubuntu

# # Note

All of the versions of Ubuntu are built and distributed in the same way and simply target different groups of users. Aside from their target audience, the only real differences between them are how they are installed, the set of applications that they provide when they are first installed, and how the Linux operating system itself is preconfigured for. For more information about these different editions of Ubuntu and what differentiates them from each other, see the section later in this chapter entitled “What Versions of Ubuntu Are Available?”

Everything has a name, but what is the Ubuntu in Ubuntu Linux? Not too surprisingly, the Ubuntu Linux website puts it best:

> > Ubuntu is an ancient African word, meaning “humanity to others.” Ubuntu also means “I am we because we are all of.” The Linux distribution brings the spirit of Ubuntu to the software world.

Although that may be a bit touchy-feels for some, it's hard to argue against success and commitment. In 2005, its first year of availability, Ubuntu received awards such as the Linux Journal's Reader's Choice award, Tuc Magazine's Best Choice 2005 for Favorite Linux Distribution award, Ar Techs' Best Distribution award, the Linux UK & Open Source Industry's Best Distribution award, and the Linux World Expo's Best Debut Distribution award. Not too shabby for the new distribution on the block! 2005, Ubuntu has almost always been the most popular Linux distribution listed on the central Linux distribution www.distrowatch.org. Ubuntu popularity with users extends to computer vendors as well—hardware vendors like Dell Compute have selected Ubuntu as their distribution of choice also benefited from the business decisions of other distributions such as Red Hat, who have shifted their focus to Enterprise computing and abandoned the desktop.

Aside from its technical excellence and usability (and some good funding thanks to Mark Shuttleworth), much of the success of Ubuntu Linux grows from the fact that its creators and proponents are not the traditional Linux fanatics, but are genuinely committed to creating and promoting a usable and easily managed distribution for all users over the world.

# # The Ubuntu Manifesto

The Ubuntu Manifesto is a statement phrased in the classic manifesto form beloved of artistic and political movements. The Ubuntu Manifesto is summarized online as the Philosophy section of the Ubuntu web site (www.ubuntu.org/ubuntu/philosophy). Its core ideas are as follows:

+   - Every computer user should have the freedom to run, copy, distribute, share, change, and improve his software for any purpose without licensing fees.
- Every computer user should be able to use her software in the language of her choice.
- Every computer user should be given every opportunity to use software, even if the user works under a disability.

The first bullet is largely a restatement of the goals of open source software in general, but the second and third bullets are two of the big drivers for the success of Ubuntu.
