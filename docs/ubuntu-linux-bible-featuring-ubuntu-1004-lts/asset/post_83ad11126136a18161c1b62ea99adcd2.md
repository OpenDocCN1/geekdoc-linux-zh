
# Part II: Ubuntu for Desktop Users

## Adding SATA Drives

SATA drives are much easier to attach and use than EIDE/ATA or SCSI drives, primarily because no jumping is required. SATA drives are automatically identified by your system based on the number of the SATA port that they are attached to. Most motherboards with SATA support have two or four SATA ports that feature the L-shaped SATA connector in their center. Stand-alone SATA controllers are also available that typically provide four SATA ports but can provide many more.

SATA has many advantages over traditional EIDE/ATA drives (which I'll call PATA drives in the rest of this section). First and foremost, each SATA drive has a separate cable connecting to the controller, reducing the shared-cable, master/slave model used by PATA drives. Although performance is approximately the same between SATA II and PATA drives, the dedicated connection and controller for each SATA drive can provide higher throughput in disk-intensive environments. SATA cables can be up to 39 inches long, while PATA cables are supposed to be limited to 19 inches. The fact that SATA cables are much smaller than PATA ribbon makes them easier to route aesthetically within the tight confines of many computer cases. And finally, the biggest advantage of SATA over PATA drives is that SATA drives can be hot-swapped, making them entirely suitable for RAID or other environments with 24 × 7 availability requirements.

To add another SATA drive to your system, simply mount it in an available drive bay, connect it to an available SATA port, and power the drive by connecting either a traditional power connector or a special SATA power connector, depending on the drive you're using. At this point, you can power up your system, boot into Linux, and then follow the instructions in the section later entitled "Locating, Partitioning, and Formatting New Drives." If you have problems booting your system after adding a new drive, see the section, "Troubleshooting Boot Problems After Adding New Drives," later in this chapter.

# Tip

Some motherboards provide two different sets of SATA ports, one for hardware RAID support and another of normal SATA ports. Do not use the hardware RAID ports unless you're sure that the hardware controller is well supported under Linux and you're sure that you want to use hardware RAID. I usually prefer Linux software to Linux hardware RAID because hardware RAID is controller-specific. Using hardware RAID often means that if your motherboard fails, you will need to replace it with an identical motherboard or one with the same RAID controller. In most cases, you will not be able to use your existing drive(s) with a different hardware RAID controller.

## Adding SCSI Drives

As for SCSI, I knew them well. SCSI drives have long been the traditional drive of choice for RAID boxes or in systems with 24 × 7 availability requirements. The primary reasons for this are high performance (SCSI drives are still faster than SATA II drives), SCSI's traditional support for hot-swapping, and higher quality control (some manufacturers even offer longer warranties for SCSI drives for this reason). The downside is that SCSI drives are substantially more expensive than today's SATA II drives, which offer the same hot-swap capabilities. Some people would rather fight switch — your call. Traditionally, only very high-end motherboards feature integrated SCSI controllers — SCSI controllers in most systems are stand-alone SCSI control cards.

SCSI connectors and controllers vary according to the version of the SCSI standard they conform to and the number of drives that they can support. Various types of SCSI connectors have evolved over the years, with different versions of the SCSI standard and are specifically associated with different performance characteristics. The most common of connectors are Centronics-style parallel and IDC-50 (typically associated with 8-bit SCSI-1 drives), DB-50 connectors (typically associated with 8-Fast SCSI-2 drives), and DB-68 connectors or SCA-80 (typically associated with 16-bit, Fast/Wide SCSI-3), SCSI-1 and SCSI-2 controllers support up to seven drives per controller, while SCSI-3 and later controllers support up to 15 drives per controller.

Each SCSI drive in a system must be uniquely identified by setting it to a unique ID number. In most cases, you must set one or more jumpers to indicate its ID, but if you have more money than I and are using a multi-port SCSI controller, that controller may identify each drive on the port that it is connected to. The last SCSI drive in a chain must be terminated (i.e., drives are installed sequentially, each to the next), although some drives and SCSI controllers do this automatically.

To add another SCSI drive to your system, set it to a unique ID if necessary, mount it in an available drive bay, connect it to your SCSI controller or to the next SCSI drive in a chain, and provide power to the drive by connecting a traditional power connector. Then you can power up the system, at which point you should see the BIOS on your SCSI controller identify the IDs on all connected drives. Once boot into Linux, you can follow the instructions in the section later in this chapter entitled "Locating, Partitioning, and Formatting New Drives." If you have problems booting your system after adding a new drive, see the next section.

## Troubleshooting Boot Problems After Adding New Drives

It is common that adding a new drive to your system will prevent it from booting immediately. Much of this has to do with settings in your computer's BIOS or other boot environment that enumerate the hard drives and tell it in which order to try various drives and interfaces in order to boot. The next sections discuss a few common problems and how to resolve them.

## Changes to BIOS Hard Disk and Boot Device Priority Settings

Most BIOS menus feature a "Boot" or "Boot Settings" menu or screen that contains entries that lead to configuration screens for "Hard Disk Drives" and "Boot Device Priority." The "Hard Disk Drives" screen usually enumerates all of the hard drives in your system. Frequently, the first hard drive listed is the first that can be identified on the "Boot Device Priority" screen. Make sure that the first hard drive in this list is the drive that actually contains your operating system.

Next, make sure that the hard drive that contains your operating system is actually listed in the "Boot Device Priority" screen and that the entries on this screen appear in the right order. Many "Boot Device Priority" screens only provide three or four slots for possible boot devices, and I usually guard the first of these for "Floppy Disk" and "CD-ROM" devices. Whatever boot device order you prefer, make sure that the hard drive that contains your operating system is present in this list and appears in the right sequence.

## Problems After Adding IDE Drives to a SATA System

Most systems are configured to try boot from IDE drives before SATA disks. If you have added IDE disks to a system that formerly only contained SATA disks, make sure that the sequence in which your system tries to boot from different drives is correct. I had a humorous experience while recycling some old IDE disks for use on one of my SATA systems, because I hadn't wiped the IDE disks. I'd forgotten that one of them contained an older Linux and was quite surprised when my Ubuntu system booted SUSE.

## Problems Related to SATA Drive Name Changes

Adding SATA drives to an existing system can temporarily cause boot problems for various reasons, the most among is how SATA drives are detected and named. SATA drives not only use SCSI emulation (and thus have names beginning with /dev/sd*), but the letter follows /dev/sd in their name is assigned based on the sequence number of the specific SATA port that they are attached to. If your system was careful, your primary SATA disk will be attached to SATA port 1, and thus will always be the name /dev/sda. If you wasn't so careful and attached your boot drive to SATA port 4, your system will boot with a single drive, but adding another SATA drive will cause that to be assigned the name /dev/sda, and your existing SATA drive will be called /dev/sdb. Adding more than one new SATA drive will continue to make the name of your original SATA disk change. This generally confuses the BIOS, as described in the previous paragraph, and certainly the boot loader used by Ubuntu systems, GRUB.

If you encounter this problem, there are two possible solutions:

- Open up your system again and connect your boot drive to the first SATA port on your motherboard. This will guarantee that it will always be named /dev/sda. You may still have to change the BIOS settings, but you won't have to modify anything in your drive's boot configuration files.
- Change the boot settings used by the Linux GRUB boot loader to reflect the new name of your drive.

The first option is always preferable unless you have a very good reason for simply modifying the GRUB boot settings. If you insist on doing the second, and modifying GRUB, see the next section.
