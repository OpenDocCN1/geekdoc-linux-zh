The degree sequence of the multigraph $G_1$ in Figure 1.30 is 5, 4, 3 and that of the multigraph $G_2$ is 4, 3, 2, 1. That is, $G_1$ and $G_2$ are irregular multigraphs.

The multigraphs $G_1$ and $G_2$ in Figure 1.30 illustrate the following result.

> **Theorem 1.16** For every connected graph G of order at least 3, there exists an irregular multigraph whose underlying graph is G.

**Proof.** Let $E(G) = \{e_1, e_2, \ldots, e_m\}$, where $e_i = u_iv_i$ for $i = 1, 2, \ldots, m$. Replacing $e_i$ by $2^{i-1}$ parallel edges joining $u_i$ and $v_i$ produces a multigraph H. Since every two vertices of G have distinct sets of edges incident with them and every positive integer has a unique base 2 representation, their degrees in H are distinct and so H is irregular.

When describing walks in multigraphs or in pseudographs, it is often necessary to list edges in the sequence as well as vertices in order to specify the edges being used in the walk. For example,

$$W = (u, e_1, u, e_3, v, e_6, u, e_6, v, e_7, w)$$

is a u − w walk in the pseudograph G of Figure 1.31.
