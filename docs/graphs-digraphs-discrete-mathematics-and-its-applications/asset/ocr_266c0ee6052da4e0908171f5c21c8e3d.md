The total chromatic number $\chi''(G)$ of a graph $G$ is the minimum positive integer $k$ for which $G$ is $k$-total colorable.

If $c$ is a total coloring of a graph $G$ and $v$ is a vertex of $G$ with $\operatorname{deg} v = \Delta(G)$, then $c$ must assign distinct colors to the $\Delta(G)$ edges incident with $v$ as well as to $v$ itself. This implies that

$$\chi''(G) \geq 1 + \Delta(G) \text{ for every graph } G.$$ 

Let $G = K_6 - e$ be the graph of order $n = 6$ and size $m = \binom{6}{2} - 1 = 14$ shown in Figure 18.6, where $e = xz$. Since $\Delta(G) = 5$, it follows that $\chi''(G) \geq 1 + \Delta(G) = 6$. The 7-total coloring of $G$ indicated in the figure shows that $\chi''(G) \leq 7$. We show that $\chi''(G) = 7$. Assume, to the contrary, that $\chi''(G) = 6$. Thus, there exists a 6-total coloring of $G$. No color can be assigned to more than two vertices. Suppose that some color is assigned to two vertices. Then these vertices must be $x$ and $z$. This color can be assigned to at most two edges and so this color can be used for at most four vertices and edges. If some color is assigned to one vertex, then this color can be assigned to at most two edges; while if some color is assigned to no vertices, then this color can be assigned to at most three edges. Therefore, the total number of vertices and edges that can be colored with six colors cannot exceed $4 + 5 \cdot 3 = 19$. However, since $n + m = 6 + 14 = 20$, this is a contradiction. Thus, as claimed, $\chi''(G) = 7$.
