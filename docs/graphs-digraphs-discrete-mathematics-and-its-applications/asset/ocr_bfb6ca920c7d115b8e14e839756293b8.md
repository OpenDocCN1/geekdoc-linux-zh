# Crossing Numbers of Cartesian Products of Graphs

Other graphs whose crossing numbers have been investigated with little success are the n-cubes Q_n. Since Q_n is planar for n = 1, 2, 3, cr(Q_n) = 0 for each such n. Roger Eggleton and Richard Guy [76] have shown that cr(Q_4) = 8 but cr(Q_n) is unknown for n ≥ 5. One might observe that

Q_4 = K_2 □ K_2 □ K_2 □ K_2 = C_4 □ C_4

so that cr(C_4 □ C_4) = 8. This raises the question of determining cr(C_s □ C_t) for s, t ≥ 3. For the case s = t = 3, Frank Harary, Paul Kainen and Allen Schwenk [120] showed that cr(C_3 □ C_3) = 3. Their proof consisted of the following three steps:

+   **Step 1.** Exhibiting a drawing of C_3 □ C_3 with three crossings so that cr(C_3 □ C_3) ≤ 3.

+   **Step 2.** Showing that C_3 □ C_3 - e is nonplanar for every edge e of C_3 □ C_3 so that cr(C_3 □ C_3) ≥ 2.

+   **Step 3.** Showing, by case exhausion, that it is impossible to have a drawing of C_3 □ C_3 with exactly two crossings so that cr(C_3 □ C_3) ≥ 3 (see Exercise 6).

Richard D. Ringeisen and Lowell W. Beineke [200] then significantly extended this result by determining cr(C_3 □ C_t) for all integers t ≥ 3.

> **Theorem 11.6** For each integer t ≥ 3, cr(C_3 □ C_t) = t.

**Proof.** We label the vertices of C_3 □ C_t by the 3t ordered pairs (0,j), (1,j) and (2,j), where j = 0,1,...,t - 1, and, for convenience, we let

u_j = (0,j), v_j = (1,j) and w_j = (2,j).

First, we note that cr(C_3 □ C_t) ≤ t. This observation follows from the fact that there exists a drawing of C_3 □ C_t with t crossings. A drawing of C_3 □ C_4 with four crossings is shown in Figure 11.5. Drawings of C_3 □ C_t with t crossings for other values of t can be given similarly.
