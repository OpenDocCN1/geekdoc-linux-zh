
so $k_{o}(G'-S')\le |S'|$. Hence, we may assume that $S'$ contains all $p$ vertices of $K_{p}$. Let $S=S'-V(K_{p})$. So, $|S'|=|S|+p$ and $G'-S'=G-S$. Consequently, by (12.2),

$$k_{o}(G'-S') = k_{o}(G-S) \leq |S| + p = |S'|.$$

Therefore, by Tutte’s theorem, $G'$ has a 1-factor and thus a perfect matching. Deleting the $p$ vertices of $K_{p}$ from $G'$ produces a matching in $G$ of size at least

$$
\begin{eqnarray*}
\frac{n'}{2} - p &=& \frac{n+p}{2} - p = \frac{n-p}{2} \\
&=& \frac{1}{2}\bigg( n - \max_{S \subset V(G)} \{ k_{o}(G-S) - |S| \}\bigg) \\
&=& \min_{S \subset V(G)} \bigg\{ \frac{n + |S| - k_{o}(G-S)}{2} \bigg\}
\end{eqnarray*}
$$

and so

$$\alpha'(G) \ge \min_{S \subset V(G)} \bigg\{ \frac{n + |S| - k_{o}(G-S)}{2} \bigg\}.$$

Therefore, (12.1) holds.

## Vertex Covers and Edge Covers

A vertex and an edge are said to **cover each other** in a graph $G$ if they are incident in $G$. A **vertex cover** in $G$ is a set of vertices that covers all the edges of $G$. An **edge cover** in a graph $G$ without isolated vertices is a set of edges that covers all the vertices of $G$. The minimum cardinality of a vertex cover in a graph $G$ is called the **vertex covering number** of $G$ and is denoted by $\beta(G)$. As expected, the **edge covering number** $\beta'(G)$ of a graph $G$ (without isolated vertices) is the minimum cardinality of an edge cover in $G$.

For $s\le t$, we have $\beta(K_{s,t})=s$ and $\beta'(K_{s,t})=t$. Recall that the (vertex) independence number $\alpha(G)$ of $G$ is the maximum cardinality among the independent sets of vertices in $G$. We have already seen that $\alpha(K_{s,t})=t$. An independent set $S$ of vertices in a graph $G$ is **maximal independent** if $S$ is not a proper subset of any other independent set of vertices in $G$. Therefore, there are two maximal independent sets of vertices in $K_{s,t}$, one with $s$ vertices and the other with $t$ vertices, namely the two partite sets of $K_{s,t}$. Furthermore, $\alpha'(K_{s,t})=s$. As another illustration of these four parameters, we note that for $n\ge 2$, $\alpha(K_{n})=1$, $\alpha'(K_{n})=\lceil n/2\rceil$, $\beta(K_{n})=n-1$ and $\beta'(K_{n})=\lceil n/2\rceil$. The independence and covering concepts are summarized below.

| Parameter | Name | Description |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\alpha(G)$ | vertex independence number | maximum number of vertices, no two of which are adjacent |
| $\alpha'(G)$ | edge independence number | maximum number of edges, no two of which are adjacent |
| $\beta(G)$ | vertex covering number | minimum number of vertices that cover the edges of $G$ |
| $\beta'(G)$ | edge covering number | minimum number of edges that cover the vertices of $G$ |

## Gallai’s Theorem

Observe that for the two graphs $G$ of order $n$ considered above, namely $K_{s,t}$ with $n = s + t$ and $K_n$, we have

$$\alpha(G) + \beta(G) = \alpha'(G) + \beta'(G) = n.$$

These two examples illustrate the next theorem [99], due to the Hungarian mathematician Tibor Gallai (1912–1992).

Gallai was a winner of national mathematics competitions, along with Paul Erdős and Paul Turán, and became lifelong friends of both. As a consequence of his accomplishments, Gallai was admitted to Pázmány University in Budapest. He was one of a group of enthusiastic students in the 1930s in Budapest that included Erdős, Turán, George Szekeres and Esther Klein. Some of these students attended the graph theory course given by Dénes König, who was a professor at the Technical University of Budapest. This was to have a profound effect on Gallai’s mathematical interests. Gallai, who was König’s only doctoral student, helped König with his graph theory book and König mentioned some of Gallai’s results in the book and used other ideas of Gallai. Many of Gallai’s contributions to graph theory were to prove fundamental to the subject and aided in the rapid development of graph theory and combinatorics. For example, he was among the first to recognize the importance of so-called mini-max theorems (such as Menger’s theorem).

Gallai was an exceptionally modest individual and rarely made public appearances or attended conferences. In fact, much of his work became known only because of the efforts of his students. While Gallai was quick to praise the work of others, he often underestimated the merits of his own contributions, even though he had important results in many areas of graph theory. Consequently, he was notoriously slow to publish his own results. Several of his results went unpublished, only later to be independently rediscovered (and published) by others.

### Theorem 12.11 (Gallai’s Theorem)

If $G$ is a graph of order $n$ having no isolated vertices, then

$$\alpha(G) + \beta(G) = n \quad (12.3)$$

and

$$\alpha^{\prime}(G) + \beta^{\prime}(G) = n. \quad (12.4)$$

**Proof.** We begin with (12.3). Let $U$ be an independent set of vertices of $G$ with $|U| = \alpha(G)$. Then the set $V(G) - U$ is a vertex cover in $G$. Therefore, $\beta(G) \le n - \alpha(G)$. If, however, $W$ is a set of $\beta(G)$ vertices that covers all edges of $G$, then $V(G) - W$ is independent; thus $\alpha(G) \ge n - \beta(G)$. This proves (12.3).

Next, we verify (12.4). Let $E_{1}$ be a maximum independent set of edges of $G$ and so $|E_{1}| = \alpha^{\prime}(G)$. Then $E_{1}$ covers $2\alpha^{\prime}(G)$ vertices of $G$. The remaining $n - 2\alpha^{\prime}(G)$ vertices can be covered by $n - 2\alpha^{\prime}(G)$ edges not in $E_{1}$. Thus, $\beta^{\prime}(G) \le \alpha^{\prime}(G) + (n - 2\alpha^{\prime}(G)) = n - \alpha^{\prime}(G)$ so $\alpha^{\prime}(G) + \beta^{\prime}(G) \le n$.

Next, let $E^{\prime}$ be an edge cover in $G$ with $|E^{\prime}| = \beta^{\prime}(G)$. The minimality of $E^{\prime}$ implies that each component of $G[E^{\prime}]$ is a tree. Select from each component of $G[E^{\prime}]$ one edge, denoting the resulting set of edges by $E^{\prime\prime}$. Then $E^{\prime\prime}$ is independent and so $|E^{\prime\prime}| \le \alpha^{\prime}(G)$. If $G[E^{\prime}]$ is a forest with $k$ components, then, by Corollary 3.16, the size of $G[E^{\prime}]$ is $n - k$. Thus,

$$\alpha^{\prime}(G) + \beta^{\prime}(G) \ge |E^{\prime\prime}| + |E^{\prime}| = k + (n - k) = n,$$

completing the proof of (12.4) and the theorem. ▪

An elementary relationship involving the independence and covering numbers is stated next.

### Theorem 12.12

If $G$ is a graph having no isolated vertices, then

$$\beta(G) \ge \alpha^{\prime}(G) \quad \text{and} \quad \beta^{\prime}(G) \ge \alpha(G).$$

**Proof.** Let $S$ be a vertex cover in $G$ and let $X$ be an independent set of edges with $|X| = \alpha^{\prime}(G)$. For each edge $e$ of $X$, there is a vertex $v_{e}$ in $S$ that is incident with $e$. Furthermore, for every two distinct edges $e$ and $f$ of $X$, the vertices $v_{e}$ and $v_{f}$ are distinct. Thus, $|S| \ge |X| = \alpha^{\prime}(G)$, which implies that $\beta(G) \ge \alpha^{\prime}(G)$.

For a graph $G$ of order $n$, it therefore follows by Gallai’s theorem (Theorem 12.11) that

$$n - \alpha(G) = \beta(G) \ge \alpha^{\prime}(G) = n - \beta^{\prime}(G)$$

and so $\beta^{\prime}(G) \ge \alpha(G)$. ▪

## The König–Egerváry Theorem

While $\beta(G) \ge \alpha^{\prime}(G)$ for every graph $G$, equality does not hold in general. If, however, $G$ is bipartite, then $\beta(G) = \alpha^{\prime}(G)$. This result was discovered independently by König [147] and Eugene Egerváry [75].

### Theorem 12.13 (The König–Egerváry Theorem)

If $G$ is a bipartite graph, then

$$\beta(G)=\alpha^{\prime}(G) .$$

**Proof.** Since $\beta(G) \geq \alpha^{\prime}(G)$, it suffices to show that $\beta(G) \leq \alpha^{\prime}(G)$. Let $U$ and $W$ be the bipartite sets of $G$ and let $M$ be a maximum matching in $G$. Then $\alpha^{\prime}(G)=|M|$. Denote by $A$ the set of all $M$-unmatched vertices in $U$. (If $A=\emptyset$, then the proof is complete.) Observe that $|M|=|U|-|A|$. Let $S$ be the set of all vertices of $G$ that are connected to some vertex in $A$ by an $M$-alternating path. Define $U^{\prime}=S \cap U$ and $W^{\prime}=S \cap W$.

As in the proof of Hall's theorem (Theorem 12.3), we have that $U^{\prime}-A$ is matched to $W^{\prime}$ and that $N(U^{\prime})=W^{\prime}$. Since $U^{\prime}-A$ is matched to $W^{\prime}$, it follows that $|U^{\prime}|-|W^{\prime}|=|A|$.

Observe that $C=(U-(U^{\prime})) \cup W^{\prime}$ is a vertex cover in $G$; for otherwise, there is an edge $vw$ in $G$ such that $v \in U^{\prime}$ and $w \notin W^{\prime}$. Furthermore,

$$|C|=|U|-|U^{\prime}|+|W^{\prime}|=|U|-|A|=|M| .$$

Therefore, $\beta(G) \leq|C|=|M|=\alpha^{\prime}(G)$ and the proof is complete.

As a consequence of the König–Egerváry theorem (Theorem 12.13) and Hall's theorem (Theorem 12.3), we have the following result of König and Richard Rado [192].

### Theorem 12.14 (The König–Rado Theorem)

If $G$ is a bipartite graph without isolated vertices, then

$$\alpha(G)=\beta^{\prime}(G) .$$

**Proof.** By Theorems 12.11 and 12.13,

$$\alpha(G)+\beta(G)=\alpha^{\prime}(G)+\beta^{\prime}(G) \text { and } \beta(G)=\alpha^{\prime}(G) .$$

Thus $\alpha(G)=\beta^{\prime}(G)$.

## 12.4 Domination

An area of graph theory that has received increased attention during recent decades is that of domination in graphs. A vertex $v$ in a graph $G$ is said to **dominate** itself and each of its neighbors, that is, $v$ dominates the vertices in its closed neighborhood $N[v]$. A set $S$ of vertices of $G$ is a **dominating set** of $G$ if every vertex of $G$ is dominated by at least one vertex of $S$. Equivalently, a set $S$ of vertices of $G$ is a dominating set if every vertex in $V(G)-S$ is adjacent to at least one vertex in $S$. The minimum cardinality among the dominating sets of $G$ is called the **domination number** of $G$ and is denoted by $\gamma(G)$.
