
## 21.1. THE PROBABILISTIC METHOD

Many of the proofs of this chapter depend on results from basic set theory and calculus. We first review the necessary set theory. For events $A$ and $B$, $A \cup B$ is the event that $A$ or $B$ (or both) occurs. It follows that

$$P[A \cup B] = P[A] + P[B] - P[A \cap B] \le P[A] + P[B].$$

More generally, for events $A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_k$, $k \ge 2$, we have

$$P[\cup_{i=1}^k A_i] \le \sum_{i=1}^k P[A_i].$$

For events $A$ and $B$, De Morgan’s laws for sets give us that

$$\overline{A \cup B} = \overline{A} \cap \overline{B} \text{ and } \overline{A \cap B} = \overline{A} \cup \overline{B}.$$

More generally, for events $A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_k$, $k \ge 2$,

$$\cup_{i=1}^k \overline{A_i} = \overline{\cap_{i=1}^k A_i} \text{ and } \cap_{i=1}^k \overline{A_i} = \overline{\cup_{i=1}^k A_i}.$$

Consequently

$$P[\cup_{i=1}^k A_i] = P[\cap_{i=1}^k \overline{A_i}] \text{ and } P[\cap_{i=1}^k A_i] = P[\cup_{i=1}^k \overline{A_i}].$$

When using the probabilistic method (described in this section) or defining a model for a random graph (described in Section 21.2), we generally need to bound the probability $P[A]$ of an event $A$. Most often, we wish to show that $P[A] > 0$ or $P[A] < 1$. There are some results from calculus that are used repeatedly in this chapter to establish such bounds, including:

1.  $1 - p < e^{-p}$ for a positive real number $p$ with $p < 1$  (21.1)
2.  $\lim_{n \to \infty} n^\alpha \beta^n = 0$ for positive real numbers $\alpha$ and $\beta < 1$  (21.2)
3.  $\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\ln n}{n^\alpha} = 0$ for a positive real number $\alpha$.  (21.3)

We now illustrate the probabilistic method in graph theory by giving several examples from a number of different areas of graph theory.

### Ramsey Numbers

In Chapter 20, it was shown for every integer $t \geq 3$ that $\lceil 2^{t/2} \rceil$ is a strict lower bound for the Ramsey number $R(t, t) = R(K_t, K_t)$. This was done by showing the existence of a graph $G$ of order $n = \lceil 2^{t/2} \rceil$ such that neither $G$ nor $\overline{G}$ contains $K_t$ as a subgraph. This fact was established by counting the number of different labeled graphs of order $n$ that contain a subgraph isomorphic to $K_t$, together with the complements of these graphs, and showing that there are fewer than $2^{\binom{n}{2}}$ of these graphs. Since the number of different labeled graphs of order $n$ is $2^{\binom{n}{2}}$, it follows that there *exists* a graph $G$ of order $n$ such that neither $G$ nor $\overline{G}$ contains $K_t$ as a subgraph, even though no such a graph $G$ was ever exhibited. This, however, shows that $R(t, t) > \lceil 2^{t/2} \rceil$.

Recall that a red-blue coloring of a graph $G$ is an edge coloring of $G$ where every edge of $G$ is colored red or blue (and where two adjacent edges may be assigned the same color). The next proof establishes, for every integer $t \geq 3$ and $n = \lceil 2^{t/2} \rceil$, the existence of a red-blue coloring of $K_n$ having no monochromatic $K_t$. This implies that $R(t, t) > \lceil 2^{t/2} \rceil$.

In the proof of the following result, it will be convenient to employ the inequality

$$2^{t^2/2} < \left( \frac{t}{2} \right)! \, t \, 2^{t/2}, \qquad (21.4)$$

which holds for every integer $t \geq 3$ and which can be verified by induction.

**Theorem 21.1** For every integer $t \geq 3$,

$$R(t, t) > \lceil 2^{t/2} \rceil.$$

**Proof.** Let $n = \lceil 2^{t/2} \rceil$. We show that there is a red-blue coloring of $K_n$ that contains no monochromatic $K_t$, that is, that it is neither a red $K_t$ nor a blue $K_t$.

Here, the probability space $S$ is the set of red-blue colorings of $K_n$, where $V(K_n) = \{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n\}$. The probabilities are defined by setting

$$P[v_i v_j \text{ is red}] = P[v_i v_j \text{ is blue}] = \frac{1}{2}$$

for each pair $v_i, v_j$ of distinct vertices of $K_n$ and letting these events be mutually independent. Therefore, each of the $2^{\binom{n}{2}}$ red-blue colorings of $K_n$ is equally likely with probability $(\frac{1}{2})^{\binom{n}{2}}$.

For a fixed $t$-element subset $T$ of $V(K_n)$, let $A_T$ denote the event that the subgraph of $K_n$ induced by $T$ is either a red $K_t$ or a blue $K_t$. The probability that the subgraph of $K_n$ induced by $T$ is a red $K_t$ is $(\frac{1}{2})^{\binom{t}{2}}$ since each of the $\binom{t}{2}$ edges joining two vertices of $T$ must be red. Similarly, the probability that the subgraph of $K_n$ induced by $T$ is a blue $K_t$ is $(\frac{1}{2})^{\binom{t}{2}}$. Consequently, the probability that the subgraph of $K_n$ induced by $T$ is either a red $K_t$ or a blue $K_t$ is

$$P[A_T]=\biggl(\frac{1}{2}\biggr)^{\binom{t}{2}}+\biggl(\frac{1}{2}\biggr)^{\binom{t}{2}}=2^{1-\binom{t}{2}}.$$

Next, let $\mathcal{T}$ be the set of all $\binom{n}{t}$ $t$-element subsets of $V(K_n)$ and consider the event $\cup_{T\in\mathcal{T}} A_T$. Then

$$P\biggl[\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{T}}A_T\biggl]\le\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}P[A_T]=\binom{n}{t}2^{1-\binom{t}{2}}
\qquad\quad=\frac{n!}{t!(n-t)!}\biggl(2^{1-\binom{t}{2}}\biggr)\le\frac{n^t}{t!}\biggl(2^{1-\binom{t}{2}}\biggr).$$

Since $n=\lceil 2^{t/2}\rceil\le2^{t/2}$, it follows that $n^t\le2^{t^2/2}$. Moreover, since $t\ge3$, it follows by (21.4) that

$$2^{t^2/2}<\biggl(\frac{1}{2}\biggr)t!\:2^{\binom{t}{2}},$$

Therefore,

$$P\biggl[\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{T}}A_T\biggl]<\frac{n^t}{t!}\biggl(2^{1-\binom{t}{2}}\biggr)\le\frac{2^{t^2/2}}{t!}\biggl(2^{1-\binom{t}{2}}\biggr)
\qquad\quad<\biggl(\frac{1}{2}\biggr)2^{\binom{t}{2}}\biggl(2^{1-\binom{t}{2}}\biggr)=1.$$

Since $P\bigl[\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{T}} A_T\bigr]<1$, it follows that $P\bigl[\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\overline{A_T}\bigr]>0$ and so

$$P\biggl[\bigcap_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\overline{A_T}\biggl]>0.$$

Hence, $\bigcap_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\overline{A_T}\ne\varnothing$ and therefore some element of the probability space $S$ belongs to $\bigcap_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\overline{A_T}$, implying that there is a red-blue coloring of $K_n$ with no monochromatic $K_t$.

The proof of Theorem 21.1 illustrates a basic technique employed in the probabilistic method. An appropriate probability function is defined on a set of objects. The set of objects in this proof was the set of red-blue colorings of $K_n$. An event $A$ is then defined representing the desired structure. In the proof of Theorem 21.1, we wanted to show the existence of a red-blue coloring of $K_n$ that contains no monochromatic $K_t$. Thus, the desired event here is $A=\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\overline{A_T}$. By showing that $P[A]>0$, we conclude that $A\ne\varnothing$, that is, there exists a red-blue coloring of $K_n$ with no monochromatic $K_t$. In general, our goal is to show that $P[A]>0$ so that we can conclude then that an object with the desired characteristics exists.

As mentioned earlier, this proof technique is generally credited to Erdős. Using a proof similar to that given in Theorem 21.1 but with more careful approximations, he actually showed that

$$R(t,t) \ge \bigg(\frac{t}{e}\bigg)^{2^{\frac{t-1}{2}}}.$$

In 1975, Joel Spencer [223] proved that this bound can be improved by a factor of 2 with the aid of the Lovász local lemma [224, p. 57]. The bound obtained by Spencer remains the best lower bound for $R(t,t)$ currently known.

### List Colorings

We saw in Theorem 15.18 that if $r$ and $k$ are positive integers such that $r \ge \binom{2k-1}{k}$, then $\chi_{\ell}(K_{r,r}) \ge k+1$. Here, we use the probabilistic method to verify that $\lfloor 2\log_2 r \rfloor$ is an upper bound for $\chi_{\ell}(K_{r,r})$, a result initially stated without proof in Theorem 15.19.

**Theorem 21.2** For every integer $r \ge 3$,

$$\chi_{\ell}(K_{r,r}) \le \lfloor 2\log_2 r \rfloor.$$

**Proof.** Let $U_0$ and $U_1$ denote the partite sets of $K_{r,r}$, where then $|U_0| = |U_1| = r$. For each vertex $v$ of $K_{r,r}$, let $L(v)$ denote a list of $\lfloor 2\log_2 r \rfloor$ colors that are allowable for $v$. Let $L = \bigcup L(v)$, where the union is taken over all vertices $v$ of $K_{r,r}$. Thus, $L$ is the set of all colors under consideration. Suppose that $|L| = n$. We now show that there is a proper coloring of $K_{r,r}$ where each vertex $v$ is assigned a color from its list $L(v)$.

For each of the $2^n$ subsets $L_0$ of $L$, let $L_1 = L - L_0$. Suppose that there is a subset $L_0$ of $L$ such that

$$L_0 \cap L(u) \ne \emptyset \text{ for each } u \in U_0 \quad (21.5)$$

and

$$L_1 \cap L(w) \ne \emptyset \text{ for each } w \in U_1 \quad (21.6)$$

In such a case, each vertex $u_0 \in U_0$ is assigned a color in $L_0 \cap L(u_0)$ and each vertex $u_1 \in U_1$ is assigned a color in $L_1 \cap L(u_1)$. Because $L_0 \cap L_1 = \emptyset$, this results in a proper coloring of $K_{r,r}$ and so $\chi_{\ell}(K_{r,r}) \le \lfloor 2\log_2 r \rfloor$.

We now use the probabilistic method to show that there is, in fact, a subset $L_0$ of $L$ satisfying (21.5) and (21.6).

Let $S$ be the probability space consisting of all pairs $L_0, L_1$ of such subsets of $L$. The probability of a color $c \in L$ belonging to $L_0$ and $L_1$ is defined by setting

$$P[c \in L_0] = P[c \in L_1] = \frac{1}{2},$$
