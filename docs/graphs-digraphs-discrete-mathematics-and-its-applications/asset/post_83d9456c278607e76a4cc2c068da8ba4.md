# Automorphisms and Reconstruction

Determining whether a given graph possesses a property of interest depends on its structure. One way of studying the structure of graphs is by investigating their symmetries. A common method of doing this is by means of groups. A problem concerning how much of the structure of a graph can be determined from certain subgraphs of a graph is also described.

## 9.1 The Automorphism Group of a Graph

An **automorphism** of a graph $G$ is an isomorphism from $G$ to itself. Thus, an automorphism of $G$ is a permutation of $V(G)$ that preserves adjacency (and non-adjacency). Of course, the identity function $\epsilon$ on $V(G)$ is an automorphism of $G$. The inverse of an automorphism of $G$ is also an automorphism of $G$, as is the composition of two automorphisms of $G$. These observations lead us to the fact that the set of all automorphisms of a graph $G$ form a group (under the operation of composition), called the **automorphism group** or simply the **group** of $G$, which is denoted by Aut($G$).

The automorphism group of the graph $G_1$ of Figure 9.1 is cyclic of order 2, which we write as Aut($G_1$) $\cong \mathbb{Z}_2$ (the group of integers modulo 2). In addition to the identity permutation on $V(G_1)$, the group Aut($G_1$) contains the *reflection* $\alpha = \langle uy\rangle\langle vx\rangle$, where $\alpha$ is expressed in terms of *permutation cycles*. That is,

$\alpha(u) = y, \alpha(v) = x, \alpha(w) = w, \alpha(x) = v, \alpha(y) = u$.

The graph $G_2$ of Figure 9.1 of order 6 has only the identity automorphism and so Aut($G_2$) $\cong \mathbb{Z}_1$. In fact, 6 is the smallest order of a nontrivial graph whose automorphism group consists only of the identity automorphism.

Every permutation of the vertex set of $K_n$ is an automorphism and so Aut($K_n$) is the symmetric group $S_n$ of order $n!$. It is known that the symmetric group $S_n$ is nonabelian only when $n \geq 3$. In fact, $S_3$ is the smallest nonabelian group. The complete graph $K_3$ is shown in Figure 9.2 along with the six elements of Aut($K_3$). Here, $e$ is the identity, $\alpha_1 = (u\ v)$ and $\alpha_2 = (u\ w\ v)$ are rotations, and $\beta_1 = (v\ w)$, $\beta_2 = (u\ w)$ and $\beta_3 = (u\ v)$ are reflections. The group table of Aut($K_3$) is also shown in Figure 9.2. Associated with the group Aut($K_3$) is a graph $G$ of order 6 where $V(G) = \text{Aut}(K_3)$ such that two vertices $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ of $G$ are adjacent if and only if $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ commute (that is, $\gamma_1\gamma_2 = \gamma_2\gamma_1$ in Aut($K_3$)). In $\overline{G}$ then, $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ are adjacent if and only if $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ do not commute. That is, $G$ models commutativity in Aut($K_3$) and $\overline{G}$ models non-commutativity.

**Theorem 9.1** *For every graph $G$, $\mathrm{Aut}(G) \cong \mathrm{Aut}(\overline{G})$.*

We mentioned previously that $\mathrm{Aut}(K_n) \cong S_n$ for every positive integer $n$. Certainly, if $G$ is a graph of order $n$ containing adjacent vertices as well as nonadjacent vertices, then $\mathrm{Aut}(G)$ is isomorphic to a proper subgroup of the symmetric group $S_n$. Combining this observation with Theorem 9.1 and Lagrange’s Theorem on the order of a subgroup of a finite group (which states that the order of a subgroup of a finite group divides the order of the group), we arrive at the following.

**Theorem 9.2** *The order $|\mathrm{Aut}(G)|$ of the automorphism group of a graph $G$ of order $n$ is a divisor of $n!$ and equals $n!$ if and only if $G = \overline{K}_n$ or $G = K_n$.*

Recall that two labelings of a graph $G$ of order $n$ from the same set of $n$ labels are considered distinct if they do not produce the same edge set. With the aid of the automorphism group of a graph $G$ of order $n$, it is possible to determine the number of distinct labelings of $G$.

**Theorem 9.3** *The number of distinct labelings of a graph $G$ of order $n$ from a given set of $n$ labels is $n! / |\mathrm{Aut}(G)|$.*

**Proof.** Let $S$ be a set of $n$ labels. Certainly, there exist $n!$ labelings of $G$ using the elements of $S$ without regard to which labelings are distinct. For a given labeling of $G$, each automorphism of $G$ gives rise to an identical labeling of $G$; that is, each labeling of $G$ from $S$ determines $|\mathrm{Aut}(G)|$ identical labelings of $G$. Hence, there are $n! / |\mathrm{Aut}(G)|$ distinct labelings of $G$. $\blacksquare$

As an illustration of Theorem 9.3, consider the graph $G = P_3$ of Figure 9.4 and the set $S = \{1, 2, 3\}$. Since $\mathrm{Aut}(G) \cong Z_2$, the number of distinct labelings of $G$ is $3! / 2 = 3$. The three distinct labelings of $G$ from the set $\{1, 2, 3\}$ are shown in Figure 9.4.
