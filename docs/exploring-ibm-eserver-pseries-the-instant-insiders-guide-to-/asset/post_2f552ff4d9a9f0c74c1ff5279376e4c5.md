## Virtual Private Networks

With the pervasiveness of the Internet, one choice for a company’s communications with customers, employees, and suppliers is simply to use the Internet. Most often, some security measures will be used, such as password protection and routers with firewall and network address translation capability. Such an arrangement may be adequate for routine communications, such as e-mail and non-confidential data transmissions. However, if the customer or business data to be communicated is sensitive—data that could be used by an “eavesdropper” to steal proprietary information or cause denial of service attacks—then there are security concerns that must be considered.

One alternative is the implementation of a private TCP/IP-based private-line network, in which the communications are controlled by the company and probably operated by a telecommunications provider. The previous two examples of distributed communications (Figures 4.2 and 4.3) showed a lease as the link between two locations, and Figure 4.2 is a leased-line network using the TCP/IP protocol.

But rather than incur the costs of a private-line network using leased telecommunications facilities, a company may choose to implement a Virtual Private Network (VPN). A VPN refers to the use of the Internet (or any public network, for that matter) as the vehicle for implementing a private network or extension of a company’s “private” in the sense that the data flowing through it is private. But since a VPN operates over a public network (i.e., the Internet), it is really no “private” in the physical sense. And, it is a “virtual” network because it has to operate over any physical infrastructure.

Thus, the VPN is “virtual” (it is not permanently associated with any specific set of physical facilities), “private” (protocols and encryption protect the company’s data from others), and a “network” (it interfaces and extends the intranet network in a well-structured way that is transparent to the computers at each end).

There are many trade-offs to be considered in designing a VPN. First, there are security concerns, which involve both the transmission across the public Internet and the internal security policies and facilities at the company and remote sites. Next, there is performance. In particular, encryption and transmission logging take a lot of processor power and storage. Service-level agreements and methodologies must be established with ISP. Another consideration is the people involved. Does the VPN provide easy-to-use communications, and are the users trained and instructed with regard to security policy? Finally, there is the question of initial and recurring costs. What is the value to a specific company of various levels of security, performance, and responsiveness in a proposed VPN? There is no single answer for every situation, but a competent networking consultant can help a company make cost/benefit decisions.

VPNs are not the private province of any one manufacturer. In fact, the VPN concept is designed to allow implementation across a heterogeneous set of servers, routers, and communications links. In summary, pSeries servers and AIX 5L operating system provide the features (e.g., Ethernet adapters, cryptographic coprocessors, and accelerators) and the networking software that conform to industry standards allows all users to integrate pSeries servers into cost-effective and secure VPNs.
