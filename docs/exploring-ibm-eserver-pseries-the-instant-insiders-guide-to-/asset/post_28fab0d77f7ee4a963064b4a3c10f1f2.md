When a controlling device, called an initiator (e.g., the SCSI I/O controller), wants to perform an information transfer with another device (e.g., a disk unit), called a target, the initiator arbitrates for control of the SCSI bus. Once the initiator has control of the bus, it issues one of the commands defined in the SCSI standard protocol. While the target device processes the command, the SCSI bus is available for any other SCSI bus traffic. When the target device is ready, it gains control of the bus and supplies the requested information along with a completion status indicator to the original initiator.

One of the primary differences between SCSI and earlier disk interfaces is that each SCSI device must have local processing capability to participate in the SCSI command protocol. That is, each SCSI device attached to the bus is responsible for much of its own processing (e.g., error checking, error correction, and retry of failed operations). Earlier disk interface standards put the responsibility for managing the various devices in a centralized I/O controller rather than distributing the responsibility among the connecting devices.

The advantage of the SCSI approach is that work (e.g., error correction and error retries) is distributed over the processing capabilities of all SCSI devices, which off load the main controller system and put the work where it can be done more efficiently. Also, activities such as the read-ahead function used in pSeries systems can be implemented completely in the disk unit itself, making it transparent to the pSeries systems and the SCSI I/O controller (“Read-ahead” means that the SCSI disk unit loads extra information from the disk platter to a buffer area in anticipation of the next request from the SCSI I/O controller.) When the next request comes, if the needed information is already available in the buffer area, it can be sent to the SCSI I/O controller immediately, eliminating the mechanical delays associated with disk seek time, latency, and so on. The result is highly efficient transfer of information between the SCSI I/O controller and the disk units.

Although we have used the term “SCSI” in this discussion, the reader will notice terms like “Ultra-SCSI” and “Ultra3-SCSI” used in descriptions of pSeries systems. Multiple versions of SCSI are used with pSeries systems. The maximum instantaneous transfer rate over the SCSI bus is 4 Mbps, whereas SCSI-2 runs at 10 or 20 Mbps. The SCSI standards provide three alternative, incompatible, electrical bus interface configurations: single-ended, differential, and a new, lower power version of differential called low-voltage differential, or LVD.

High-performance disk units and efficient SCSI interface implementations can provide high information transfer rates between the disk units and the SCSI controller. However, the information transferred must flow between the pSeries system and the SCSI controller. Efficient transfer between the pSeries and the SCSI controller is just as important as disk seek times and SCSI bus rates. Finally, main memory in pSeries systems acts like a giant disk cache because of the implementation of single-level storage (discussed earlier in this chapter). In the spirit of the pSeries system's instruction and data cache is a temporary holding area that contains information likely to be requested by the active program. When the requested information is in the disk cache, delays associated with a traditional I/O access are eliminated, dramatically improving system performance. Single-level storage reduces the need for a disk cache on the SCSI I/O controller, reducing costs and eliminating I/O delays associated with such a disk cache. ## Serial Storage Architecture Disk Storage

In 1995, IBM announced its second generation of serial disks. The architecture was dubbed simply enough Serial Storage Architecture (SSA). Serial Storage Architecture has cost and power requirements that are similar to those of SCSI, but does not require SCSI's discrete terminators or address switches. It supports up to 96 devices per adapter, whereas SCSI supports only 15, and allows 25 meters between devices on the loop. This gives much more flexibility than the 25-meter total bus length supported by SCSI. Fiber-optic extenders support device distances up to 10 km. The pSeries attaches to disk subsystems supporting SSA, such as the 7133 disk drawer or deskside.

Loops, in which the ends of the cables are attached either to the same adapter or to different adapters, are the most popular topology today. The signals on the loop rotate in both directions.

## Fibre Channel Arbitrated Loop and Storage Area Networks

In the early 1990s, IBM helped pioneer a networking technology called Fibre Channel (FCS), which was used to connect systems together using optical fibers. These connections allowed high bandwidth (up to 100 Mbps) and long distances (up to 10 km) between hosts and other processors or storage systems. Costs were high, however, and FCS saw limited acceptance.
