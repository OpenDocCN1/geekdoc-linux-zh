Data moves around the loop in a single direction, and much like a shared bus, only one activity occurs on the loop at a single point in time. To transfer data, the initiating device must get control of the loop by arbitration, much like SCSI. FC-AL sends bursts of data at 100 Mbps around the loop.

In response to competition to SSA, the FC-AL community added a second, redundant loop to the FC-AL definition. This second loop attaches to the same devices as the first loop and, along with additional switching circuitry, eliminates the single point of failure that is inherent in a single loop. Also, performance improvements can be achieved with a second loop. However, because of the shared, arbitrated nature of FC-AL, questions remain about its ability to scale and perform as well as SSA in large, complex configurations. FC-AL disks are not used in pServers.

Just as SCSI had to evolve until it became a truly robust, multivendor architecture, Fibre Channel networks are going through similar "growing pains." ## Tape Storage

The next type of storage we will cover is magnetic tape, or simply tape. One primary use of tape storage is to keep a backup copy of the information stored on the computer's disks. In the event of a disk failure, the backup tape can be used to restore the lost information to a new disk unit. The low cost and high recording densities inherent in tape also make it ideal for archiving information to which immediate access is no longer needed. Tape can also be used for distributing programs or transferring information from one computer system to another.

Tape consists of a long flexible strip coated with magnetic material rolled on a reel and placed in a cartridge. In addition to industry-standard half-inch tape reels, there are multiple types of cartridges used with pSeries systems: the 3-inch tape cartridge, the 4-mm tape cartridge, the 8-mm tape cartridge, VXA tape, digital linear tape (DLT), Ultrium tape, and 2-inch Magstar tape. Some of these tape technologies are available for installation in the internal media bays of pSeries systems, others are available only as external tape units.

In each case, a tape unit reads and writes information on the tape much as a cassette recorder records and plays music on audio cassette tapes. The tape unit runs the tape across the read/write head, which is in contact with the tape surface. Electrical impulses in the read/write head are used to transfer information to and from the tape surface. Chapter 2 will explore some of the tape units used with pSeries systems.

## Optical Storage

The final type of storage device we will discuss is optical storage. Optical uses light and a reflective surface to store information. There are two basic types of optical storage used with pSeries systems: CD-ROM disk drives and rewritable optical disk drives. Let's briefly look at each type.

CD-ROM disk drives use the same technique to store information as audio CDs do. Rather than using magnetism, as with disks, and, and tape, CD-ROM systems use optical techniques to achieve much higher recording density. A single disk used in pSeries CD-ROM drives can hold over 600 MB of information. That's enough storage to hold over 300,000 sheets of computer (or a stack over 90 feet high). The optical disks used with pSeries systems are read only. That is, users can view the information but can't change it. The information is preordered on the disk using specialized equipment and distributed to users. The primary use of CD-ROM storage is to distribute large amounts of information in a convenient package. For example, the entire library of pSeries and AIX 5L user manuals are stored on a single CD-ROM disk. Not only is this much easier way to distribute and store manuals, but the electronic method of looking up information is much more convenient. Other potential uses for CD-ROM include distribution of program libraries, financial reports, operations manuals, phone directories, or any large (and relatively stable) database.

The extremely high storage capacity of CD-ROMs can be attributed to the technique used to store the information. When the CD-ROM is first recorded, a laser beam is used to burn tiny patterns on the reflective surface of an optical disk according to industry standards. By bouncing the low-power laser beam in the CD-ROM drive off the optical disk's surface, a series of mirrors and sensors can read back the information burned into the disk. Although the optical technology itself quite well limited speed (compared with disks) and inability to record information preclude using CD-ROM as normal disk storage. Most pSeries and RS/6000 systems have internal CD-ROM drives. For systems that do not have an internal CD-ROM drive, an external unit (7210 External CD-ROM) is available. These devices will be covered in Chapter 2. Also available on pSeries systems are DVD-ROM drives, which can read both CD-ROM formatted disks and DVD-ROM drives, including DVD-ROM drives.

On some pSeries systems, the user has choice of either CD-ROM or DVD-ROM. Rewritable optical disks use a similar technique to store information. ## Expansion Slots

The PCI is a local bus that was designed for high-speed traffic. Aside from the memory subsystem, the PCI is the primary backbone of the I/O. The middleman between the processor and the PCI slots is called a bridge. pSeries systems are designed with multiple separate buses for improved I/O throughput. These separate buses can communicate. The PCI bus is multiplexed, which means that some pins are used for more than one purpose, depending on the operation. This is a bit more difficult to design, but results in fewer pins on each component, meaning that the components are less costly to produce. The PCI bus was also designed from the start to have many autoconfiguration features. This allows new devices to be added without setting switches or having conflicts.

We have already seen that pSeries systems come standard with one or more Ultra-SCSI (or faster) ports. Other ports are provided for serial, parallel, Ethernet 10/100 Mbps or gigabit Ethernet, keyboard, mouse, and HMC interfaces. Ports are the control electronics and connectors accessible from outside the pSeries cores that provide a means of attaching external devices (such as storage, LANs, and printers) to the system without using one or more of the PCI slots.
