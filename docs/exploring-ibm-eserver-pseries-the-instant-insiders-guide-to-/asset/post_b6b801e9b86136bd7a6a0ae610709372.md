An increasingly important requirement in today's fast-paced business environment is the need for adding capacity to a company's computer system almost instantaneously, in order to meet rapidly changing or seasonal workloads. To help such "on-demand" requirements, some pSeries servers offer Capacity Upgrade Demand (CuOD), allowing processors and memory to be installed, but remain inactive until the user requires the additional system resources.

The most important consideration in choosing a server is whether the required application is available on the platform and operating system of choice. Other key factors to be weighed in selecting a system are the technical skills of a company's information technology professionals, the installation and support services available from the manufacturer or supplier, and the number of users to be supported. All these matters must be considered to determine which server and which operating system will yield the lowest overall cost in investment, software, and operating costs. The solution with the best-fitting application programs will normally provide the best results.

Since PCs run operating systems as the various Windows offerings, they are generally preferred as end-user systems for popular word-processing, spreadsheet, and Internet browser applications. Given today's business environment of people on the go, many users have laptop PCs and connect to centralized servers from home, hotel rooms, and airports—using wireless communications. In multiuser environments, many users—each are connected (typically by a local area network, or LAN) to servers that provide shared data, applications, and services such as printing and access to public or private networks. Hundreds of thousands of pSeries and RS/6000 systems have been sold. The UNIX server market is extremely competitive for a performance and price standpoint. If IBM announces a new machine, you can rest assured that other vendors will react by dropping their prices and/or announcing a new higher performing system as soon as they can—and vice versa.

# Choices for Your Needs

Now, how do you select the right pSeries hardware and software for your needs? It gets complex with the multitude of models, options, peripherals, and software products from which to choose. Every situation and application is unique. It is a good practice to consult trained specialists, such as a local server professional, an IBM representative, or an IBM Business Partner (independent companies who have been trained and authorized to sell pSeries servers). Here are a few questions and concepts that you might keep in mind. There are some example configurations in Chapter 5 as well.

- Is there a critical component to the system? Could this be an application, specialized adapter, or communications facility that might force your decision in some other areas. For example, older systems may not support a new adapter card.

- Is availability an issue? How long can the system be down, if ever? This will drive a number of decisions regarding the system(s), disk, communications, and software. High Availability Cluster Multiprocessing (HACMP), discussed in Chapter 4, can be a life in these situations.

- Will this system have a single primary user or will it be a server for multiple users?

- What capacity is required? You must determine what your low high marks are for memory, storage, and adapters. This is a very difficult undertaking, but application providers and other similar customers can help.

- What performance is required? Consider floating-point or integer (for database and traditional business applications)?

- What future growth is anticipated? If your business is growing or you foresee the use of a particular application increasing in the near term, it may be better to invest in a system that can grow with you. # Meet the Family

The IBM pSeries family is designed to address the computing needs of both high-performance computing (HPC) users and those who run general commercial applications. High-performance computing applications can be found in business, government, and academic environments in areas as statistical analysis, geological analysis, molecular chemistry simulations, artificial intelligence, and life sciences. The engineering/scientific user may perform such tasks as designing mechanical devices on the screen (computer-aided design, or CAD), simulating physical stresses to test the strength of the design (computer-aided engineering, or CAE), and then automatically generating the programming necessary to manufacture the part with numerical control tools (computer-aided manufacturing, or CAM). Many HPC applications are best implemented on shared servers or clusters of servers, with each user having a PC as his or her workstation.

If three-dimensional (3D) graphics capability is required for the application (in the case of automotive or aircraft design), then an IBM IntelliStation POWER 275 or 265, the pSeries 630 Model 6E4, or one with large display and specialized input devices, as the IBM Spaceball, should be selected as that user's personal workstation. These workstations are usually networked to allow the user community to share programs, data, and peripheral equipment.

Another type of environment addressed by pSeries computers is of traditional commercial applications found in business, government, and academic organizations. In these environments, a single pSeries server or cluster of servers is shared by multiple users, each working at a personal computer (PC), which is connected to the server on a local area network (LAN).

# Exploring IBM @server pSeries

The POWER architecture combines the concepts of the original RISC architecture with a sprinkling of more traditional concepts to create systems with optimum performance. The pSeries of servers, the third generation of RISC products, was introduced using the POWER3 architecture. The latest RISC generation—POWER4—was introduced in October 2001 on the pSeries 690 server, and this architecture has been followed by POWER4+ technology on the most recent pSeries models.
