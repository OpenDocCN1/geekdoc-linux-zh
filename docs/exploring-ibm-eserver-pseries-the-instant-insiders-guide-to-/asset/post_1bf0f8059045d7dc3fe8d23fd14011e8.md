## The Who, What, Why, and How of pSeries

Before going any further into the details of pSeries, it would be useful to look at who is using pSeries, what they are doing, why they chose it, and how. In general, the UNIX marketplace can be divided into two camps. One is high-performance computing (HPC) or scientific computing. These users require high-performance processing, high availability, and scalability. pSeries models are also used in areas such as 3D graphics, seismic modeling, and data analysis. The other primary camp is commercial. This group deals with business applications, such as database and transaction processing.

Although this book is about pSeries systems, it is important to understand how they differ from the PC family in order to make good buying decisions. In fact, because PCs can run the Linux operating system or versions of UNIX for Intel-based systems, many of the applications available for pSeries systems can also be on PCs.

Key differentiators between pSeries (or many other UNIX servers, for that matter) and PCs are performance and the robustness of the hardware and operating system. pSeries systems combine high-performance integer and floating-point processing power with very high memory and data bandwidth and extensive scalability for storage and communications. There are pSeries models suitable for the full range of environments—both workloads, as large-scale database and transaction processing, and engineering and scientific applications. The kinds of business-critical applications run on pSeries servers require high levels of reliability and system availability. PCs have been designed more for low cost and performance for personal productivity applications, games, and the like. It should be noted that Intelbased servers on the market have some of the system robustness features that are common on most UNIX servers, and the performance of some systems using the latest 64-bit Intel processors is excellent.
