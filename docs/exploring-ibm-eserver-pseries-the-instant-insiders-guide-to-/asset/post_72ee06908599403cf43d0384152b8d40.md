The pSeries family uses the AIX 5L operating system, IBM's version of the UNIX operating system. In AIX 5L, IBM has combined the basic functions of the UNIX operating system with many enhancements, both developed by IBM and those designed by other companies and academic institutions. AIX 5L adheres to many industry standards that have been developed to make systems from various vendors more compatible and to facilitate interchange between different manufacturers.

Because the AIX 5L operating system conforms to many industry standards, the pSeries family is considered an open system. This means that the AIX 5L operating system conforms to standards (programming interfaces, communications protocols, and so on) defined by independent standards rather than using an IBM proprietary standards not generally adhered to by other computer manufacturers. The advantage of the open-system strategy comes when an independent development company writes an application program conforming to these industry standards. Because many computer manufacturers of open-system computers can offer its application program on many brands of computers. The advantages of open systems to computer users are that (1) after selecting the application program that meets their needs, they have more flexibility as to which brand of open-system computer to buy, (2) after users have purchased the large body of open application programs they can choose to meet new needs as they emerge, and (3) users have the flexibility to purchase multiple open-system computers of different brands and know that they will be able to interoperate over a communications network.

The open-system concept is not without its difficulties, however. First, multiple organizations are simultaneously defining standards for the same open-system environment. This leads to conflicts and incompatibilities. Further, although open-system computer manufacturers conform to industry standards, they also offer proprietary extensions to help differentiate their open-system computers from those of others.

The more a software development firm exploits these proprietary extensions, the more it diverges from the spirit of the open-system concept. Even with these difficulties, the open-system approach provides the most widely compatible environment today and shows great promise for the future.

In support of the open-system concept, IBM has also made a significant commitment to supporting Linux on all pSeries systems. IBM sends I/O driver software to the Open Source community, thus enabling Linux distributors to support pSeries servers, and also works closely with Linux distributors to help test pSeries servers running the Linux operating system. At the time of writing this edition, most pSeries servers are supported on SuSE Linux Enterprise 8 (SLES 8) for iSeries™ and pSeries. In Japan and some other Asia Pacific countries, Turbolino distributes its Turbolinux Linux Enterprise Server 8 (TLES 8), which supports the same pSeries servers as does SLES 8 from SuSE.

Figures 1.1a and 1.1b show the pSeries family and the RS/6000 at a glance. We'll take a look at each of these models, but here is a brief overview of IBM's naming convention on these systems. The pSeries class (e.g., p690 or p615) distinguishes the servers by performance, system capacity, and physical packaging. In general, a larger pSeries class number indicates a more powerful system, but that is not necessarily the case when packaging characteristics are considered. For instance, the pSeries 640 is a rack-optimized, NEBS (Network Equipment Building System) Level-3-compliant server for use in server farm environments, but it does not offer the performance and expansion capacity of the pSeries 630. Likewise, within a pSeries class, model numbers are used to distinguish by performance and capacity, but also by package type. To summarize, greater pSeries classes and model numbers indicate more powerful systems, except when used to distinguish characteristics such as packaging (rack vs. deskside).
