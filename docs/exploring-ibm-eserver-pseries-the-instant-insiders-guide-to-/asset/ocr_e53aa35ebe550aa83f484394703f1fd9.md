The UNIX operating system has evolved to be the basis for the open-system marketplace, in which compatibility with industry standards is the hallmark. Compatibility means that programs written for one brand of “open system” can easily be migrated to another brand. The open-system concept is good for application developers because they can easily offer their products on many different brands of computers. This is good for users because they have a multitude of software from to choose no matter what brand of open system they buy.

IBM took the UNIX operating system and incorporated many enhancements developed by other organizations (e.g., the University of California at Berkeley), added many enhancements of its and came with its version, called the Advanced Interactive Executive. This system approach was a departure from IBM’s proprietary systems such as the AS/400 or MVS operating system for IBM’s S370 and S/390 mainframes. pSeries computers running the AIX operating system are IBM’s third generation of UNIX computer platforms. (The IBM RT System was the first, and RS/6000 systems were the second generation.) Figure 3.6 shows a timeline of the evolution of AIX from its inception to the current, AIX 5L v5.2. As shown in Figure 3.6, the introduction of AIX 5L in April 2001 marked several new capabilities for IBM’s UNIX operating system. AIX 5L v1 provides a 64-bit operating kernel and device drivers. It supports up to 32-way SMP servers like the pSeries 690, logical partitioning, up to 256 GB of memory, and Linux affinity tools. The second version, AIX 5L v5.2, offers the JFS2 file system, which allows file and file-system sizes of 16 TB and supports dynamic LPAR, dynamic processor and merging Capacity Upgrade on Demand, dynamic processor sparing with CUoD, and up to 512 GB of memory.
