Exploring IBM @server pSeries

# A Glance Backward

To appreciate the pSeries family and the AIX 5L operating system fully, it is helpful to be familiar with some basic history. The AIX 5L operating system is IBM's version of the UNIX operating system, which was originally developed by AT&T Bell Labs in 1969. The original UNIX was not intended to be a commercial product but rather a tool for computer programmers at AT&T. In fact, at that time AT&T was not in the business of selling computers or operating systems. However, in 1975 AT&T began to license universities to use the UNIX operating system at no charge and soon the UNIX operating system became widely used in the academic community. Students quickly took advantage of the freedom in the academic world to make their own improvements to the UNIX operating system, often resulting in new commands that although obvious to the creator seemed cryptic to others. For example, the BFF command used to pick up electronic mail stored in the computer system was added to the UNIX operating system. Why BFF? Because the student who made the enhancement had a dog named BFF who was trained to go out and get the newspaper every morning. With this freedom to make unstructured improvements to the UNIX operating system at all helped UNIX become more powerful, it also left holes in areas such as data security and reliability.

In 1981, the University of California at Berkeley offered its version of the UNIX operating system with many enhancements. Berkeley's version of the UNIX operating system, known as Software Distribution 4 (BSD 4), became a very popular operating system in its own right—so that most of the enhancements contained in the Berkeley version were incorporated by AT&T's later versions of the UNIX operating system.

Meanwhile, IBM was busy on the “801 project,” which was started in 1975. Named after the building in which it was resident, the 801 project was an experiment to develop a minicomputer bucked the existing trend toward complex computer programming instructions. Under the leadership of IBM scientist John Cocke, the 801 approach was to simplify the range of instructions used to perform tasks and optimize the computer to execute this limited range of instructions with extreme efficiency. Born of this approach is the name “Reduced Instruction Set Computing,” or RISC.
