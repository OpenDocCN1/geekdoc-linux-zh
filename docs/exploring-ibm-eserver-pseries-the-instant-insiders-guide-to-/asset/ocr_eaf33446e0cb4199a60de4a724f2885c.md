Figure 1.34 illustrates a typical loop configuration using only two ports of the four-port SSA adapter. The loop addresses four hot-swappable disk drives (though we could have many), and its ends are attached to the single adapter. Now Let's try some hypothetical situations to test SSA's reliability. Say we lose cable A between the adapter and disk 1; cable B can still address all four drives. Another example could be a failure of drive 2 and the severing of the cables from each side of drive 2 (note that cable will not necessarily occur in the event of disk failure by the bypass electronics). In this case, signals travel in both directions, availability of drive 1 is maintained through cable A while availability of drives 3 and 4 is maintained through cable B.

This is obviously a fairly simple example. System designs can be very complex, with multiple adapters and systems providing a system with no single point of failure and high performance as well. For those ready for change, check out Figure 1.35. This illustrates a high-availability, 4-way SSA disk configuration with two loops containing 16 disk drives. The two loops are depicted by light and dark lines. Imagine the availability and throughput that this configuration provides when all adapters are in separate Pseries servers. IBM has not built SSA a vacuum. The architecture has been approved as an industry standard by the ANSI XT310.1 committee.

Storage Architecture is an important high-performance disk technology used with Pseries servers, although its maximum bandwidth of 160 MBps per loop is now matched by Ultra3-SCSI performance.
