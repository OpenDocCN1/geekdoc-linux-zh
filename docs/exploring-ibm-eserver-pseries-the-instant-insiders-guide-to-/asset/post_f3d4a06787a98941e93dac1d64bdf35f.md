## Advanced SSA Fibre-Optic Extender (7133 Feature 8851)

This is a particularly interesting way to distribute SSA disks in a campus environment and consists of a pair of short cables with a standard SSA connector at one end and a dual-bayonet ST connector at the other. It includes the circuitry to convert the electrical SSA signals to optical signals. Two multimode optical fibers (not included) are required to replace a single copper cable.

The 8851 SSA Fibre-Optic Extender is designed for situations in which SSA disks need to be more than 25 meters, but not more than 10 km, apart. The 7133 Serial Disk System Models D40 and T40, SSA160 (160 MB instantaneous) data transfer is supported up to 10 km away, depending on the type of fiber-optic cable installed. For other SSA configurations the limitations are 2.4 km maximum distance and SSA80 (80 Mbps) data transfer rate.

## Communications Interfaces/Protocols—A Road Map

One of the easiest areas in which to get confused in today's computer environment is communications. This section will serve as a quick communications tutorial providing you with the background necessary to understand the differences among the various communications options covered in this chapter.

A communications line can be thought of as a telephone wire or wireless link between two or more computer systems across long distances, or as a cable between a computer and peripheral devices in the same room, or by using multiple types of communications lines, information and host access can be provided to users in many locations.

To be attached to a communications line, the pSeries system must first have the proper electrical interface. The term “interface” refers collectively to the connector, electrical voltage levels, connector pin functions, and so on that are provided for the attachment to a communications line. We will discuss options such as the EIA-232D, EIA-422A, MIL-STD-188-114, CCITT X.21, CCITT V.35, and Token-Ring interfaces. For our purposes, it is not necessary to understand exactly all these cryptic names mean. Simply know that different types of interfaces are necessary to support different types of communications.

In addition to the different interfaces, you must also know about communications protocols to be conversant in computer communications. Just as there are different rules of grammar and punctuation in English, French, and other languages, there are different rules for types of computer communications. In computer communications, a set of rules calls a communications protocol. The protocols of most interest for our purposes are the async, bisync, SDLC, X.25, CSMA/CD (IEEE 802.3), and Token-Ring (IEEE 802.5). Each of these protocols has the same basic goal of moving information from one place to another efficiently and reliably. Each protocol has advantages and disadvantages, and the proper choice depends on system requirements in the area of transmission speed, cost, and compatibility with the other devices in the network. At all times, however, all devices using a given communications line must be using the same protocol.

The async protocol (short for asynchronous) is a low-speed, low-cost communications method very commonly used, but now used mainly for legacy devices such as ASCII terminals and hand-held devices such as barcode scanners or personal organizers. The async protocol is a good place to start in understanding the fundamentals of communications protocols. With async, individual bytes of information are transmitted (one bit at a time) with no fixed relationship between bytes. Next is the bisync (short for Binary Synchronous Communications, or BSC). The “synchronous” means that a special character preceding the information synchronizes the receiver with the incoming information. This synchronization allows many bytes of information to be sent as a single block, in contrast to the asynchronous protocol, in which transmission occurs one at a time. The ability to send blocks of characters makes bisync more efficient than the asynchronous protocol. Bisync is an older protocol used by various kinds of equipment and different computers, including IBM's System 360 and 370 mainframes. As a result of its popularity, many of today's systems use bisync.

The Synchronous Data Link Control is basically an updated version of bisync. As with bisync, SDLC is a synchronous protocol. However, SDLC is a more flexible protocol that is part of IBM's Systems Network Architecture (SNA), which is a set of communications standards published by IBM that was used for many years as a road map to ensure compatible communications are provided for current and future computer systems and software. SNA has mostly been replaced by today's Internet Protocol (IP)-based communications.

The X.25 protocol is an industry standard used in the packet-switched networks available to the public. Whereas more traditional communications networks are based on analog (or voice-type) communications signals, packet-switched networks use digital (or computer-like) communications signals. Because computer information is naturally in digital form, packet-switched networks are better to carry information and can move the information more quickly. The X.25 protocol used on packet-switched networks splits the information (using the SDLC protocols as a packaging format, for example) into small groups of digital data called packets. Each packet is sent through the most economical and available route to its destination in the network. Because each packet is routed in the most efficient way, overall information flow is improved over conventional techniques. X.25 is fully supported in IBM's Systems Network Architecture (SNA) and Internet Protocol.

The Carrier Sense Access/Collision Detection protocol is a protocol used on some Ethernet LANs to move information around the network in an orderly fashion. Its name is a mouthful, but really its is a simple protocol. In fact, we follow this protocol in our everyday telephone conversations. There, too, only one person can speak at a time. One party waits for the other to finish before beginning to speak. The phone line thus carries only one party's voice at a time, so the message is clear. This protocol handles the times when two nodes start simultaneously the same way two people do when both begin talking at the same time during a phone call. Both stop talking and begin again a few seconds later, hoping that time one begins sooner than the other. Similarly, with CSMA/CD protocol, if two (or more) nodes begin transmitting at the same time, the messages "collide" on the network. If such a collision occurs, all nodes stop transmitting and begin again after a pause of random length. All Ethernet LANs today are connected through a network of hubs and switches, rather than the CSMA/CD protocol is still used to negotiate among nodes connected to a hub. But at the next level in the LAN hierarchy, the Ethernet switch handles the contention among all nodes without using the CSMA/CD protocol.
