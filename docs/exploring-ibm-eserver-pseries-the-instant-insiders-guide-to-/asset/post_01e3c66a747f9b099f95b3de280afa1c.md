There are two halves to the cost-justification analysis: costs and benefits. The price paid to the computer vendor is easily identified early in the project. What many people feel to consider are the other costs of owning a computer system. The costs of operating the computer installation should also be considered. This is called "total cost of ownership." Some costs that should be considered beyond the price tag are discussed next.

**Hardware and Software Costs.** This should be an obvious point, but one must include the costs for the pSeries system plus other hardware peripherals (e.g., external storage systems, printers, PCs, etc.) and all the costs of the software required for both the pSeries system and any PCs to be purchased.

**Hardware Maintenance.** This is usually a monthly or annual fee for hardware. There are various alternatives, but the basic deal is that if the computer breaks down, the service company will come out and fix repairs at no charge. If the system is not on a maintenance contract, then service providers will charge an hourly fee and parts costs when the system breaks down. Because these parts/labor billing approach can be extremely expensive, most businesses choose to put their system on a maintenance contract.

**Software Maintenance.** In many cases, the software supplier will have to pay an annual or monthly fee to get software fixes and updates for the programs purchased from that company.

**Technical Support.** Some companies charge fees for technical support, whereas others include some level of support in the price of their products. This support is a way to get answers to questions and resolve any technical problems in either the hardware or software. This kind of support ranges from providing a telephone number to having assigned personnel from the computer company on premises. Sometimes this support is provided free of charge; other times it is on a fee basis.

**Facilities.** Often a company must modify its building to accommodate a computer system. These modifications might include running cable between workstations and the computer or modifying the electrical power services available. Fortunately, the cost of such building modifications is relatively low with pSeries systems as compared with larger computers, which may require water cooling and raised floors.

**Space.** Whether raised floor construction is needed or not, one must pay for the floor space required for the systems to be installed. Customers who use rack-mounted systems should consider the “footprint” of each system being compared. This simply means to evaluate the area required for the entire system (processor drawer, I/O drawers, storage subsystems, other devices). How many systems can be installed per 19-inch rack? One, or more? Does the system require more than one rack? Using this approach, one can calculate a figure of merit system density (systems per 19-inch rack footprint).

**Utilities.** In the area of facilities, an increasingly important aspect of total cost of ownership is utilities cost. Computer systems can require a lot of electricity—both to power the hardware and to run the air conditioning to compensate for the heat generated by that hardware. So, when selecting among different computer systems, compare the power (in kilowatts, or kw) for each system and the air conditioning load that amount of power consumption represents. All system components (server, storage, printers, etc.) must be considered in order to make a valid comparison.

**Education/Training.** The people who will be using the computer system may need training. The computer operators will need to understand how to manage the day-to-day operations of the computer. The users of the computer system will have to understand the application programs. It may also be necessary for a company to train its own programmers to write custom application programs. Although pSeries systems are designed for ease of use, some training will still be necessary. Many different types of training are available, as is discussed later in the chapter.

Computer-based classes allow a user to sit at a terminal and learn about various subjects are available for free IBM Education Services. Some courses are for the users; others for the systems administrator and any programmers who may use the system. In addition, pSeries systems provide access to the IBMLink network, which allows the users and the systems administrator to electronically research technical issues, place service calls, and so on. The services provided by IBMLink are available seven days a week around the clock.

**Communications-Line Costs.** If the computer system will be attached to remote workstations, to other remote computers, or to the Internet (very likely), there will be communications costs. Many different communications services are available today, and these should be considered in your justification.

On the plus side of the ledger, the computer solution is being purchased to solve identified problems or address known needs. In other words, there will be benefits to the company after the computer system is installed. (Or else will you install it?) Although it is fairly easy to identify and quantify the costs associated with a computer system, it is often difficult to do the same for the benefits. This doesn't mean that benefits are less than costs, it simply means that they require some work to uncover. Benefits are specific to a particular situation, so it would be impossible to list them all. Some common benefits associated with the application of computer systems are discussed here.

**Improved Business Cycle.** The basic cycles of most businesses and other organizations have the same components. The business buys goods or equipment, takes orders for goods or services, delivers the goods, and bills the customer. The classic application of computer systems to these areas produces improvements in the basic cycle, resulting in collecting accounts receivable more quickly or taking better advantage of payable discount terms.

**Inventory Reduction.** Many carrying costs are associated with inventory. These include warehouse space, insurance, taxes, and interest expense. The proper application of computers can reduce the level of inventory that must be kept on hand, thus reducing carrying costs.

**Improved Productivity.** Given the proper tools, anyone in any part of an organization can do his or her job more efficiently. This allows an organization to get the same amount of work done in less time or with fewer people. Excess personnel can be redirected to perform other tasks that help meet the organization' objectives. Further, as natural attrition reduces the workforce, it may be possible not to hire replacements, allowing for a reduction of the workforce over time.

**Improved Quality.** By providing more timely or better organized information to personnel, organizations can improve the quality of the services or products they provide. For example, in manufacturing environments, computers can be applied to everything from design situations to statistical quality control.

**Improved Customer Service.** By allowing an organization to respond to customer orders, questions, and special requests, computer systems can improve customer service. These improvements can include, for example, quickly responding to requests for price quotations and accurately quoting or meeting delivery dates.

**Competitive Advantage.** The items just discussed, which contribute to reducing costs, improving quality, and improving customer service, all work to improve the effectiveness of an organization. The flexibility provided by a computer system can also help the competitiveness of a business by allowing it to respond more quickly to changing demands. Change is inevitable, and it represents opportunity for the flexible and doom for the inflexible.

This list of general benefits is in no way complete. Each organization will be able to add to the list based on its current situation and objectives. Once benefits have been identified, however, the job is still not done. The benefits should be quantified in dollars and cents where possible to help focus on the areas with the largest payoff. However, quantifying benefits can be difficult and subject to judgment. Unlike the price of a computer, which can be looked up in a catalog, benefits must be calculated based on expected results. For example, if it is estimated inventory can be reduced by 10% by installing an inventory management application program, one would multiply 10% of inventory value the carrying costs to determine the annual benefit. Other areas that are more difficult to quantify include increased employee productivity, increased sales (resulting from improved customer service), or lower employee turnover (resulting from improved working conditions and pride). That these benefits (and others like them) are difficult to quantify doesn't make them any less valuable, but they are often overlooked.

## Lease or Buy?

After evaluating all the software and hardware alternatives and doing the cost/benefit analysis, a company must then decide on the acquisition method to be used. Several methods are commonly used to acquire a computer system. The most obvious alternative is to simply pay cash for the computer system, called an outright purchase. This is usually the least expensive way to acquire a computer system, but it has a direct impact on the organization's cash flow and capital position. The next acquisition alternative is to finance the purchase over a period of time, just as one would a new home.
