## Is a pSeries System the Right Choice?

When looking for ways to meet computing needs, one is faced with a seemingly endless series of questions. Before we get into the details of how to select pSeries hardware, let's pause and glance at some other hardware alternatives.

## What About Personal Computers?

We covered this topic at the introduction of this book because it seems to be the first question that everyone asks. For more information the personal computer (PC) family, read IBM eServer xSeries by Jim Hoskins and Bill Wilson (Maximum Press).

## What About iSeries Systems?

iSeries systems are IBM midrange computer system based on the OS/400® operating system, replacing the popular AS/400® computer family. iSeries computers are multiuser systems designed to meet the needs of business applications. The pSeries family, on the other hand, offers models suitable for either commercial or high-performance computing (HPC) environments, as described in Chapter 1. Because the iSeries family has no capability to be used for technical or HPC applications, the pSeries family is clearly the pick for situations where those applications prevail. In the general-purpose multiuser computer system arena, however, there is certainly room for confusion when deciding between the pSeries and the iSeries families of servers. To resolve this confusion, let's look at some of the design points of each family.

## pSeries and Your Environment 267

The iSeries systems architecture includes a built-in relational database. In other words, the iSeries hardware and operating system have been designed specifically to provide the database function commonly needed in business environments. Most other computer systems (including the pSeries) require separately purchased database systems that sit on top of the operating system to provide users with a relational database. Because the iSeries system's database is implemented in the hardware- and operating system, the performance and elegance of database implementation are improved. Further, the programmer productivity afforded by the built-in iSeries database makes it a good system for those writing and maintaining their own application programs. iSeries systems are designed around an advanced application architecture that facilitates the implementation of distributed computing environments. Other things being equal, if there is a requirement to coexist with a network of other IBM systems following this application-centric architecture or to run applications for the OS/400 operating system, then an iSeries system is probably the right choice. iSeries computers can also participate in “open” environments through their support of TCP/IP communications.

The pSeries has no built-in database, but many relational database programs are available that add this capability to the system. For those who currently use a multiuser computer system running the UNIX operating system, migrating to a pSeries system will be much more natural than migrating to an iSeries system. If there is a need to coexist with computer systems of many different brands, the open-system approach taken by the pSeries system will be a plus. By supporting many industry standards, pSeries systems and the AIX 5L operating system are designed to participate with other types of computing equipment. For more information about the iSeries family, read Exploring IBM eServer iSeries by Jim Hoskins and Roger Dimmick (Maximum Press).

## What About zSeries Systems?

Like pSeries systems, zSeries servers are multiuser computer systems that allow many users to share the system. Either zSeries or pSeries systems might provide better performance, depending on the specific applications, the number of users involved, and other variables. Because zSeries computers can also run UNIX and Linux applications on the OS/390® operating system, some of the applications that run on the pSeries are available for the zSeries, and vice versa. For these reasons, the choice between pSeries and zSeries systems is not always clear and will depend heavily on the details of a situation. However, here are some general things to consider.

The zSeries spans the computational power range that overlaps with high-end pSeries models and extends to very large mainframe computing systems. To maintain a zSeries computer, a highly trained programmer, called a systems programmer, is needed, because the operating systems used by zSeries computers are both powerful and complex. Experienced personnel are needed to install and manage these operating systems. Although zSeries computers can easily be managed by systems programming personnel at a central site, the expertise must be available at some level. pSeries systems and the AIX 5L operating system, on the other hand, require a systems operator and a programming staff only if custom application software is developed and supported.

Another way of comparing a zSeries computer with a group of pSeries systems connected via a LAN brings us to the discussion of centralized versus decentralized computing. A single system being shared by a community of users is considered a centralized computer environment where the significant processing and all auxiliary storage are resident in a single computer. One advantage of the decentralized approach is easier system management because the computer operators, systems programmers, and programming staff have complete control over the computer system. Important practices such as nightly tape backup of disk storage are enforced. The decentralized approach is represented by multiple systems located throughout a building, throughout a campus, or around the world, connected via a LAN and/or telecommunications links. Advantages of this approach are physical separation and greater independence of operations at each location while retaining the ability to share data. There are good and bad points to both the centralized and decentralized approaches. zSeries systems, being larger than most pSeries models, offer less granular choices for decentralization.

Using Cluster 1600 technologies, pSeries systems can be clustered to help achieve the best of the decentralized and centralized models. Users can have separate systems for a distributed client/server environment, while management of the cluster is centralized in a single point of control, the cluster management server.

## pSeries and Your Environment 269

Many times, the decision between pSeries and zSeries systems will be based on the current situation. For instance, if a zSeries or S/390 computer is already in place, it may be natural to expand with zSeries systems. On the other hand, if the UNIX operating system is being used today, it probably makes sense to migrate those applications to a pSeries system, which can easily interface with zSeries servers as required. The decision may become a matter of philosophy. For more information about the zSeries family, read Exploring IBM eServer zSeries and S/390 Servers by Jim Hoskins and Bob Frank (Maximum Press).

## When Should a pSeries System Be Considered?

As is evident from the foregoing discussions, there is no magic formula for selecting a particular type of computer. There are complex and interrelated factors to be considered—application software, operating system, and IT skills, existing equipment, workload, number of users, and so. If the appropriate application software is available for the UNIX environment, then there is probably a pSeries model that will handle the workload and number of users. In addition, the scalability of processing and storage capacity of individual pSeries models (and the software compatibility across the pSeries family) are designed to accommodate increased workloads as business grows. Finally, the support of pSeries and AIX 5L for open-systems standards and a broad range of communications options can help users incorporate pSeries systems into environments with other types of computers.
