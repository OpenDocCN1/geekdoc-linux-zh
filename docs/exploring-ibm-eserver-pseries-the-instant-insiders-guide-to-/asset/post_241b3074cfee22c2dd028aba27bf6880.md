
## # pSeries and Your Environment

An important first step in bringing in a pSeries system or any computer into a business environment is planning. Largely depending on how well planning is done, introducing new computer resource(s) can be like pouring water or gasoline on a fire. Many readers will already have computer systems installed in their businesses and are considering the addition of a pSeries system to their computer arsenal. Others are considering a pSeries system as their first computer. In either case, the information in this chapter should help in understanding how to introduce pSeries systems into a particular environment.

The chapter starts by discussing the evaluation process critical to any automation project and then covers software selection, followed by a brief general discussion of differences among PCs, iSeries, and zSeries systems. Finally, some specific Series hardware configurations appropriate for example environments are offered.

The latter part of the chapter includes discussions on financial decisions, physical planning, education, technical support, ergonomics, security, and service. This chapter is by no means a complete guide to introducing pSeries systems into a business environment, but it will give you a starting point for developing your plan and will discuss some important issues. Perhaps you will find in this chapter some new considerations that are applicable to your situation.

## What Are My Computing Needs?

Many people today use some type of computer system(s) to do their job or run their business. These users constantly ask themselves if their current system is good enough. Those organizations that are still operated using manual methods must ask themselves if automating their organization might help. This book can't answer the question for every customer, but it does give some of the relevant considerations. Whether an organization has millions of dollars' worth of computer systems or none at all, the place to begin answering the question of automation (or additional automation) can help a company to look very closely at the organization and its needs. Too often, organizations buy computer systems and then look for problems to solve. A properly managed computer project should start with introspection into the collective needs of all functional areas within the organization. Independently attacking specific problems can often result in a “dead-end” computer solution that provides no coherent strategy for the future. From the very start, people from all areas of the organization should be collected into a project team. Because all areas will likely be involved, the top management of the organization must consistently demonstrate a commitment to the project. Without top management involvement, disagreements among the peer business areas are slow to be resolved and the sense of priority is diminished. Lack of consistent management commitment at either the investigation or the implementation phase of a computer project is a common cause of unsuccessful projects. The cost of a computer project's failure is much higher than the cost doing it right in the first place.

The project team should start by reviewing the overall goals of the organization (or segment of a larger organization) over the next few years. In some these goals will be well known, but in others a great deal of soul-searching will be necessary. These goals should be as specific as possible and include organizational strategies in place to achieve the goals.

After the organization's goals are clearly defined, the project team should look closely at the day-to-day operations of the organization as it is. Consider the movement of information through the entire organization. Only after the information flow is understood can the team realistically discuss what is good and what is bad about the current way of doing things. Work to identify the sources of problems rather than focusing on symptoms. Understand the interaction among the various areas of the organization. Consider the flow of information from one group to another as you trace the activities. Chances are, not every problem you will uncover with a computer solution. For example, an inventory management application program will not solve inventory problems in a business if unauthorized people can walk into the storeroom and get what they need. Computers and application programs are simple tools that can be used to effectively manage an organization. People, not computer systems, manage organizations.

After the current operation has been examined with a critical eye, it will become whether a computer solution makes sense for an organization. In a small organization, this type of analysis can be done in a matter of weeks or even days. In larger organizations, it can span months or years and typically is done on ongoing basis. Some organizations choose to do the analysis on their own. For assistance in solving problems with computers, there are plenty of places to turn to, such as consultants, software companies, and computer manufacturers.

## Choosing the Software

Computer systems become a useful tool only when they are executing the appropriate application programs. There are many ways of generating a strategy for introducing computers, but considering software needs before selecting detailed hardware configurations usually makes sense. The hardware requirements, such as processor speed, memory size, disk space, and so on, will be based in part on the needs of the application program(s) selected.

The application programs selected must perform the tasks needed by the end users in a company—both today and in the foreseeable future. Chapter 3 has already discussed some basic types of application programs commonly used today. Selecting the best type of application program usually is fairly simple. For example, an accounting department needs an accounting application program, a secretary needs a wordprocessing application program, and an engineer needs a computer-aided design application program. What is more difficult the specific application program that fits a company’s needs. Is a custom application program preferred, or will a prewritten application program be acceptable? If a prewritten application program is desired, exactly which one is best? If a custom application program is desired, who should write it and what should it include?

The answers to these questions depend largely on the specifics of a given environment and are beyond the scope of this book. However, a few basics remain the same whether a program is being selected for a multinational corporation or for a corner fish market. First of all, the task to be done must be precisely understood before pursuing any application program alternatives. A thorough knowledge of the task helps to identify specific requirements an application program must meet. After a detailed understanding of the task is attained, a search can begin through the sea of prewritten, or "off-the-shelf," application programs. If an appropriate prewritten application program is found that fits a company’s needs, then the expense, delay, and effort associated with custom application program development can be avoided. Good prewritten application programs can be quite flexible. However, because everyone has slightly different needs and methods, within a given business function, it is likely that any prewritten application will have features not needed in a specific company and won't have some features that would be desirable.

There are many sources of information about the prewritten application programs. Software companies and consultants can recommend application programs to fit the needs of a business. Many popular computer magazines consistently extend reviews of prewritten application programs. These can be excellent and timely sources of information.

For specific or highly specialized needs, prewritten application programs may not be adequate. In this case, custom-developed software may be desirable. The development and maintenance of custom software involves a long-term commitment that typically is expensive, but it may be costly in the long run to pay for the development of custom software than to settle for a prewritten application program that does not do the job. If the custom software is chosen, an important step is to select the proper developer. Organizations that have their own programming staff can do their own custom application program development. Companies that do not have their own programmers (probably small and medium-sized businesses) will find it necessary to seek help (i.e., an outside software developer). In either case, it is the developer who will have the largest effect on the ultimate success or failure of the custom development activity. The developer’s job is not an easy one. Besides programming expertise, developers must become experts in all aspects of the organization's activities right down to every step performed.
