RAID 5 will obviously cost more because of the extra disk space, but it provides a better balance between performance and data protection/availability. RAID 1, often called mirroring environments. Two or three separate copies of all data are kept on separate disk disks. In the event of a disk failure, AIX uses another copy of the data from a surviving disk. Although performance is higher than RAID 5, RAID 1 also requires the use of more disks. Options also exist to combine multiple RAID functions such as striping with mirroring (RAID 0+1) using hardware and software. RAID 10, which combines striping (RAID 0) and mirroring (RAID 1), offers some performance advantages over mirroring by itself, but not all controllers provides RAID 10 mode of operation. AIX provides software implementations of striping or mirroring using host CPU processing. Hardware RAID adapters and external controller-based systems off-load the CPU processing and implement the RAID function in their microprocessors.

There are two ways to provide external storage with RAID capacity in pSeries systems. One method is to attach the 2104 Expandable Storage Plus and the 7133 Serial Disk System described in the previous section using a RAID controller, as the PCI Ultra2-SCSI Ultra3-SCSI RAID adapters or the SSA multi-initiator/RAID adapter. The electronics to control the RAID functions are on the adapter, not in the external disk system. The other alternative is to attach an external system that incorporates RAID technology within the unit itself and is attached to the pSeries system by a non-RAID adapter. Several disk systems with integrated RAID capability are available from IBM.

# Storage Area Networks

Networks of host servers and high-capacity, high-performance storage systems are known as storage area networks, or SANs. SANs provide users of large systems with the capacity of sharing, managing, and protecting vital business information in widely dispersed locations. The design of a SAN system is complex and requires careful evaluation of many factors, including such things as the applications and amount of data traffic to the storage server, the type and location of host systems to be attached, data integrity and system availability requirements, and communications costs. A wide variety of IBM storage servers designed for use in SANs, coupled with other building blocks such as Tivoli Storage Manager, Fibre Channel switches, and storage gateway products, allow be used in high-performance, scalable SAN environments.

A key technology for SAN connectivity is a serial communication protocol Fibre Channel Standard (FCS) and a lower cost implementation called Fibre Channel Arbitrated Loop (FC-AL). Fibre Channel-based products were first offered for IBM UNIX servers in 1999, and the breadth of offerings has grown rapidly over the past four years. In addition to the storings themselves, other SAN building blocks include Fibre Channel adapters, wiring hubs, switches, and network management software.

SANs are a complex and evolving technology, and a wide variety of SAN products are available from various suppliers. The key is to have “open” system solution, meaning that heterogeneous systems and operating systems can operate together and share—if not a particular file—at least the storage subsystems and connecting network. IBM’s SAN architecture integrates many technologies—including operating systems, Fibre Channel adapters, hubs and switches, storage subsystems with RAID controllers, tape, optical, and SAN management software—to provide reliable, scalable, and versatile storage solutions. SANs can vary in scope and therefore, in the definition of openness. SANs may involve multiple servers using the same operating system and located in a single building or campus. Or, they could include servers over extended distances and requiring interoperability with servers from multiple vendors. A discussion of this topic is beyond the scope of this book, but the reader will find a link to the IBM Web site on SAN storage on the “Members Only” Web site at www.maxxpress.com.

There are many storage subsystem and SAN products available from IBM. We will describe a few of the key storage servers designed for either the SAN environment or for attachment to a single pSeries server.

# IBM 2105 TotalStorage Enterprise Storage Server

The Enterprise Storage Server (ESS), shown in Figure 2.7, is the flagship of the IBM SAN storage family. With capacity up to 384 disks, it provides much as 55.9 TB of high-performance, RAID 5 and RAID 10 protected shared storage and is an ideal consolidation solution for multiple concurrently attached servers, such as pSeries servers and SP nodes. In addition, mainframes, non-IBM UNIX servers, and Intel-based servers can be used with an ESS server. The ESS system provides exceptional levels of performance and reliability.

The ESS can be partitioned among simultaneously attached AIX, iSeries, mainframe, and other UNIX servers, enabling users to easily manage and share storage resources.
