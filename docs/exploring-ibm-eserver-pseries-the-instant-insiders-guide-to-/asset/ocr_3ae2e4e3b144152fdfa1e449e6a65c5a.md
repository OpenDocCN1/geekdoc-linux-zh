Disk subsystem performance is important to the overall performance of a computer in most applications. This is especially true in virtual storage or LAN server environments in which there is heavy transfer of information between the disk storage and main memory. Performance of a disk seek to the rate at which information can be located and transferred between the disk drive and the disk controller circuitry. As traditional record albums, information on disk is stored in concentric rings on the disk platter surface. Each ring is called a track. To read information from a track, the actuator must first move the read/write head to the proper track. The time it takes (on the average) for the actuator to move the read/write head over the proper track (or “seek the track”) is called the average seek time—usually expressed in milliseconds (1/1000 sec).

Once the read/write head is located over the right track, it must wait until the disk rotation brings the right part of the track under the head. The time it takes for this to happen (on the average) is called the average latency of the drive, also expressed in milliseconds. Finally, after the proper track and proper part of the track are positioned under the read/write head, the information is transferred between the disk controller circuitry and the disk bit at a time in a continuous stream as the disk surface passes underneath the read/write head. The speed at which this is done is called the data transfer rate and is expressed in millions of bytes per second (Mbps). The shorter the access time and latency, the better the data transfer rate, the better. All of factors determine how the disk subsystem will contribute or hinder overall system performance.
