The X.25 protocol is an industry standard used in the packet-switched networks available to the public. Whereas more traditional communications networks are based on analog (or voice-type) communications signals, packet-switched networks use digital (or computer-like) communications signals. Because computer information is naturally in digital form, packet-switched networks are better to carry information and can move the information more quickly. The X.25 protocol used on packet-switched networks splits the information (using the SDLC protocols as a packaging format, for example) into small groups of digital data called packets. Each packet is sent through the most economical and available route to its destination where in the network. Because each packet is routed in the most efficient way, overall information flow is improved over conventional techniques. X.25 is fully supported in IBM's Systems Network Architecture (SNA) and Internet Protocol.

The Carrier Sense Access/Collision Detection protocol is a protocol used on some Ethernet LANs to move information around the network in an orderly fashion. Its name is a mouthful, but really it's a simple protocol. In fact, we follow this protocol in our everyday telephone conversations. There, too, only one person can speak at a time. One party waits for the other to finish before beginning to speak. The phone line thus carries only one party's voice at a time, so the message is clear. This protocol handles the times when two nodes start simultaneously the same way two people do when both begin talking at the same time during a phone call. Both stop talking and begin again a few seconds later, hoping that time one begins sooner than the other. Similarly, with CSMA/CD protocol, if two (or more) nodes begin transmitting at the same time, the messages "collide" on the network. If such a collision occurs, all nodes stop transmitting and begin again after a pause of random length. All Ethernet LANs today are connected through a network of hubs and switches, rather than the CSMA/CD protocol is still used to negotiate among nodes connected to a hub. But at the next level in the LAN hierarchy, the Ethernet switch handles the contention among all nodes without using the CSMA/CD protocol.
