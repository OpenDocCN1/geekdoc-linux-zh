The purchaser of a computer also has the ability to sell the computer when it is replaced or no longer required, thus recovering the residual value of the computer system.

Another acquisition alternative is the term lease. In this alternative, the lessor (computer owner) grants use of a computer system to the lessee (using the company), which in turn agrees to make payments for some specified period of time (or term). Terms can be any length, but they typically run for two to five years. If the lessee decides to terminate the lease before the end of the term, there is usually a termination fee. Some of the advantages offered by the term lease alternative include the conservation of capital and lines of credit. Common types of leases are “rent-to-own.” At the end of the term, the computer system can be purchased for a relatively small fee (e.g., 5% or 10% of the original cost), and the lessee gets the benefits of ownership. Operating leases are “real” leases, in which there is no discount purchase option at the end of the lease term. The lessor is the owner and retains the benefits of ownership, and capital lease payments are usually lower than with operating leases.

One final alternative is to rent the computer system month by month. This provides the most flexibility because this kind of arrangement typically requires only one month’s notice to discontinue of, with higher rental payments are usually required to get this flexibility and, because the payment is usually not fixed, a company is subject to rental price increases.

The acquisition of computer software is a different story. Typically, one does not have the option to “buy” software; most companies license the right to use the software under the conditions specified by the software company, calling the licensing agreement. These licenses usually allow the licensee to use the software on a specified computer system for a specified fee. Three common ways to pay for this are one-time charge, lease, and monthly charge. With the one-time charge, the licensee pays a set price and retains the right to use the software indefinitely. This charge can be paid in cash or financed. The leasing alternative is really another way to finance the one-time charge with no down payment. Finally, the monthly charge is like renting the software month to month indefinitely.

# User Education

The discussions in the chapter so far should assist you in selecting the appropriate software and hardware to fit your needs. However, no matter what computer hardware and software you select, you will need people to operate them. To maximize efficiency and morale, these people (or users) must be provided with education in the use of the computer system itself as well as its software.

Proper education is critical to the success of any automation project. Inadequate education will prevent the productivity benefits afforded by moving a task to the computer. The goal of education is to make the users proficient at using the computer hardware and software and to make their interaction with the computer enjoyable. If using the computer is enjoyable, users will be more motivated. If the computer represents a frustrating struggle because of inadequate education, users will be less productive or perhaps will the computer altogether.

The education should cover the pSeries hardware, the AIX 5L operating system, and the application software that will be used. Both pSeries and AIX 5L have been designed with close attention to making them as easy to use as possible. The documentation and on-line education courses provide a self-guided tour through pSeries and AIX 5L basics. These offerings should provide adequate education for most users.

For programmers and IT personnel who will be responsible for installing and managing a pSeries system, more detailed on-line courses and many classroom training offerings are available from IBM and others. The amount of education needed will depend on the tasks performed the experience of the student with AIX 5L or another UNIX operating system. Again, the on-line education courses offered by IBM are a good starting point for learning the AIX 5L operating system. Although the systems administrator will probably need such formal AIX 5L operating system education, often the operating system can be all hidden from the users, minimizing their needs in this area.

Education in the use of the application program is usually the most important thing for the users. The content of this education depends on the particular application program selected. Typically, manuals provided with the application program will contain step-by-step tutorials designed to train the user. Depending on the complexity of the application program, the experience of the user, and the quality of the tutorial, this education method achieves varying levels of success. There are other ways of getting application program education. Software companies offer classes tailored to teaching how to use their application programs. Businesses sometimes send one person to a class of this type and then have the person teach other users upon returning. IBM, other software vendors, and consultants can provide information about various classes, books, and on-line courses.

Access to IBM education offerings for pSeries and AIX 5L products is available on the companion Web site (www.maxpress.com) for this book. Once you have logged onto the “Exploring pSeries” site, look under “Access Other IBM eServer pSeries Resources.” “AIX and pSeries Curriculum” will be one of the selections in that list.

# IBMLink Support

No matter what computer system a business chooses, the system and its users will require ongoing support. The users will have questions that need answering, problems that must be addressed, software updates that must be installed, and so forth. This support can be delivered in many different ways. Personnel from the computer manufacturer, the software company, the business’s own staff, and so on are generally involved in supporting the computer system and its users.

In an effort to make this support more effective and convenient, IBM offers IBMLink. Just as electronic mail and on-line business information streamline a business’s operation, IBMLink streamlines the support to the business. # Ergonomics

No plan would be complete without addressing the human needs that directly affect the day-to-day productivity of computer users—ergonomics. pSeries systems have been designed with careful attention to ergonomics. Everything from the power switch to the length of the keyboard cable is scrutinized. Much effort is expended fine-tuning the ergonomics of most application programs. To get the most out of your pSeries system, such as the American Optometric Association (AOA) and the New York Committee for Occupational Safety and Health (NYCOSH), as well as IBM, have probed deeply into the relationship between the computer and the user. Items such as desks, chairs, lighting, noise, and the like are important to productivity.

# Comfort for the Eyes

With the increase in computer use over recent years, we have seen an increase in eyestrain.
