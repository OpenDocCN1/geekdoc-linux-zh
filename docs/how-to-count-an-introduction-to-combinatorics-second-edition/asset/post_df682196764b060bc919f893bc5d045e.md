## DEFINITION 11.3 Isomorphism of Groups

Let $(G, \bullet)$ and $(H, *)$ be groups. We say that these groups are $\textit{isomorphic}$ if there is a bijection $\Theta : G \rightarrow H$ such that for all $\mathbf{g}_1, \mathbf{g}_2 \in G$, we have

$$\Theta(\mathbf{g}_1 \bullet \mathbf{g}_2) = \Theta(\mathbf{g}_1) * \Theta(\mathbf{g}_2). \qquad (11.1)$$

Such a mapping $\Theta$ is called an $\textit{isomorphism}$ between the two groups.

For example, the mapping $\Theta$ from the permutations corresponding to the rotations of a square and the numbers modulo 4 (given by iii and ii in Figure 11.4), namely,

$$\Theta(e) = 0, \Theta((1\ 2\ 3\ 4)) = 1, \Theta((1\ 3)(2\ 4)) = 2, \text{ and } \Theta((1\ 4\ 3\ 2)) = 3,$$

is an isomorphism between the two groups. We check just one case of the Equation 11.1. We have

$$\Theta((1\ 3)(2\ 4) \circ (1\ 4\ 3\ 2)) = \Theta((1\ 3)(2\ 4) +_4 (1\ 4\ 3\ 2))$$

since the left-hand side is $\Theta((1\ 2\ 3\ 4)) = 1$ and the right-hand side is $2 +_4 3 = 1$. That then, [By] Definition 11.3, the equation $\Theta(\mathbf{g}_1 \bullet \mathbf{g}_2) = \Theta(\mathbf{g}_1) * \Theta(\mathbf{g}_2)$ expresses the fact that combining elements in the group $(G, \bullet)$ then mapping to $(H, *)$ produces the same result as first mapping to $(H, *)$ and then combining in $(H, *)$. This condition implies

that the Cayley tables of the two groups have the same pattern. As with isomorphisms between two graphs, an isomorphism between two groups is a *structure-preserving correspondence* of their elements. We regard two isomorphic groups as being essentially the same. So, for example, if we are asked, "How many different groups with four elements are there?" we are being asked to find the largest set, say X, of groups with four elements such that no two of the groups in X are isomorphic, but each group with 4 elements is isomorphic to one of the groups in X. Since the groups whose Cayley tables are shown in Figure 11.4 are isomorphic, any such set X can include at most one of them.

We have seen that each row of a Cayley table corresponds to a permutation of the group elements. For example, if we look at table (i) in Figure 11.4 we see from the a-row that a corresponds to the permutation that in bracket notation we can write as

$$\begin{pmatrix} e & a & b & c \\ a & b & c & e \end{pmatrix}$$

and in cycle notation as $(e \ a \ b \ c)$. A theorem due to Cayley says that this correspondence is always an isomorphism between a group $(G, \bullet)$ and the associated group of permutations of its elements. This is not difficult to prove once we notice that the permutation of G that corresponds to the element $g \in G$ is the permutation, $\Theta_g$ of G defined by $\Theta_g(x) = g \bullet x$. Here is the proof.

## THEOREM 11.3
### Cayley’s Theorem for Groups

Each group $(G, \bullet)$ is isomorphic to a group of permutations of its elements, with the operation of composition.

**Proof**

For each $g \in G$, we let $\Theta_g: G \to G$ be the mapping defined by:

$$\text{for each } x \in G, \; \Theta_g(x) = g \bullet x .$$

By Exercise 11.1.2A, each mapping $\Theta_g$ is a permutation of the set G. We let G* be the set of all these permutations, and we let $\Theta: G \to G*$ be the mapping defined by $\Theta(g) = \Theta_g$. Since $\Theta(g)(e) = \Theta_g(e) = g \bullet e = e$ we see if $g \neq g'$, then $\Theta_g(e) \neq \Theta_{g'}(e)$ and hence that $\Theta(g) \neq \Theta(g')$. Consequently, $\Theta$ is injective. By the definition of G*, $\Theta$ is surjective, and hence $\Theta$ is a bijection.

We also have that, for all $g_1, g_2 \in G$, and all $x \in G$,

$$\Theta(g_1 \bullet g_2)(x) = \Theta_{g_1 \bullet g_2}(x) = (g_1 \bullet g_2) \bullet x = g_1 \bullet (g_2 \bullet x) = \Theta_{g_1}(\Theta_{g_2}(x)) = \Theta_{g_1} \circ \Theta_{g_2}(x),$$

and therefore

$$\Theta(g_1 \bullet g_2) = \Theta_{g_1} \circ \Theta_{g_2} = \Theta(g_1) \circ \Theta(g_2).$$

Hence $\Theta$ is an isomorphism between the group $(G, \bullet)$ and the group $(G^*, \circ)$. This completes the proof.
