We obtain a new simple graph, $G'$, by adding a vertex, $v_{n}$, and joining it by an edge to the vertices $v_{n-k}, \dots, v_{n-1}$. The degrees of the vertices $v_{1}, \dots, v_{n}$ in $G'$ are then $d_{1}, \dots, d_{n-k-1}, d_{n-k}, \dots, d_{n-1}, d_{n}$ respectively, and therefore this latter sequence is a simple graph degree sequence.

For the “only if” converse we suppose that $d_{1}, \dots, d_{n}$ is a simple graph degree sequence, with $d_{1} \leq d_{2} \leq \dots \leq d_{n} = k$. It follows that there is at least one simple graph whose $n$ vertices, $v_{1}, v_{2}, \dots, v_{n}$ have these degrees. Among all such graphs let $G$ be one in which the sum of the degrees of the $k$ vertices adjacent to $v_{n}$ is as large as possible. We show that, in this case, the degrees of the vertices adjacent to $v_{n}$ are $d_{n-k}, \dots, d_{n-1}$.

Suppose not. That is, suppose that $v_{n}$ is *not* adjacent to $k$ vertices whose degrees are $d_{n-k}, \dots, d_{n-1}$. Then, since $v_{n}$ is adjacent to $k$ vertices, there must be vertices $v_{i}, v_{j}$ such that

+   i. The vertex $v_{i}$ is adjacent to $v_{n}$ and $\delta(v_{i}) < d_{n-k}$.
+   
+   ii. The vertex $v_{j}$ is *not* adjacent to $v_{n}$ and $\delta(v_{j}) \geq d_{n-k}$.

Now, since $\delta(v_{j}) < \delta(v_{i})$, there must be a vertex, say $v_{l}$, that is adjacent to $v_{i}$ but that is not adjacent to $v_{n}$. We now change $G$ to a new graph $G'$ by replacing the edges $\{v_{i}, v_{n}\}$ and $\{v_{i}, v_{l}\}$ by edges $\{v_{j}, v_{n}\}$ and $\{v_{i}, v_{l}\}$ (see Figure 9.10).

The degrees of the vertices $v_{i}, v_{n}, v_{l}, v_{j}$ are unchanged, and so $G'$ has the same degree sequence as $G$. However, as in $G'$ the vertex $v_{n}$ is adjacent to $v_{j}$ rather than $v_{i}$, and $\delta(v_{j}) > \delta(v_{i})$, we see that the sum of the degrees of the vertices of $G'$ to which $v_{n}$ is adjacent is greater than the sum of the degrees of the vertices of $G$ to which $v_{n}$ is adjacent. This contradicts our choice of $G$.

We can therefore deduce that the $k$ vertices to which $v_{n}$ is adjacent in $G$ have degrees $d_{n-k}, \dots, d_{n-1}$. Thus if we delete the vertex $v_{n}$ and the edges joining it to these $k$ vertices, the result will be a graph with $n-1$ vertices with degrees $d_{1}, \dots, d_{n-k-1}, d_{n-k-1}, \dots, d_{n-k-1}$, and so this sequence of degrees, reordered if necessary, is also a degree sequence. This completes the proof.

We can illustrate this by the examples we have previously mentioned. We use $S \sim S'$ to indicate the transition from the sequence $d_{1}, \dots, d_{n}$ to the sequence $d_{1}, \dots, d_{n-k-1}, d_{n-k-1} - 1, \dots, d_{n-1} - 1$ and $S' \sim S''$ to indicate that $S''$ is obtained from the sequence $S'$ by reordering where necessary.
