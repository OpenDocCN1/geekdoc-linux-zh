For Intel's desire to break into the maker market dominated by ARM and microcontrollers, is the third time the charm?

Intel has been trying to make headway for a few years now, having seen the success of the Arduino project and the Raspberry Pi. The Intel Genuino, thanks to poor IO performance from the Quark processor, its successor, the Edison (reviewed in Li&S Issue 138), has unpopular processor issues but its old form and high-density connectors are off-putting,

which, at a casual glance, looks just like the classic microcontroller-based Arduino Uno. Yet, while it shares the Uno's layout, the Genuino 101 is a different beast.

It seems to be the crack of the whip. Abandoning its previous approach of producing an Arduino-compatible microcontroller, the company has partnered with the Arduino project to create a fully official device; however, the Arduino 101 in the US and Genuino 101 elsewhere avoid trademark issues. The result is a device—an ultra-compact, low-power system-on-chip (SoC) designed primarily for wearable projects, this chip is part of the Intel Quark-RISC processor, with a 32MHz Argon Core (ARC) - a tacit admission that Quark's ability to directly drive GPIO isn't quite where it should be.

The presence of two processors splits the Genuino in an interesting manner. The Quark, which is fully compatible with an architecture based on the company's old Pentium, runs a real-time operating system (RTOS); the Curie module contains two cores: an x86 (Quark) and a 32-bit ARC core.

Fast performance, onboard accelerometer and Bluetooth, x86 core may allow for future exploration. Intel has pledged to open-source the RTOS in March 2016, at which point the true potential of Genuino 101 should become apparent.

## Pros

- Fast performance, onboard accelerometer and Bluetooth, x86 core may allow for future exploration.
- The x86 core directly is to execute whatever Arduino program you care to load, in a manner which is theoretically indistinguishable from any other Arduino.
- This architecture offers the best of both worlds: the flexibility and power of a microcontroller with the low power of Arduino.

## Cons

- Poor floating point performance, less-than-3.3V logic; no way to access the x86 core directly.
- While locked and closed-source, while the 8KB of SRAM is impressive compared to 2KB of Arduino Uno, the memory is shared between the two processors: the ARC has access only 4KB, with the remaining memory locked up by the Quark.
- By microcontroller standards, performance of Genuino 101 is good: the board can toggle Intel ARC-performance from 16MHz to Arduino Nano's 94MHz, while de-performance evolves from 27 MIPS to the Nano's 6 MIPS. Floating-point performance is poor; running on the ARC MIPS — a possible sign of optimization in the compiler. While, the ARC does at least offer the option of using double-precision mode — something the Atmega328 on board can't handle.

## Summary

The Genuino 101 is a clever device, and an interest on improving's potential. Its true potential is locked behind a closed-source design. If Intel fulfills its pledge to release the source code, the venture into the market is, however, a sold of fencing.
