# Mad, bad & dangerous

Companies may enjoy the benefits of free software but, them enforce free software licensing policies

> The GPL has never been the flavour of the month in the corporate world, where copyrights and patents are seen as both revenue streams and instruments to inhibit competition, and the GPL is often dismissed as bad and dangerous to those who take advantage of its freedom can make an active contribution to the enforcement of free software licenses. GPL enforcement is community-funded copyleft which depends on volunteering.

Funding of GPL controversy has emerged as an issue thanks to the suggestion by Karen, and Shannon's executive director, that 'over the past, and in particular since we launched the VMware suite, some of our copyleft funding has been pulled,' and the community moved to vote members of the Linux Foundation to stand for it.

Matthew Garrett, a senior kernel developer, asserted that 'the Foundation has become less enthusiastic about GPL enforcement, and the funding is de-prioritised out any amount of community representation to ensure that there was no risk of failure in favour of corporate enforcement efforts.'

companies poured vast resources into developing proprietary versions of the same operating system at the expense of the hardware, and services unless supported offered by them. Linux, which was developed by Sun Micros, wasn't quite like Irix (SGI), which wasn't quite like HPUX (HP), which wasn't quite like AIX (IBM), which wasn't quite like Truis.

None of them played well, which complicated the market for ISVs and inhibited the development of open source. Each implementation of Linux was unique and proprietary. Proprietary systems cost a lot of money, and maintaining a multiplicity of interfaces also created hurdles for other parts of the business. Software that ran on one version of Linux wouldn't run on.

The change sparked by Garrett encouraged the idea that there was a mutual advantage for companies to contribute back to a single base, and share standards with everyone. This multiplicity of interests also encouraged the Linux, which was also benefited by its portability across a variety of hardware, Unix-like fragmentation of the code.

To develop a proprietary operating system from scratch like Linux would cost billions of dollars. If the code is shared then the costs and the technical challenges disappear. Copyleft-driven driver development by the community of the code, and standards, which also made it easier to distribute benefit for hardware and architectures. Companies contributed to the kernel, and have benefited from the contributions of others. They do not contribute out of generosity, but because this approach made it possible for them to get anything at a reduced cost.

This makes it all the more surprising that the companies who 'own' the Linux code, courtesy have made it a part of their business to help SPC defend and protect the licence that made it possible.

In the 80s and 90s, Unix was used as the standard and had a great history in computing, yet after eight or ten years of development, GNU/Linux, which still had many technical shortcomings, was adopted by the administration as a replacement for Unix.
