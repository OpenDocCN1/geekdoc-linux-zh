In 1983, Stallman, then an employee of the MIT Artificial Intelligence Lab, announced
a couple of newsgroups that he was going to "write a UNIX-compatible software system
codel GNU (for Gnu's Not UNIX) and give it away to everyone who can use it." You can
 the original message on the Free Software Foundation's Web site at www.fsf.org. He
 discouraged by the shrinking world of cooperative programming that tified his
experience in the 1970s. In those days, according to Stallman, programmers, or more
hackers, willingly shared software with each other just because it was the right thing to do. He
and other programmers had become increasingly dismayed by the trend on the part of many of
the large companies to maximize their profits by restricting the use of software,
particularly operating systems. In January 1984, Stallman left his job and writing
G software.

The GNU was, in effect, to be a system that looked like UNIX, acted like UNIX,
smelled like UNIX, but was in fact not UNIX. Stallman said in his announcement, "GNU will
able run UNIX programs, but will not be identical to UNIX." In other words, he was not going
to take AT&T's code and modify it. Instead, he and others would simply redevelop all the
features of a UNIX system: compilers, editors, sellers. So, the first pieces
of GNU software we would expect from a UNIX system:cher included GCC, a compiler, and GNU Emacs, a text editor.
