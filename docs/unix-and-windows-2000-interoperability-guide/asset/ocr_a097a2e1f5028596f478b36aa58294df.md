# Chapter 1 · What is UNIX?

UNIX underwent several editions in the early 1970s, but it remained primarily an internal operating system to BTL until 1974. In that year, Ritchie and Thompson published a paper describing UNIX in a computer science journal, and as a result, a number of academic institutions began copies of it for educational purposes. AT&T agreed to do so for a small fee, just enough to cover the cost of reproducing the tape and documentation. AT&T, operating under a consent at the time and really not in the computer operating system business, decided that releasing UNIX to universities in this manner and without support would be permissible.

So, institutions receiving a copy of UNIX in those days got not only the compiled code as well. And because AT&T provided no support for UNIX, these sites felt to fix bugs and make changes and additions on their own. These changes made their way back to BTL and became incorporated into the next release of UNIX. One significant source of change came from the University of California-Berkeley. In 1975, Ken Thompson went to Berkeley, his alma mater, as a visiting professor, bringing with him the latest version of UNIX. Two graduate assistants, Bill Joy and Chuck Haley, quickly started hacking on the operating system, and this work eventually led to a separate distribution of UNIX, called BSD (Berkeley Distribution), first appearing in 1978.

Meanwhile, work continued at AT&T, which released version 7 of UNIX in 1979. The releases of UNIX—one from AT&T and one from Berkeley—each had differences to warrant the widely growing view that they were two separate entities. In reality, contributions to UNIX came from many sources and grew tremendously in the 1980s. Sometimes, individuals passed on their contributions to BSD, and some of BSD’s were passed on to AT&T. Companies like SCO and Microsoft got into the act, releasing a version of UNIX called Xenix in 1980. Others, like Hewlett-Packard, Sun Microsystems, DEC, Apollo, and IBM began offering UNIX systems for their hardware, each containing elements of both AT&T and BSD of UNIX, as well as their enhancements.

The result is that today there are many flavors of UNIX, all of which have roots in the work that began about 30 years ago. Over time, there have been literally hundreds of individual and corporate contributors to UNIX, creating something of an operating system mélange. This diversity has resulted in robust and flexible operating systems, while has also led to a great deal of criticism for the lack of standards. The 1990s saw efforts to create a unified UNIX system, but commercial pressures have caused these efforts to fail. In 1993, AT&T sold its UNIX business to Novel, and sold it two years later, where the UNIX source code continues to reside. However, Novel passed ownership of the UNIX trademark and specification to the X/Open Company, one of the groups formed to create UNIX standards. In 1996, X/Open merged with the Open Software Foundation (OSF) to form The Open Group, where the trademark resides today.
