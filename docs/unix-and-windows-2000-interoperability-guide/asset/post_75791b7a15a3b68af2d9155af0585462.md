# What Is UNIX?

Formally, the name UNIX is a registered trademark of The Open Group, an international consortium of vendors and customers dedicated to the advancement of open systems. In everyday use, however, means any variant, derivative, or clone of the operating system first developed by AT&T's Bell Laboratories in the 1970s. These variants include the commercially available HP-UX from Hewlett-Packard, AIX UNIX from IBM, Solaris from Sun Microsystems, UnixWare from SCO, and Tru64 UNIX from Compaq. These UNIX systems run mostly on RISC-based or Intel-based processors and usually contain modifications, additions, or other "enhancements" to make each of them different from the other, sometimes significantly different. Also available, for free on the Internet, are NetBSD, FreeBSD, and Linux. FreeBSD runs exclusively on Intel processor-based computers, while the others run on different platforms. Such operating systems are developed and maintained by volunteers who believe strongly in open systems and some form of open source software.

Obviously, then, there is no such thing as THE operating system; there are many. Each has its own distinct characteristics, these operating systems have enough in common to warrant the designation of UNIX operating system. So, why is this the case? Where did UNIX come from anyway? Well—please don't groan—to answer these questions, we have to look at a little history.

# 1.1 In the Beginning

UNIX began as an in-house project within AT&T's Bell Telephone Labs (BTL) in 1969. Two BTL staff members, Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie, began writing an operating system on a DEC PDP-7, in part to run a simulation program (and game) called Space Travel. Originally, Space Travel ran on a mainframe operating system called Multics (Multiplexed Information and Computing Service), and as it is on this acronym the new operating system eventually became called UNIX.

# Chapter 1 · What is UNIX?

UNIX underwent several editions in the early 1970s, but it remained primarily an internal operating system to BTL until 1974. In that year, Ritchie and Thompson published a paper describing UNIX in a computer science journal, and as a result, a number of academic institutions began copies of it for educational purposes. AT&T agreed to do so for a small fee, just enough to cover the cost of reproducing the tape and documentation. AT&T, operating under a consent at the time and really not in the computer operating system business, decided that releasing UNIX to universities in this manner and without support would be permissible.

So, institutions receiving a copy of UNIX in those days got not only the compiled code as well. And because AT&T provided no support for UNIX, these sites felt to fix bugs and make changes and additions on their own. These changes made their way back to BTL and became incorporated into the next release of UNIX. One significant source of change came from the University of California-Berkeley. In 1975, Ken Thompson went to Berkeley, his alma mater, as a visiting professor, bringing with him the latest version of UNIX. Two graduate assistants, Bill Joy and Chuck Haley, quickly started hacking on the operating system, and this work eventually led to a separate distribution of UNIX, called BSD (Berkeley Distribution), first appearing in 1978.

Meanwhile, work continued at AT&T, which released version 7 of UNIX in 1979. The releases of UNIX—one from AT&T and one from Berkeley—each had differences to warrant the widely growing view that they were two separate entities. In reality, contributions to UNIX came from many sources and grew tremendously in the 1980s. Sometimes, individuals passed on their contributions to BSD, and some of BSD’s were passed on to AT&T. Companies like SCO and Microsoft got into the act, releasing a version of UNIX called Xenix in 1980. Others, like Hewlett-Packard, Sun Microsystems, DEC, Apollo, and IBM began offering UNIX systems for their hardware, each containing elements of both AT&T and BSD of UNIX, as well as their enhancements.

The result is that today there are many flavors of UNIX, all of which have roots in the work that began about 30 years ago. Over time, there have been literally hundreds of individual and corporate contributors to UNIX, creating something of an operating system mélange. This diversity has resulted in robust and flexible operating systems, while has also led to a great deal of criticism for the lack of standards. The 1990s saw efforts to create a unified UNIX system, but commercial pressures have caused these efforts to fail. In 1993, AT&T sold its UNIX business to Novel, and sold it two years later, where the UNIX source code continues to reside. However, Novel passed ownership of the UNIX trademark and specification to the X/Open Company, one of the groups formed to create UNIX standards. In 1996, X/Open merged with the Open Software Foundation to form The Open Group.

# 1.2 The New Face of UNIX

Technically speaking, UNIX is proprietary software. AT&T originally created it. The Open Group owns the UNIX trademark and name. Hewlett-Packard owns HP-UX, IBM owns AIX, Sun Microsystems owns Solaris, SGI owns IRIX, SCO owns UnixWare, and Compaq owns Tru64 UNIX.

# 1.3 UNIX and Open Source Software

GNU Linux systems, as well as others such as NetBSD and FreeBSD, are the result of efforts to provide free software to the public. To what extent free means depends on who does talking. In this context, however, free means offered to the public free of charge and can be modified and distributed by anyone in any manner.
