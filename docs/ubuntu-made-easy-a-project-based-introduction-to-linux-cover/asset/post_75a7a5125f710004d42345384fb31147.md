## 13B-1: Publishing Images to Online Albums and Galleries

As mentioned, Shotwell allows you to easily publish images to numerous online sites, much in the way that Windows Live Photo Gallery does. The method I show here for Facebook is essentially the same one you use for the others, and they're all pretty simple. Here's what you need to:

- 1. In Shotwell, select the photos you want to post online by holding down the CTRL key and clicking each of the photos you want to publish.
- 2. Once you've made your selection, click the Publish button at the bottom of the window. A window like that in Figure 13-5 will appear.
- 3. The default online site selected in this window is Facebook. If you would like to upload photos to Flickr or PicasWeb, select that site in the dropdown menu at the top right of the window. Once you've done that, click the Log button.
- 4. The login page for the site you've selected will then appear in the window (Figure 13-6). Fill in your login information and then click the Log In button (or equivalent for the other sites).
- 5. In the case of Facebook, you will be asked to name the machine you are logging from. Once you have done that, you will need to allow Shotwell to access your Facebook information, something you'll need to do whenever you log from a new machine.

## 13B-3: Organizing Your Photo Collections with Tags

As your photo collection grows and grows, it can get tough to sort things out and find exactly what you need. Frankly, Shotwell can make this a bit easier through the use of tags. Tags are categories that you create and then apply to whatever images you want in order to organize your collection thematically. You might, for example, create tags for each of your family members, or just create a broader tag for Family. You could create tags for cities and countries you've visited, providing categories and subcategories for your travel pics. The choices and combinations are endless, and you can decide what they all all. Once you've applied these tags to your photos, narrowing your photo searches will be much easier.

The first step is to create a few tags. This is easily done by right-clicking an image and selecting Add Tags in the pop-up menu. A small window will appear in which you can just type a single tag, or kill two birds with one stone, whatever tags you want to assign to the photo (Figure 13-9). Just be sure to separate each tag with a comma. Once you're done, click OK, after which the tags will appear both under the thumbnail and in the left pane of the window.

![](img/d35fa962e6f9664d66dc83916f426418_273_0.png)

Figure 13-9: Creating tags in Shotwell

Once you have tags in the left pane of the Shotwell window, adding tags to other photos is a cinch. Drag a thumbnail to one of the tags and release your mouse button, the image will be tagged. If you want to add a second tag, drag the thumbnail to that as well. Once you are done tagging your images, they will be far easier to sort, as you can see in Figure 13-10.

## Getting Airy with the GIMP

The Windows and Mac worlds may have Photoshop, but the Linux world has the GIMP (see Figure 13-12). Although not as fully featured as Photoshop, the GIMP is a powerful contender, might explain why it has been ported to both Mac and Windows. The GIMP allows you to create bitmap graphics and, importantly, retouch or completely doctor image files. With the GIMP you can get rid of red-eye in your digital photos, airbrush unwanted shadows (or facial blemishes), view your image as a canvas texture, change a photo into an oil painting, and even add a bell pepper here and there—with drop shadows no less.

## RawTherapy

RawTherapy is not some solution for your troubled soul but rather handy application that allows you to manipulate RAW files. RAW image files are images that have not been processed by the on-board software of your camera that converts the data it receives into a format that your computer is able to handle. While not all cameras have the option to save in RAW mode, many amateur and professional camera models do. Once you load RAW files onto your computer, the thumbnails for those files will appear as black rectangles. That's essentially blobs of raw data that your computer on its own can make heads or tails of. This is where RawTherapy comes in (see Figure 13-11). Once you open your RAW files with RawTherapy, you will be able to see what they actually look like and then go on adjust to your heart’s content, usually with much better results than you would get if working on JPEG files. Once you have made your adjustments to the RAW files, you can save them into other, more universally compatible formats, such as JPEG, while retaining your original RAW file for future use.
