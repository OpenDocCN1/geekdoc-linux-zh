## Chatting on IRC

Internet Relay Chat (IRC) is where Ubuntu users can go to talk to one another online. Chats happen in real time, and using IRC is very similar to using an instant messaging service. Chatting is a particularly good option if you want to talk to someone as soon as possible about a question without waiting for a reply on the forums.

IRC can be daunting at first, so let's walk through a typical session. First, you'll need to make sure you have the right chat software. Open the Ubuntu Software Center, search for XChat IRC (not to be mistaken for XChat-GOME), and install it. Once you've done that, limber up your finger and

1. Bring up the Dash and XChat. A Network List window like the one in Figure 21-10 will appear.
2. Make a few choices for your nickname. Your nickname is how other users will be able to identify you, so choose something (mine is philbull). You should try a few alternatives in case someone has already used your first choice—adding random numbers to the end of your first choice to secure it. Just make not to use spaces in your nickname.
3. Make sure that Ubuntu Servers is selected in the Network list and then click Connect.
4. A load of text will fill across the screen as you are connected to the IRC server. After a few seconds, you'll be switched over to the #ubuntu channel.

## Registering Your Nickname

It's a good idea to register your nickname so nobody else can steal it and so people will recognize you when you use IRC. In the left of the window, click the nickname item to switch to a welcome channel for Freenode, the IRC server used by Ubuntu (Figure 21-12). Now, type /ns register password email into the message box, replacing email with your email address and password with a good password. For example, typing /ns register nopeekingjane@example.com would do this if your details were correct. Press ENTER to finish the registration.

![](img/d35fa962e6f9664d66dc83916f426418_403.png)

Figure 21-12: registration of your IRC nickname

If everything went to plan, you'll see a message saying An email containing instructions has been sent to jane@example.com. Check your email, and there should be activation instructions to type something like /msg NickServ VERIFY nickname into the free channel you were in. Do this, and you will see a message along the lines that nickname has been verified.

If you get a message saying nickname is already registered instead of that, try a new choice, and you'll see a message that says You are now known as nickname. The next time you go on IRC, you can retrieve your registered nickname by going to the free channel, typing /nick nickname and pressing ENTER. Then type /msg NickServ identify nickname and password when you do this.

## IRC Etiquette

There are rules and social norms to follow on IRC just as anywhere else. It goes without saying you shouldn't go out of your way to offend anyone, just as in the real world. Here are a few conventions that you should also be aware of:

- **Don't shout.** Writing in ALL CAPS is the IRC equivalent of shouting all the time. This is pretty everyone's peeve, and you're bound to get censured for doing it. own- **Say things only once.** Sometimes you won't get a reply for a while, so it can be tempting to keep repeating the same message until someone acknowledges it. Repeating yourself too many times is considered annoying. Being patient is a necessary skill on IRC because people tend to dip in and out sporadically rather than watching all the time. It's okay to repeat your message if you don't get a reply after some minutes or, though.
- **Don't send loads of text.** Sending more than a few lines at once is flooding. You should avoid doing it. If you have lots of text that you want people to read, use a service like http://pastebin.com/ to hold the text and simply post a link to it on IRC.
- **Stay on topic.** There are different channels for different purposes, so you should try to stick to the topic for which a particular channel is intended. Advertising is considered to be off-topic in most channels and is particularly frowned upon.
- **Smile for the camera.** Most of the conversations in the public Ubuntu chat rooms are logged and can be viewed at http://logs.ubuntu.com/. This is useful if you forget a link that someone mentioned last week, but it can be awkward if you said something you shouldn't have. As a general rule, never post any sensitive or personal information on IRC (especially your password or credit card details), because it could come back to bite you.

## More Help with IRC

I hope this expedition into IRC hasn't put you off for life! Lots of people find it confusing at first, but it's a really great way to talk to other Ubuntu users you're comfortable with. If you feel need to hone your IRC skills, the IRC Help website at http://www.irchelp.org/ is a very useful resource. And if that still doesn't get you into the IRC vibe, you can try a more familiar web interface to the Ubuntu IRC channel, which can be found at http://webchat-reenode.net/?channels=ubuntu.

## An IRC Alternative: Mailing Lists

If the whole idea of IRC chat has you tearing your hair out, another gentler option is available in the form of the support mailing lists. All you need to do is send an email to the appropriate list and hope for a reply. Visit https://lists.ubuntu.com/ to see your options—the ubuntu-users list is probably the one you want.

## Keeping Up with the News

If you want to find out what’s going on in Ubuntu-land, there’s no shortage of news outlets. Here are just a few them (you’ll also find additional resources in Appendix D):

- **Full Circle Magazine**(http://fullcirclemagazine.org/) Full Circle is independently produced Ubuntu magazine that is free to download in PDF (you can also get it from the Ubuntu Software Center). It contains everything you might expect to find in a traditional dead-tree magazine, such as reviews, interviews, stories from readers, and how-to guides.
- **Ubuntu Weekly Newsletter**(https://wiki.ubuntu.com/UbuntuWeeklyNewsletter/) This newsletter aims to provide an overview of the goes-on in the community each week and typically includes Ubuntu news stories, interviews with developers, and updates on recent changes to software. It’s pitched to the geekier end of the spectrum.
- **The Fridge**(http://fridge.ubuntu.com/) The Fridge is a place where community can stick notes and updates on upcoming events. Interviews are sometimes posted here.
- **Planet Ubuntu**(http://planet.ubuntu.com/) This is where you can read the personal blogs of Ubuntu developers. Some of the posts are technical, but if you’re interested in cutting-edge developments, this is the place to go.

News and opinion get passed around microblogging services too. Subscribe to the #ubuntu hashtag on Twitter or join the Ubuntu group on Identity.ca (http://identi.ca/group/ubuntu/) to join in the tweeting, denting, or whatever you want to call it.
