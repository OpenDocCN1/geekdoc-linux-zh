# Music

With iPads and other audio devices having ever higher capacities and online music stores gaining popularity, large digital music collections have become the norm. A quick peruse of my one collection reveals 3,206 songs by 202 artists. The is the equivalent of sometime nights of music! No matter how your audiophile compares to mine, you can admit that finding that one favorite song among the rest of it would be a daunting task without some trusty computerized assistance. You'll learn more about managing your music collection in Chapter 14, using a handy Dash Rhythmbox, but in the meantime, the Dash has plenty to offer.

Click the musical symbol at the bottom of the Dash, and your music collection will be spread out before you, assuming you've transferred it to the computer (if not, see Chapter 14 to learn how to do this). In exactly the same manner as you work with other types of file, the Music search is capable of matching more than just a song's filename—it also looks as song title, artist name, and album title. What's more, the search results come back with album artwork displayed for each song—a nice touch that makes the experience feel a little bit closer to rooting through physical stacks of records.

When you search for something, the results will be displayed in three bands this time—Songs, Albums, and Available for purchase. The last one has to do with the music store that's built into Ubuntu—more than in Chapter 14. The first two show only those songs and albums that are saved on your computer. From here on, it's pretty similar to what you had with the Files & Filters lens—you can click Show results to see a full list of everything that matches your search terms, and you can click Filter results to narrow your search. The only difference is, that time, you can filter by Decade and Genre rather than by Size and File type. Neat, huh?

# Nautilus: The Fully Featured File Manager

The Dash is a handy tool for finding your files in a hurry, as you've just seen. It's not so useful if you need to move, collect, or otherwise organize your files. It's also light on more advanced features, like accessing files over the network and transferring documents to USB memory sticks and other devices. Fortunately, Ubuntu has something a bit more substantial to handle that sort of thing in the form of Nautilus. Nautilus is the program that creates the file-viewing and organizing interface on your system. You may not have thought of an operating system's file manager as a program before, but in fact, that is what it is. (The Windows file manager is called Windows Explorer.) # The Sidebar

Nautilus has a lot of interesting features that deserve mention, and the most obvious of these is the sidebar, which appears at the left side of the window. The sidebar allows you to view a variety of information via a couple of select-able views. You can make your choices by going to View Sidebar in the menu and choosing either Places or Tree.

The default in Ubuntu is Places, which is a sort of quick navigation tool. In Places, you will find icons representing various data-storage locations available to your system, such as your Home folder, filesystem, and network shares you are connected to (more than in a moment), and any removable storage media or devices that you have connected to or your system. Clicking any of these icons will show the contents of that location in the right panel of the Nautilus window. The other view is Tree, which provides you with an expandable hierarchical view of your filesystem.

# Now You See It; You Don’t

The sidebar is a handy feature, but sometimes you may prefer to have more space to view the contents of your window and thus want to get rid of the bar temporarily. You can do this easily by going to View > Sidebar in the Nautilus menu and by deselecting Sidebar. The checkmark to that entry will disappear, with the sidebar. (Alternatively, press F9 on the keyboard to do the same). To get the sidebar back, return to the View > Sidebar menu and select Sidebar again. The checkmark will reappear, with the sidebar itself.

If you'd prefer not to hide the sidebar but still appreciate a little more space, you can change its size instead. Move your mouse over the gray line that marks the boundary between the sidebar and the rest of the window (note that there's also a subtle change in the shade of the ground color on either side of the line). When it's hovering over it, the mouse pointer will change from its usual appearance, a single arrow, to a horizontal array of arrows pointing in opposite directions. This tells you that the border of the sidebar, the thin gray line, can be dragged to resize the sidebar. Getting the pointer directly over the line can be a little frustrating, in that it might take a few goes to get the double arrow to appear, but once it does, click and drag to resize the sidebar.

# Creating, Naming, and Renaming Folders

Creating a folder is a simple enough task, and there are two ways of going about it. The easiest (in my opinion) is to right-click any empty space within a Nautilus window and select Create Folder in the pop-up menu that appears. If you prefer using menus over right-clicking empty space, you can instead start things selecting File > Create New Folder.

Regardless of where you made your Create Folder selection, a new folder with the Untitled Folder will appear in the Nautilus window. The name box of the folder will be highlighted and surrounded with a black box, which means you can immediately give that folder a name by simply typing one—nothing to click or do other than that. Press ENTER or click any open space in the window to complete the job.

If you later decide that the name you gave your folder needs tweaking or even a complete revision, you can rename it by right-clicking and selecting Rename in the pop-up menu. Alternatively, you can click the folder to highlight it and then select Edit > Rename. After that, you can type the new name for the folder and then press ENTER, or you can click any open space in the Nautilus window to seal the deal.
