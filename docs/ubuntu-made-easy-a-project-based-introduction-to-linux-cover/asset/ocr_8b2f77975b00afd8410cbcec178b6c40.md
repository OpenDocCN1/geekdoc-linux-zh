Then there were those annoying Windows-type problems that were especially prevalent back then, such as the constant freezes and restarts and the gradual system slowdowns, which were eventually remedied by reinstalling the system. Getting the software I needed to do the things I wanted to also took a toll on my wallet, and I began to rethink my initial resistance to Linux. With Linux’s multilingual support, system stability, and extensive free software packages, there were plenty of incentives for me to get over my preconceived notions about the typical Linux user.

After a few failed attempts at getting Linux to work on the offball, Frankenstein-like collection of junk that was my computer, I finally succeeded with a live-CD distribution called Knoppix, which worked well enough to reel me in. I eventually tried and used number of distributions that appeared, before eventually making my way to Fedora and then, ultimately, Ubuntu.

I started off with my three desktop machines at work and home set up dual-boot systems running Linux and Windows, but I found myself using only Linux. Although I expected to encounter numerous limitations in Linux that would force me to return to Windows, actually increased my productivity. Despite a lack of support for Windows streaming media, I was able to do more because of the extensive software that was now installed on my machine. Without having to fork out money I could afford to spend, I was able to manage my digital images, rip songs from CDs, create vector drawings, generate PDF files, and do a variety of things I wasn’t able to do under Windows. To be sure, a bit more hands-on tinkering had to be done back to get all the things you wanted to work in Linux working, but I am happy to report that things have continued to get easier and better, and those early frustrations I suffered trying to get things to work with this machine or that piece of hardware are mostly a thing of the past. Best of all, with even more distributions available as live CDs, which allow you to try Linux before you actually install it, you usually don’t even have to take a leap of faith to get started. Ubuntu, fortunately, is one such distribution.

Of course, getting started in the world of Linux, no matter which distribution you are using, can be a bit intimidating, especially when you don’t have anyone to help you out when you have questions. In fact, I started writing this book as a Linux primer for my mother, to whom I had given one of my extra computers with Red Hat installed. At that time, I was not planning on writing a book at all; it was that there really weren any books out written with average users in mind. The books available were all more or less geek oriented, and as such, they would not do for the mater. This created a somewhat elaborate instruction set that would tell her how to do everything she might want to do in a way she could understand. Since I was in Japan and she was in the United States at the time, I tried to make it as user-friendly as possible.
