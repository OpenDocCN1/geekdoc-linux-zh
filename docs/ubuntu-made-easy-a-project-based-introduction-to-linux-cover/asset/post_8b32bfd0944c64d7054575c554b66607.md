
- 5. Make sure that the Available to all users option is checked if other users of your computer will be using the connection.
- 6. Click Save to be asked to enter the password for your user account, so do that and then click Authenticate.
- 7. Network Manager will try to establish the connection using the details you provided. If it's successful, a message will pop up saying that the network was connected.
- 8. If that doesn't happen, click the Network icon on the top panel and choose Disconnect from the Wired Network section at the top of the pop-up menu that appears. Then click the Network icon again and select your network underneath where it says Wired Network.

NOTE If you are wondering what Internet Protocol (IP) and Domain Name Service (DNS) are all about, you can simply think of them in this way: DNS translates the easy-to-remember URLs that you have to know, such as http://www.google.com/, to numerical, or IP, addresses that the Internet can understand. The address http://www.google.com/ becomes http://209.85.229.104. You can type the numerical version into your browser later to see for yourself.

## Setting Up a Wireless Connection

With the right hardware, you can now surf the Web just about where you can catch a (radio) wave. Whether you happen to be at breakfast table, in a public library, or at your local coffee shop, you can now go online without physically hook up your computer to anything.

Fortunately, the process of setting up wireless networking in Ubuntu is easy; it's not very different from the procedure for wired networks, described in the previous section.

## Hardware

If you have led a wired existence or are just inexperienced in this area, there are a few things worth knowing. To get started, you need to have the right hardware. If you just want to go wireless, then all you need is a wireless network card (sometimes almost referred to as an 802.11a/b/g/n or Wi-Fi card). These are built into all modern laptops, for older models they are usually add-ons in the form of cards that pop into the PCMCIA slot on the side of your computer (as shown on the right of Figure 4-4).

Some wireless cards plug in to one of your machine's USB ports, or the case of desktop models, of its internal PCI slots. Although support for cards of this type has improved in the past couple of years, there are still gaps, and some cards can be tricky to deal with. You will find tips on how to deal with awkward cards in Chapter 22, but if you're looking for a sure thing, cards listed as having a Ralink 2500/RT2400, Realtek RTL8180, or Atheros AR9170 "chipset" are definitely supported by Ubuntu.

![](img/d35fa962e6f9664d66dc83916f426418_83_0.png)

Figure 4-4: All you need for a Wi-Fi setup—an access point and a network interface card

As for cards of the built-in or PC card variety, you will find support much better, but even then you have to make sure, or at least hope, that the card you are using is Linux compatible. No matter what wireless card you are using-wondering about, the easiest way to find out whether it works is just to try it. The Linux kernel now comes with many wireless drivers built in, and Ubuntu updates provide new ones, so if you use a card that is compatible with one of those drivers, things will be smooth sailing. If things don't seem to work, check Ubuntu’s list of supported cards (https://help.ubuntu.com/community/WirelessCardsSupported/) or check the Ubuntu forums to find a driver that is compatible or to see whether anyone has experience with your particular card. If you want to set up a wireless system in your home or office, then you will also need to get a wireless access point (WAP), shown on the left of Figure 4-4. Fortunately, Linux compatibility is not much of an issue in this department, because the access point does not physically interface with your computer and the settings are handled via your web browser.

NOTE Access points come in several types. The most common type is a wireless router, which many ISPs provide for free.

You should be aware, however, that a small minority of access points require you to use Internet Explorer to handle their setup chores. Although it is possible to get Explorer up and running in Linux via Wine (which you will learn more about in Chapter 19), you can't be 100 percent sure it will work. Unless you have a Windows machine somewhere in your house or office to handle such chores, it is probably a good idea to steer clear of access points of this kind.

### Activating Your Wireless Card

Setting up a Linux-compatible wireless card is relatively simple. First connect your point to your Internet source and then turn on the access point. Once it is up and running, plug your wireless card in to PCMCIA slot or USB port on your laptop (unless your wireless connection is built in, which case you can forgo this step). Any LEDs on the external card will most likely light up at this time.

If your access point was on when you booted your computer and your wireless card was in place during booting, you probably don't need to do anything to access the Internet. Just follow these steps:

- Click the Network icon on the top bar to reveal a drop-down menu showing the wireless signals in your vicinity and the strength of each signal (Figure 4-5).
- From that, select the signal for your access point (or any other wave you are entitled to latch onto) by clicking it.
- The Network icon will start to pulse, showing that your system is connecting to the Internet. Once a connection is made, the icon will morph into a small signal-strength indicator. Pretty handy.
- If you have a WEP or WPA password, you will be prompted to enter it. It can be useful to check Show password before you type can connect for any mistakes. Click Connect when you're finished.

> Most access points employ an added level of security in the form of a Wired Equivalent Privacy (WEP) or a Wi-Fi Protected Access (WPA) key. If you didn't choose your own password when you set up the access point, looking for a sticker on the base of the access point to see a default password is used. If you're trying to connect to a network for which you don't know the key, then chances are you not supposed to be making the connection in the first place. Naughty, naughty.

If you were not successful in making your wireless connection (the system will tell you that's the case), you have a few possibilities beyond the annoying card scenario. First, double-check to make sure your card is physically turned on. I know that sounds dumb, but I have been guilty of oversight myself on numerous occasions. Many laptops have a key you can press to enable or disable the wireless card; on mine, you have to press the function key (FN) and F2 at the same time (there's a little wireless antenna on the F2 key to denote this).

Another possibility is that you are not entering the correct WEP or WPA password. The first thing to check is case sensitivity. It sometimes matters the letters you type are upper-lowercase, make sure you type them as written. Additionally, some wireless access points require you to type the “wireless key” rather than a passphrase or password. A wireless key normally consists of a seemingly random string of numbers and letters is 10, 26, or even 58 characters long looks something like this: af56aec31c64a67b23fe5981. Try typing your own wireless key (not the one I used as an example!) instead of the passphrase and see if that works. If you don't have the wireless key written down, look through the access point's manual to see how to find it.

If you are trying to connect to a wireless network in a public place (for example, at a conference center, university or airport or a train), you may need to perform some additional steps to finish setting up connection. These might include visiting a specific website in your web browser to register for the service or providing your MAC address to the network administrator. For these of first open Firefox and type the address that the provider of the wireless connection should have given you. Hopefully, a page will load, and you will be asked to enter some details. If you're asked for your MAC address, you should instead click the Network icon and select Connection information from the menu that appears. Then find the Hardware Address (which will look similar to the one in Figure 4-6) and copy it and give it to whoever needs it. This is your MAC address.
