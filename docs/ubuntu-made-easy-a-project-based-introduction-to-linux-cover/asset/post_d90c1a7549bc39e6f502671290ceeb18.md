## What Is Linux?

Your computer, despite being a collection of highly sophisticated parts, is really just... well, a collection of highly sophisticated parts. On its own, it can do nothing other than switch on and off a disk or drive. For it to do anything truly useful, it needs an operating system (OS) to guide it. The OS takes a well-owed but completely uneducated hunk of a machine and educates it, at least so that it will understand what you want it to.

You already know of and have probably used at least one of the operating systems that exist today, or did in the past. Windows and Mac OS are such operating systems, as is Unix, the commercial-grade operating system behind many Internet sites and databases. And don't think that computers are the only place you run into operating systems. Those of you with smartphones, who are themselves miniature computers, are probably interacting daily with the Android operating system or iOS, the operating system on iPhones and iPads.

Linux is yet another operating system. It is, however, different from other operating systems in terms of both its capabilities and its heritage. Linux was not created by a corporation or by some wannabe to make money. The Linux core, referred to as the kernel, was created by computer enthusiast Lin Torvalds, a member of Finland's ethnic minority, who wanted to create a Unix-like system that would work on home computers—particularly him.

Rather than keeping his creation to himself, Torvalds opened it up to the world, so to speak, and computer geeks around the globe to make it better and more powerful. It is this combination of applications around the core of the Linux kernel that is the essence of all Linux distributions today.

Linux has acquired many fans and followers since its creation in 1991. Those devote praise Linux for its many features, as well as for being robust, reliable, free, and open. Despite these positive characteristics, Linux is, on its own, just a text-based system. There is no desktop, and there are no windows or charming icons to make you feel safe and comfy when you are behind the keyboard. Powerful it may be, Linux is strictly a black-screen, command line–driven operating system. I guess you could think of it as DOS on steroids, though a Linuxist would surely cringe at the thought.

Although you can use Linux by itself, accomplishing all your tasks by typing commands on a black screen (the most common way of doing things when Linux is used as a server), you don't have to do that. It is fair to say that with the advent in 1984 of the Macintosh and its easy-to-use graphical user interface (GUI, pronounced "goo-ee"), DOS users began suffering something akin to GUI envy. They began clamoring for a GUI to call their own. The final result was Windows, which gave DOS a GUI and eased many command users into the Microsoft world.

Similarly, many members of Linux world felt the need and desire to go graphical. The community at large developed GUIs (called window managers and desktop environments) and a subsystem with which to handle them (somewhat confusingly referred to as the Windows System). The graphical desktop environment that is included in Ubuntu distribution—Unity—is one example of the fruit of that development.

## What Is a Distribution?

An operating system consists of a lot of files that perform a lot of different functions. And because there is no Linux corporation to package and distribute the files that make Linux, the task of getting Linux onto your computer in working order, along with the applications you are likely to want, has fallen to a varied group of entities—companies, universities, groups, and even private individuals. These entities created Linux system and application collections called *distributions*, or *distros*. You could bypass such distros and try to collect everything you'd need to set up a system yourself, but would undoubtedly lose your mind in the process. Most people, even the geekiest, opt for the distros.

There are, of course, a large number of distros, such as businesses, educators, gamers, students, system administrators, and specific language users. What makes each distro different is the software that is bundled with the Linux kernel, well as other convenience features such as the package (or application) installation mechanism and the installer for the system itself. Some distros are especially appropriate for home users because of their ease of installation. Ubuntu, a relative newcomer to the Linux world, is one of these, joining other distros that have long been popular in the ease-of-use arena, such as openSUSE and Fedora.
