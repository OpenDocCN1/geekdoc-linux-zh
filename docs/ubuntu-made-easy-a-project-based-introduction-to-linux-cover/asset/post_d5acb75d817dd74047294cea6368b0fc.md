# ABOUT THE AUTHORS

# Rickford Grant

My computing life began long ago, in the Commodore/Atari days. No doubt inspired by Alan Alda's television commercials at the time, I purchased my first machine, an Atari 600XL with a cassette drive for storage and 16KB of RAM—more than I thought I would ever need. Most of my time on that machine, I must admit, was spent playing cartridge-based games and transcribing pages and pages of machine code from the now-defunct magazine Antic to create even more games. Eventually, my interest in computers increased, especially after seeing my first (and actually the first) Macintosh at the UCLA bookstore. The very in-your-face nature of the Mac's operating system caused me to become an operating system maniac. To date, I have worked with different operating systems, including Mac OS up to and including OS X, and even Windows from 3.1 onward, and even IBM's most forgotten OS/2.

Though tempted to join the Linux fray, I continued to steer away from it for a long time. I could not help see it, as many others do, as a system for never-seen-the-light-of-day-faced, late-night Dr Pepper-drinking Domino's pizza-eating comp-geeks. However, when I moved to Japan and was suddenly surrounded by machines loaded with Japanese versions of Windows, I encountered numerous problems, including language constraints. Since everything, including Help files, was written in Japanese and my reading skills in Japanese were less than spectacular, I ended up using only a fraction of the full potential of most software. Then there were those annoying Windows-type problems that were especially prevalent back then, such as the constant freezes and restarts and the gradual system slowdowns, which were eventually remedied by reinstalling the system. Getting the software I needed to do the things I wanted also took a toll on my wallet, and I began to rethink my initial resistance to Linux. With Linux’s multilingual support, system stability, and extensive free software packages, there were plenty of incentives for me to get over my preconceived notions about the typical Linux user.

After a few failed attempts at getting Linux to work on the offball, Frankenstein-like collection of junk that was my computer, I finally succeeded with a live-CD distribution called Knoppix, which worked well enough to reel me in. I eventually tried and used a number of distributions that appeared, before eventually making my way to Fedora and then, ultimately, Ubuntu.

I started off with my three desktop machines at work and home set up dual-boot systems running Linux and Windows, but I found myself using only Linux. Although I expected to encounter numerous limitations in Linux that would force me to return to Windows, it actually increased my productivity. Despite a lack of support for Windows streaming media, I was able to do more because of the extensive software that was now installed on my machine. Without having to fork out money I could afford to spend, I was able to manage my digital images, rip songs from CDs, create vector drawings, generate PDF files, and do a variety of things I wasn’t able to do under Windows. It was only a matter of time before my dual-boot setups became Linux-only setups. I ceased to be a...

To be sure, a bit more hands-on tinkering had to be done back to get all the things you wanted to work in Linux working, but I am happy to report that things have continued to get easier and better, and those early frustrations I suffered trying to get things to work with this machine or that piece of hardware are mostly a thing of the past. Best of all, with even more distributions available as live CDs, which allow you to try Linux before you actually install it, you usually don’t even have to take a leap of faith to get started. Ubuntu, fortunately, is one such distribution.

Of course, getting started in the world of Linux, no matter which distribution you are using, can be a bit intimidating, especially when you don’t have anyone to help you out when you have questions. In fact, I started writing this book as a Linux primer for my mother, to whom I had given one of my extra computers with Red Hat installed. At that time, I was not planning on writing a book at all; it was that there really weren't any books written with average users in mind. The books available were all more or less geek oriented, and as such, they would not do for the matter. This created a somewhat elaborate instruction set that would tell her how to do everything she might want to do in a way she could understand. Since I was in Japan and she was in the United States at the time, I tried to make it as user-friendly as possible.

My auntie caught hold of the "manuscript" and decided that I must try to publish it. I responded with the obligatory, "Yeah, right," putting it out of mind. My auntie, being a rather persistent character, did not relent, so I moved quickly submitting the book for publication. Surely a rejection would do the trick; however, I did not get a rejection slip, and instead my first book, Linux for Non-Geeks, based on Fedora Core I, was born. I later switched to Ubuntu, and the Non-Geeks line followed by Ubuntu for Non-Geeks.

I continued with two subsequent editions of the Ubuntu books, my new job was making it harder and harder to devote the time necessary to keep the book up-to-date and up-to-snuff and get it all written when it was needed. It seemed to bring in a co-author to keep things wisely. Enter Phil Bull. Phil was the technical editor on the third edition of Ubuntu for Non-Geeks, and because of his familiarity with the book, in addition to his work on Ubuntu documentation, seemed a perfect candidate for the job. The fact he lives closer than Stanage Edge, where I still imagine myself one day standing, pulling the Keira Knightley Pride and Prejudice pose, matters.

Su révé vacances aside, Phil on the project allowed us not only to update the book so as to reflect changes in the system and bundled applications but also expand to cover changes in available technology and hardware and to address suggestions from readers of past editions. The changes, I made, for even one more well-rounded volume before—a book that even better served the original purpose of the series: helping us get into the world of Linux easily and comfortably.

The collaboration with Phil continues with this follow-up, Ubuntu Made Easy, and though the title is new, the new book continues to improve and expand upon the previous Non-Geeks editions. It attempts, and so makes your transition into the world of Linux a smooth and easy one.

# Phil Bull

As my illustrious co-author already mentioned, I worked on two previous editions of this book, both as a writer and technical reviewer. It turned out to be so much (Rickford and the folks at No Starch are really great to work with) that I've come back for more!

So, what's my background? Well, computers have taken up all my free time since I was eight years old. I started playing games on my uncles' old DOS machine, and I found soon myself ripping apart home PC and putting it back again just to see how it worked. The geekness doesn't stop there; I taught myself to program using Microsoft Word and Visual Basic for Applications (ugh) when I was 14, and I worked in technical support for a while in my teens. At the end of high school, however, I realized physics was my calling in life, and now I'm a grad student in astrophysics at the University of Oxford in the UK. I haven't completely foreseen computers, though—far from it, in fact.
