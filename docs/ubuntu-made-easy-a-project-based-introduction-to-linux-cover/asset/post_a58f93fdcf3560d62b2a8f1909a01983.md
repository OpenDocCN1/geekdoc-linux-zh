
If you still have no luck connecting wirelessly, the drivers may not be installed for your card, or Ubuntu may not support the card. You can try plenty of tricks to get things working, head over to Chapter 22 and look for “Installing Drivers for Your Network/Wireless Card” on page 388.

## Switching Off or Refreshing Your Wireless Connection

Sometimes you will want to disconnect from a wireless network, such as when you use your laptop on an airplane or when you just want to connect to a different network, as you might when moving your laptop from one wireless hotspot to another.

To do this, click the Network icon to reveal the list of all wireless networks (that is, signals). In that list, click the Disconnect option below the name of the wireless network to which you are connected. Within a second or two, you will be disconnected.

If you want to turn off your wireless card (as you should do if you're boarding an airplane) and you don't have a physical way to do it, like a switch or a button, click the Network icon and then select Disable Wireless. And if you want to turn that card back again, just get back to that menu and select Enable Wireless again.

Sometimes, your wireless card might lose connection for no apparent reason, so you will want to refresh the connection (that is, turn it off and on) to get it back. The quickest way to do this is to click the Network icon and then click the name of the wireless network you're currently connected. You will be disconnected and automatically reconnected.

If that doesn't work, try disabling and enabling the wireless connection using the Enable Wireless option, as described earlier. If you still have no luck, try turning the wireless card off and on again using a switch or button, if you have one, unplug it and plug it back in again. Finally, if nothing seems to be working, try restarting the computer.

## Setting Up a Dial-up Connection

Much of the world is moving to high-speed Internet connections, you may still be using a dial-up Internet connection, which means you need to have a traditional dial-up modem to reach your box to the outside world. In case broadband surf-gods have forgotten, modems are those wonderful machines that whistle, chime, screech, and spit whenever you use your Internet provider. I suppose you could think of them as noisy phones in a world of good phones.

Now comes the bad news—in the world of Linux, very few internet modems are supported, and for those the process becomes frustrating. The main reason behind this compatibility problem is that most are software dependent and the software they depend on is part of, or designed for, Windows. Such modems are called Winmodems.

Of course, the Linux community has been working on ways to deal with these Winmodem beasts so that they will work with Linux systems. Though support for the wide variety of Winmodem modems out there is still spotty, things are better than before, so you might luck out. My advice is to hold off on the wondering and worrying and give your modem a try to see whether it works. If it does, then you're all set. If it doesn't, well, you have some options, and at least you haven't done anything to your system.

With all that intro-babble out of the way, let's get down to the steps for setting up your dial-up connection. First, get the settings information from your Internet provider. Most providers aim their operations at Windows and, often, Mac users, and very few offer support. Still, there is no technical reason for your Linux system not to work with their setups, so just nag and push until they give you the information you want. After, make sure your modem is connected to a live telephone connection—for example, the phone jack in your wall.

Now it's time to set up the connection. If you use the GNOME PPP program to do all of the setting up here, there's a caveat: it's not installed by default, and you need an Internet connection to install it! If you have access to some other Internet connection that you can use temporarily, connect to and use the Ubuntu Server to download and install GNOME PPP (see Chapter 6 to learn how to install software). The alternative is to use ppconfig command, which is installed by default but difficult to use. Find another computer and check out https://help.ubuntu.com/community/DialupModemHowto/SetUpDialer/ for instructions if you have no alternative but to use ppconfig.

Assuming that you got GNOME PPP installed using a borrowed Internet connection, you can follow these steps to set up your modem:

- 1. Open the Dash, search for GNOME PPP, and click to open it.
- 2. Click Setup and make you're looking at the Modem tab.
- 3. Click the Detect button to identify your modem. If you get a message saying that no modem was found on your system, you're out of luck—the best you can do is ask for advice on the Ubuntu forums (https://www.ubuntuforums.org/) or try selecting items from the Device list by trial and error.
- 4. How you choose the rest of the settings depends on how your modem and ISP are set up, select the defaults for now. If they don't work, trial and error is a potential way forward again.
- 5. Click Close to get back to the GNOME PPP window. Type your username and password and enter the phone number provided by your ISP.
- 6. Click Connect. You should hear your begin dialing, spitting, and churning sequence as it makes the connection with your provider.
- 7. If you manage to get connected, you can start your browsing, or whatever else you do online. If you get an error message, there's little you can do but take a deep breath and click the Setup button to tweak your settings.

## What to Do If Your Modem Isn't Compatible

Though it may sound that way, what I've just described is pretty much a best-case scenario. What happens, however, if your modem and Ubuntu do not see eye to eye? Well, there are a few options. My, first radical suggestion is to dump your dial-up ISP and find a broadband provider that covers your local area. If that is not an option, then you have two ways to go. One is to try get around with your modem to see whether you get it to work. This is a slightly complicated process, but there are instructions at https://help.ubuntu.com/community/DialupModemHow/. If you are of heart or a novice user, you may find the process to entail a bit more than what you're willing to deal with.

The simpler, although costly, way to get your modem to work is to chase a hardware modem. Hardware modems are not software defined, so they work with any operating system. You can think of them as telephones without a headset.

Some might come in two forms: internal and external. For the internals, the USRobotics modems 56K V.92 Performance Pro Modem (internal slot) and 56 PC Card Modem (PC card slot, for laptops) are true hardware modems that are easily available and ready to work. You can check out the USRobotics site (http://www.usr.com/) for more information on these models, though checking the Ubuntu for suggestions is always a good idea. Perhaps the safest of all solutions is to buy an external dial-up modem.
