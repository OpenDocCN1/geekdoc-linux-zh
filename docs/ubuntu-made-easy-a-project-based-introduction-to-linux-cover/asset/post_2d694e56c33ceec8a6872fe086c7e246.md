
Android-powered device in Ubuntu is quite simple, and it's only slightly different from working with one of Apple’s “i” devices. Basically, this is the process:

- 1. Connect your Android-powered device to your computer using the USB cable that came with it.
- 2. In the dialogue that appears on your Android device, asking what you want to do now that you are connected to an external device, press the Mass storage button.
- 3. In the next screen that appears on your Android device, press Connect storage to PC.
- 4. The first time you do this, another small window will then appear on your device telling you that some apps might not work when your device is connected as a USB storage device. Click OK.

Similar to what happens in the case of the iPhone, iPod, and iPad, or one more windows will appear asking what you want to do with the photos and music files on your device, and an icon for your device should appear in the side pane of any open file manager and in the Launcher.

To “eject” your device, right-click either the file manager or Launcher icon for the device and then click Eject in the pop-up menu. On your phone, press Disconnect storage from PC.

## What About Kindles, NOOKs, and Android 3.0+ “Honeycomb”-Based Devices?

In the very recent past, getting Kindles or NOOKs or tablets with the newer Android 3.0+ operating system (known as “Honeycomb”) to work with Ubuntu involved a lot of around the command line. The reason was that these devices did not use the USB protocol that most of the other devices in this class use. Instead they used a transfer protocol called MTP (Media Transfer Protocol) which was not Linux (or ever Mac) friendly. The good news is that while these devices still use the MTP file transfer protocol, Linux, as of this Ubuntu 12.04 release, has no problems working with these devices. This means that all connection and file transfer problems are gone, and your device should work with Ubuntu just like all the others mentioned in the chapter.

## Copying Songs from Your Audio Device to Hard Disk

One feature of digital media players that aren't available in iTunes is the ability to copy songs from your iPod to hard disk. If you have an iPod (iPhone, iPod Touch included) that you were last using with a Mac, you may find that although you can read the files on iPod, you cannot write files to it. If this is the case, most likely your iPod is formatted in Apple's HFS+—although this in itself is not a problem. The problem is that HFS+ has journaling. Journaling is an HFS+ feature that acts to protect the filesystem from damage due to power surges, power failures, or hardware breakdowns.

As fate would have, however, the Linux kernel doesn't deal well with journaling enabled devices. Fortunately, the tweak to fix this is quite simple, and it isn't particularly geeky: You just have to disable journaling on your iPod.

Disabling journaling is easy if you have a Mac nearby. Just plug your iPod into a Mac, which automatically opens, and then run Disk Utility (in the Utilities before the folder). When Disk Utility opens, select your iPod (by name) in the left pane of the window, press the OPTION key, and then choose Disable Journaling in the File menu. After a moment, journaling on your iPod will be disabled without any loss of data. If you connect your iPod to your Linux machine again, you will be able to use it more or less like normal.

## Ejecting your iPod or Other Digital Device from Rhythmbox

When you're done working with iPod or other digital device in Rhythmbox, you can eject it in one of two ways. Either click its entry in the left pane of the window and then click the Eject button near the top, or right-click its Launcher icon and select Eject.
