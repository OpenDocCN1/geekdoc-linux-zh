In practical terms, this means it can take a while for a problem to be fixed, although the actual timescale will depend on how important the bug is judged to be. I normally reckon on waiting two to three months for an "average" bug. Having said that, my Rhythmmbox bug hasn't been fixed—five years later! (To be fair, it is a particularly minor problem that people are even likely to experience.)

Once a bug is fixed, its status is changed to Released, and the Ubuntu developers will often provide an updated package that you can install to fix the problem on your computer. Fixes aren't made straight away, since they have to go through a series of stringent tests to make sure that they don't screw anything else. This means that some bug fixes might not be available until the next version of Ubuntu.

# Faster Fixes

If you know that a problem has been fixed but an updated package hasn't been released, there may be a way around the delay—if you don't mind taking a small risk. When updates are being tested to get them ready for official release, they are made available online for anyone to try. There's a chance the updated versions could cause problems (after all, they're being tested!), but they're usually fine. There are also backported packages, which are unofficial updated versions of programs. These will have been tested, not just very rigorously, so use them at your own risk.

To gain access to less stable packages, open the Ubuntu Software Center, click Edit -> Software Sources, and make sure that Proposed updates and Unsupported updates are checked (as in Figure 21-8) and click Close. Now, go to the Dash, open the Update Manager, and Check to see which packages be updated.
