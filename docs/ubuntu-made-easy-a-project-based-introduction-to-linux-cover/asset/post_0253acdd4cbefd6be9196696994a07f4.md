# Speaking Ubuntu

It's worth noting that you will become a lot of weird phrases when dealing with Ubuntu, especially when searching for information on the Internet. I'm referring to seemingly incongruous phrases, such as Warty Warthog, Gary Hedgehog, Breezy Badger, Dapper Drake, Edgy Feisty, Fawn, Gutsy Gibbon, Hardy Heron, Intrepid Ice, Jasper Jalapako, Karma Kola, Lucid Lynx, Maverick Meerkat, Natty Narwhal, and Oneric Ocel. These are the unique names for each of the releases of Ubuntu since its first appearance in 2004. The important one for you to remember is that of the current release, which is the one on the book's disc: version 12.04, known as Precise Pangolin.

You are also likely to come across a few other variations of the Ubuntu theme. These are Kubuntu, a KDE-based version of Ubuntu; Edubuntu, a special version of Ubuntu designed for use in the classroom; Xubuntu, a lightweight version of Ubuntu based on the XFCE desktop; Lubuntu, another lightweight version of Ubuntu based on the LXDE desktop environment; and Ubuntu Studio, a new flavor of Ubuntu, aimed at “creative people,” which contains a strong suite of graphics, video, and music applications. There are also two specialized versions: Mythuntu, specifically designed for your home theater PC with MythTV, and UbuntuTV, designed to create a TV-based online streaming and viewing environment.

# Going for a Dip

To get a taste of what Ubuntu is all about (and to check out your hardware to see whether it's all comfy-zy with Ubuntu), there is probably no better way to run Ubuntu directly from the live CD. To do this, place the Ubuntu CD in your disc drive and restart your machine. When the machine starts up, it should boot up from the CD, and after a second or two, you will see a purple Ubuntu startup screen with a couple of icons at the bottom of it. If the screen does not appear and your machine restarts, then very likely your machine's BIOS settings need to be changed to allow you to boot from a CD. Worry not: the BIOS settings aren't much of a hassle. Although the procedure might sound rather technical, altering the BIOS

You can access your machine's BIOS by restarting and then pressing whatever the onscreen instructions assign to accessing the BIOS setup. This is usually DELT, or F2, F10, F12, and ESC all common—not all machines are the same. If the onscreen information passes by fast that you miss it, you can check your user's manual to see what the correct key is.

Once you get into the BIOS setup, change the boot sequence so that your CD drive is first. To do this, you'll probably have to use the arrow keys on your keyboard to navigate between different options and the ENTER key to select them. Basic instructions on how to do this are normally onscreen. When you're finished, make sure you save your changes and restart the computer.

Once your machine boots from the live CD and you see the purple Ubuntu startup screen, either you can wait for a few seconds for Ubuntu to start booting into the live CD, or you can press a key (any key) to display a menu where you can choose your language and other settings. The default settings should suffice for your purposes, leave the keyboard untouched for a few seconds, and the screen will give way to a loading screen (all purple).

Click Try Ubuntu, and the Install window will give way to a desktop with row of icons down the left side. You should now be able to use your mouse as well as the keyboard, feel free to explore. And remember, your hard disk will go untouched,you're not going to change, let alone hurt.
