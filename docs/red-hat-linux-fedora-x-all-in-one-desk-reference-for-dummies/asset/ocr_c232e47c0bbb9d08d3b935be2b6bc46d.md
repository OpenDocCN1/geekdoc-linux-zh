# 276 Connecting with DSL

+   **ADSL:** Asymmetric DSL, the common form of DSL, has much higher download speeds (from the Internet to your PC) upload speeds (from your PC to the Internet). ADSL can have download speeds of up to 8 Mbps and upload speeds of up to 1 Mbps. ADSL works best when your location is within about 2 miles (12,000 feet) of your central office. ADSL service is priced according to the download and upload speeds you want. on the same line for voice calls. A popular form of ADSL is G.lite, which has a maximum download speed of 1.5 Mbps and an upload speed of 512 Kbps.
* **ISDN DSL:** ISDN DSL (ISDN is an older technology called *Integrated Services Digital Network*) is a special type of DSL that works at distances of up to five miles between your phone and the central office. The downside is that ISDN only offers downstream (from the Internet to your PC) and upstream (from your PC to the Internet) speeds up to 144 Kbps.
* **DSL:** Symmetric DSL provides the equal download and upload speed of up to 1.5 Mbps. DSL is priced according to the speed you want, with higher speeds costing more. The closer you are to the phone company office, the faster the connection you get.

DSL speeds are typically specified by two numbers separated by a slash, as this: 1500/384. The numbers refer to data-transfer speeds in kilobytes per second (that is, thousands-of-bits per second, abbreviated Kbps). The first number is the download speed, the second the upload. Thus 1500/384 means you can expect to download from the Internet at a maximum rate of 1,500 Kbps and upload to the Internet at 384 Kbps (or 1.5 Mbps). If your phone's line is not perfect, you may not get these maximum rates — both ADSL and DSL adjust the speeds to suit existing lines.

The price of DSL service depends on which variant — ADSL, ISDN, or DSL — you select. For most users, the primary choice is ADSL (or more accurately, the G.lite form of ADSL) with transfer rating speeds of 1500/128.

# Typical DSL setup

To get DSL for your home or business, you have to contact a DSL provider. In addition to your phone company, you can find many other DSL providers. No matter who provides the DSL service, some work has to be done at your central office — the place where your phone lines connect to the rest of the phone network. The work involves connecting your phone line to equipment that can work with the DSL modem at home or office. The central office equipment and the DSL modem at your location can then do whatever magic is needed to send and receive digital data over your phone line.
