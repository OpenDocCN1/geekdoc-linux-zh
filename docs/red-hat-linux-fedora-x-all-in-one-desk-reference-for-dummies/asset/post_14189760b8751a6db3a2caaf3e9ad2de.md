
DSL and cable-modem service connect you to the Internet and also act as your Internet Service Provider (ISP); in addition to improved speed, what you're paying for is an IP address and your e-mail accounts. If you use a dial modem to connect to the Internet, first you have to connect to the phone line (for which you pay the phone company) and then select and pay ISP — which gives you a phone number to dial and all the necessary goods (such as an IP address and e-mail accounts).

DSL stands for Digital Subscriber Line. DSL uses your existing phone line to send digital data in addition to normal analog signals (whereas digital data is represented by different frequencies). The phone line goes from your home to a central office, where the line connects to the phone company's network — by the way, the connection from your home to the central office is called the local loop. When you sign up for DSL service, the phone company hooks up your phone line to special equipment at the central office. That equipment can separate digital data from voice. From then on, your phone line can carry digital data that is directly sent to an Internet connection at the central office.

### How DSL works

A special box called a DSL modem takes care of sending digital data from your PC to the phone company's central office over your phone line. Your PC can connect to the Internet with the same phone line that you use for voice calls — you can make calls even as the line is being used for DSL. Figure 1-1 shows a typical DSL connection to the Internet.

Your PC talks to the DSL modem through an Ethernet connection, which means that you need an Ethernet card in your system. Your PC sends digital data over the Ethernet connection to the DSL modem. The DSL modem sends the digital data at different frequencies than those used by analog voice. The voice signals occupy a small portion of all the frequencies the phone line can carry. DSL uses the higher frequencies to transfer digital data, both voice and data can travel on the same phone line.

The distance between your home and the central office — the loop length — is a factor in DSL's performance. Unfortunately, the phone line can reliably carry the DSL signals over only a limited distance—three miles or less, which means you can get DSL only if your home (or office) is located within three miles of your central office. Your phone company can tell you whether your location can be DSL or not. It has a website where you can type in your phone number and get a response about DSL availability. For example, www.dslavailability.com for U.S. locations.

## DSL alphabet soup: ADSL, DSL, DSL

I have been using the term DSL as if there were only one kind of DSL. As you may imagine, nothing is ever simple. There are in fact three variants of DSL, each with different features. Take a look:

- **ADSL:** Asymmetric DSL, the common form of DSL, has much higher download speeds (from the Internet to your PC) than upload speeds (from your PC to the Internet). ADSL can have download speeds up to 8 Mbps and upload speeds up to 1 Mbps. ADSL works best when your location is within about 2 miles (12,000 feet) of your central office. ADSL service is priced according to the download and upload speeds you want on the same line for voice calls. A popular form of ADSL is Glite, which has a maximum download speed of 1.5 Mbps and an upload speed of 512 Kbps.
- **ISDN DSL:** ISDN DSL (ISDN is an older technology called Integrated Services Digital Network) is a special type of DSL that works at distances of up to five miles between your phone and the central office. The downside is that ISDN only offers downstream (from the Internet to your PC) and upstream (from your PC to the Internet) speeds up to 144 Kbps.
- **DSL:** Symmetric DSL provides equal download and upload speed of up to 1.5 Mbps. DSL is priced according to the speed you want, with higher speeds costing more. The closer you are to the phone company office, the faster the connection you get.

![](img/d9230b719dc0efd80851f363d8cf571_302_0.png)

DSL speeds are typically specified by two numbers separated by a slash, as this: 1500/384. The numbers refer to data-transfer speeds in bits per second (that is, thousands of bits per second, abbreviated Kbps). The first number is the download speed, the second the upload. Thus 1500/384 means you can expect to download from the Internet at a maximum rate of 1,500 Kbps and upload to the Internet at 384 Kbps (or 1.5 Mbps). If your phone's line is not perfect, you may not get these maximum rates — both ADSL and DSL adjust the speeds to suit existing lines.

The price of DSL service depends on which variant — ADSL, ISDN, or DSL — you select. For most users, the primary choice is ADSL (or more accurately, the Glite form of ADSL) with transfer rating speeds of 1500/128.

### Typical DSL setup

Because of the need to set up your line at the central office, it takes some time after you place an order to get your line ready. The first step for you is to check out DSL providers and see if you can actually get the service. Because DSL can work only over certain distances—typically less than 2.5 miles—between your location and the central office, you have to check if you are within that limit. Contact your phone company to verify. You may be able to check this on the Web. Try typing into Google (www.google.com) the words DSL, availability, and your local phone company's name. You probably get a DSL site where you can type in your phone number to find out DSL is available for your number.
