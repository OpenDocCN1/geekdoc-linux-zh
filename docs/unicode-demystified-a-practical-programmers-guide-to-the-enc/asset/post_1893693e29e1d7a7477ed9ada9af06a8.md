## The GNU Connection
page 14

An operating system is the low-level software that schedules tasks, allocates storage, and handles the interfaces to peripheral hardware, such as printers, disk drives, and your screen, keyboard, and mouse. An operating system has two main parts: the kernel and the system programs. The kernel allocates machine resources, including memory, disk space, and CPU (page 46), to all other programs that run on the computer. The system programs perform higher-level housekeeping tasks, often acting as servers in a client/server relationship. Linux is the name of the kernel that Linus Torvalds presented to the world in 1991 and that many others have worked to enhance, stabilize, expand, and make secure.

## 1983
Richard Stallman announces the GNU Project for creating an operating system, both kernel and system programs, and presents the GNU Manifesto, which begins as follows:

GNU, which stands for G's Not UNIX, is the name for the complete UNIX-compatible software system I am writing so I can give it away to everyone who can use it.

Some years later Stallman added a footnote to the preceding sentence when he realized that it was creating confusion:

The wording was careless. The intention was that nobody would have pay for *permission* to use the GNU system. But the words do not make this clear, and people often interpret them as saying that copies of GNU should always be distributed at little or no charge. That was not the intent; later on, the manifesto mentions the possibility of companies providing the service for a profit. Subsequently I have learned to distinguish carefully between “free” in the sense of freedom and “free” in the sense of price.
