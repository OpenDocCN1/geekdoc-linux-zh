
## Chapter 19: The Essential Road Kit

### The List

### Before You Leave

This short chapter provides a list of “must-haves,” safety items, and other goodies for the mobile Linux user. Although you might think this list of notebook toys is overly simple, you’d be surprised at how many times people forget some of the basics when hitting the road.

I’m sure you’ll have your own list of favorite toys and essentials when you hit the road, such as a soldering iron, rosin, and heat sink if you’re a Linux hardware freak, or a six-pack of Jolt Cola and a case of caffeinated Penguin peppermints if you’re a Linux code geek.

### Ultimate Linux Geek Site?

Got root? Need a jolt, but don’t know where to get the very best hyper-caffeinated products to use during your all-night Linux coding sessions? Browse to the ultimate Linux geek site at http://www.thinkgeek.com. You’ll find at least a dozen different products, including caffeine-spiked water bottles that can give you that oh-so-needed midnight lift. And by the way, a tin of Penguin peppermints is an essential part of any mobile Linux road kit.

### The List

- **AC adapter and cables.** You’ll need at least one to keep your notebook’s battery charged in between day trips while on the road.

- **AC extension cord.** You might or might not need one of these, depending on the length of your notebook’s AC adapter. Most cords for Sonys are 7.5 feet. Cords for IBM ThinkPads are 9.5 feet. Apple iBook cords are 10 feet, 2 inches.

- **Acoustic coupler.** Unless you’re going to Timbuktu, Mali, you might want to forego an acoustic coupler—that is, until you find yourself at a pay phone without a phone jack when you need to check or send e-mail.

- **Alarm system.** Unfortunately, there are many dirtbags who would love to snarf your brand-new $3,500 laptop. You might want to invest in a motion-sensitive alarm if you park your notebook on a desk or by your side at the airport. However, the best advice is to not leave your notebook unattended!

- **Blank express delivery envelopes and labels.** Save time and effort by packing a few blank overnight envelopes before you go on your trip. Many airports, hotels, and businesses have overnight-service mailboxes you can use.

- **Blank floppy disks.** If your notebook has a floppy port, take along a few blank floppy disks. Even better, take along an emergency Linux boot disk. Use Red Hat's `mkbootdisk` command to create one before you go on the road.

- **Blank Zip disks.** If your notebook has a Zip or LS-120 drive, take along a spare in case you want to share information.

- **Business cards.** Always have some on hand. These aren't as trendy as beaming your Palm PDA business card to another Palm, but business cards, descended from Carte(s) de Visite (see http://www.storereviews.com/actors.html), serve quite well for introductions and contacts.

- **Calling card.** If you have your calling card number memorized, that's great! If you don't have a calling card from your long-distance provider, research costs first. Some phone companies charge exorbitant fees for using a calling card. One alternative is to buy a specific-dollar-amount calling card and keep it on hand in case you need it. (Check for any expiration clause when you buy these cards.)

- **Car confirmation number.** Don't forget your rental confirmation!

- **Carrying case.** The essential road warrior's kit bag. Many mobile Linux users have more than one notebook case. Unless you have a Panasonic Toughbook (with a sealed LCD, magnesium case, sealed keyboard, internal dampeners, and dust-sealed hinges), you'll need a good notebook case. Cases come in many styles from many manufacturers and are made from a variety of materials, ranging from fine leather to nylon and metal alloys. But if you just need a slipcover case for your notebook with the slight protection afforded by neoprene, browse to http://www.thepouch.com to order a low-cost, custom-fit cover for your Linux laptop.

- **Cash.** Until taxi drivers take plastic, you'll need some cash on the road. Although I've actually seen geeks at LinuxWorld pay for coffee with a credit card, cash still reigns in the fast-food line.

- **Cell phone.** The smaller, the better. One-rate plans now provide coast-to-coast service. If you're going overseas, check on coverage and availability of service. Better yet, lease a satellite phone (see http://www.gmpcs-us.com/rental/). At the time this chapter was written, rates were only $450 per week for 64KBps at $13 per minute. It's too bad Iridium went down the tubes!

- **Cell phone car adapter.** This is essential unless you're taking a short road trip and either you carry an extra charged battery or your cell phone works for 100 hours on a single charge.

- **Credit cards.** They're an evil, nasty necessity in today's world. Use only one, find a credit card with no annual fee, and always pay off your balance by the end of the month.

- **Day planner.** Why anyone using a Palm PDA, StarOrganizer, korganizer, plan, or ical for Linux would need one of these is beyond me. However, some people swear by them and like to stuff other things inside, such as checkbooks, calculators, and credit cards.

- **Digital camera.** Early models of these instruments were bulky and expensive and required lots of online storage, such as a separate hard drive encased in a shoulder or belt pack. Today you can get a reasonably inexpensive digital camera with re-movable Compact Flash storage that provides at least 5×7-inch pictures for less than $300. Many of these devices have a serial or USB port, but the fastest way to transfer data is by using the Compact Flash card in your notebook's PC Card slot. If you have a serial camera, take a look at the gPhoto client (http://www.gphoto.org), which supports nearly 100 different digital cameras under Linux and X11. You'll need the latest gtk+ software libraries and the gcc C compiler to build and install the software. However, you'll be pleasantly surprised to find that gphoto works better than the commercial support software provided with your camera. (In many cases, it excels by having more features.) In some instances, espe-cially with a close-up lens kit, your digital camera can also function as a scanner!

- **Driver's license.** Flying by E-ticket? You won't if you forget your driver's license (unless you have a police report stating that your wallet was stolen).

- **External CD-ROM drive.** In the recent past (12 months ago), these were bulky hunks of plastic with thick cables and brick transformers. Nowadays, most have trimmed down to slick and sleek devices that power off your PCMcia card. Many initialize as `/dev/hdg` under Linux, depending on the number of spindles in use on your notebook. They are usually used with an ultralight or mini-notebook computer. These devices suck power like crazy when used externally, although some models are equipped with internal rechargeable batteries or an external battery pack.

- **Flash card for backup.** This is an excellent, safe choice for backing up important documents and files. The drawback is that flash memory (using ATA or Compact Flash Type II cards) is still somewhat expensive. However, if you read David Hind's PCMcia-HOWTO, you might be able to craft a bootable filesystem on a card, especially if your notebook has a floppy drive. These cards require little power and have life spans measured in 100,000 or more read/write cycles.

- **Gel wrist pillow.** This is a great item to use, especially if you can find one that can double as a neck pillow for when you're commuting. Although many larger notebooks have taken ergonomic design lessons from Apple's PowerBook line, ultralight and mini-notebook users may avoid Carpal Tunnel Syndrome (see http://www.carpal-tunnel-syndrome.net/carpaltunnelsyndrome.htm).

- **GPS.** Many different Global Positioning Satellite receivers are supported under Linux. In many cases, you can easily access raw satellite data just by hooking up the GPS to your notebook's serial port and then running xminicom with the correct speed and protocols. For example, the DeLorme Tripmate receiver works at 4800N81. After plugging in the unit, starting xminicom, and then typing `ASTRAL`, you'll receive standard National Marine Electronics Association (NMEA) data at your console. Most Linux software works by translating received strings of data and then properly displaying the data in a window (such as date, time, satellite number, altitude, speed, direction, and latitude and longitude).

- **Hotel confirmation number.** Don't forget to write this down or put it in your PDA, especially if you're going to arrive late to LinuxWorld or Comdex!

- **International phone and electrical adapters.** If you're traveling abroad, these adapters (usually sold in convenient pouches) can be a lifesaver. There's nothing like being able to overcome technical obstacles without having to search for an electronics store.

- **Itinerary.** Itineraries help keep you organized, on track, and on time. Use your favorite Linux software or hardware PDA to input your schedule, and then print two copies. Leave one at home, and put the other in your briefcase.

- **Linux installation CD.** Recovery floppy aside, this is the next best thing to having a backup. Of course, if you have a CD writer, you could try burning a bootable image of your Linux filesystem before you go on the road. See Vinfried Trumper's CD-Writing-HOWTO (found under the `/usr/doc/HOWTO` directory or at http://www.linuxdoc.org).

- **Meeting schedule.** A possible subset of your itinerary, meeting schedules should also include names, phone numbers, and e-mail or Web information about the attendees. Keep a copy on your notebook, a hard copy back at the office, and an extra copy in your briefcase.

- **Microdrive for Type II slot.** This is one of the newest and hottest accessories for the mobile Linux geek. These incredibly small (1-inch) hard drives developed by IBM are manufactured in the same form factor, and in many instances, they can replace Compact Flash cards in digital cameras. Although currently they are available only in 170MB and 340MB sizes, these low-power drives portend the future for removable storage. You'll need a Type II PCMCIA adapter in order to use one
