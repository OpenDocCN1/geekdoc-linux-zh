You can also use this approach to make a copy of your entire Linux filesystem (although this really isn’t necessary). For example, if you have enough room on your hard drive or another partition, you can back up everything with this:

```
`# tar cvsf /linux.tgz /`
```

# Be Careful!

If you want to make a copy of your entire filesystem, be sure to unmount any unwanted partitions or other filesystems, such as /mnt/dos, or you’ll back up those files, too!

Creating a single compressed file of an entire filesystem is known as archiving. You could consider a Linux distribution on CD-ROM as a permanent archive or snapshot of a filesystem. There are other approaches to backing up data that you can use with great success to reduce the amount of time, effort, and file sizes of the backup.

# ## Incremental Backups

The best way to back up your system is to devise a backup strategy, or schedule. You’ll have to decide whether it is worth the effort to perform backups on a daily, weekly, or monthly basis. For example, you could devise a strategy like this:

+   - First of the month: Full backup
+   - First day of second week: Backup of changed files
+   - First day of third week: Backup of changed files since first day of second week
+   - First day of fourth week: Backup of changed files since first day of third week

This approach has an advantage over backing up only once every month in that you would be able to restore a week’s worth of files and easily backtrack to your full backup. The disadvantage over a daily backup schedule is that you could lose six days of work. A daily, incremental backup schedule would look like this:

+   - Sunday: Full backup
+   - Monday: Backup of all changed files since full backup
+   - Tuesday: Backup of all changed files since Monday
+   - Wednesday: Backup of all changed files since Tuesday
+   - Thursday: Backup of all changed files since Wednesday
