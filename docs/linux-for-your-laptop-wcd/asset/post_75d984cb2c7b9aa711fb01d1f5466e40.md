
## Listening to Music CDs

Listening to music CDs while you work or travel can be a relaxing way to help pass the time. It can also provide extra stimulus to help you increase your productivity. Many different types of music and multimedia players are available for Linux. You'll soon develop your own set of favorite software to use during your Linux sessions.

If you don't use X11, you can still play music CDs. Some of the more popular console (text-based) audio CD player programs are `cdp` and `tcd`. The `cdp` command is also known as `cdplay` (the name of the symbolic link to the `cdp` command). This text-based player provides all the controls you need to play music CDs. If you insert an audio CD, type `cdp` and press Enter, the `cdp` program will start playing music starting at the CD's first track. When you use the `cdp` command, you'll be presented with an interactive menu that has controls to start, stop, pause, skip, and select tracks.

The `tcd` command is the text-based version of the GNOME `gtcd` audio CD player. Like `cdp`, this command may be used without X11. At the command line, type `tcd` and press Enter. You'll see the control dialog box shown in Figure 18.9.

![](img/70d85e6f4d1ff2750ca2a98ba3d9855c_423_0.png)

**Figure 18.9** The GNOME tcd player does not require X11.

The `tcd` player, unlike `cdp`, does not require you to use your keyboard's keypad keys. Instead, it relies on normal keys, such as P, U, E, S, M, G, and so on to control your CD. This makes `tcd` much easier to use on your notebook.

If you use X11, you'll find an overwhelming array of choices of graphical player software packages. Some of the most popular include `kscd`, `gtcd`, and `xplay`. The `xplay` client is the oldest program and does not require either KDE or GNOME software libraries. However, `gtcd` and `kscd` have several interesting features, such as the ability (if you have an active Internet connection) to grab your CD's title, artist, and names of individual tracks. This works when you configure the player with the correct hostname of a remote music track database server. After a CD is inserted, the client uses the CD's serial number to search the remote database. When a match is found, the information about your CD is sent back and displayed by the program.

![](img/70d85e6f4d1ff2750ca2a98ba3d9855c_424_0.png)

**Figure 18.10** The KDE kscd player can retrieve audio CD information from remote servers on the Internet.

For example, to launch the `kscd` client from the desktop panel, select Application Starter, Multimedia, CD Player. Next, insert your audio CD into your notebook. To configure `kscd` to use the Internet, click on the Tool button. You’ll then see the dialog box shown in Figure 18.10.

Make sure you have an active Internet connection, and then click to select a sample server. When finished, click on the OK button to save your selected server.

After you get the information, you can save it in a local database for use the next time you play the CD. Click on the small CDDb (CD database) icon in the `kscd` player. You’ll then see the dialog box shown in Figure 18.11.

![](img/70d85e6f4d1ff2750ca2a98ba3d9855c_424_1.png)

Use the dialog to save your CD's information into the local CD database (stored under the `/usr/share/apps/kscd/cddb` directory). The `kscd` client also has a great feature you can use—its icon will dock into your desktop's panel. This means you do not have to use the client's main window to control your playing session, freeing up valuable screen real estate. If you right-click on the `kscd` icon on your panel, you'll see the pop-up menu shown in Figure 18.12. You can use it to play, pause, stop, or jump back and forth through selected tracks.

# Ripping Audio CDs into MPEG

Listening to music CDs is a lot of fun, but carrying a case with half a dozen or more CDs while on the road can be a hassle. If you have enough drive storage, you can store the music from your CDs on your notebook and leave the CDs at home! Linux comes with many different utilities you can use to extract and convert music tracks from audio CDs into different sound formats. Of course, the most popular format and hottest topic on the Internet is MPEG (.mp3) audio. This section shows you how to “rip” tracks into .mp3 files. Storing music on your notebook this way is not only handy, but essential, especially if your ultralight mobile Linux notebook doesn’t have a CD-ROM drive!

# Choosing an Audio Format

There are many different types of sound formats, and Linux supports nearly every sound format in use today. However, the most common formats are .au (raw audio), .wav (Windows sound format), and .mp3 (MPEG III). Use .mp3 to store high-quality digital sound on your notebook; you’ll benefit from its compressed format.

# Using the Command Line

You can manually rip audio tracks from a CD to your hard drive, but you’ll most likely want to convert the track to .mp3 format. Use the `cdcd2wav` command to grab an audio track and save it on your hard drive. The track will be saved in .wav format. If you use a SCSI CD-ROM drive, you can quickly grab the first track of an audio CD by using `cdcd2wav`, along with your drive’s device specification:

```bash
# cdcd2wav -D 0,1,0
```

This specifies that the audio CD is on a SCSI drive with an ID of 1 on bus 0. After you press Enter, you’ll see this:

# DVD on the Horizon?

Despite the insipid machinations by the U.S. motion picture industry over the cracking of Digital Video Disc (DVD) encryption, Linux users might soon have a legal way to play and enjoy DVD movies. Although you can still download, build, and install the DeCSS decryption package and related programs from numerous sites on the Internet, a company named InterVideo announced in early 2000 that an inexpensive, binary-only (of course) driver would be developed and released for Linux six months later. Check http://www.intervideo.com/ for the latest details.

If you have a Creative Labs Dxr2 DVD card for your PC, you can download drivers from http://opensource.creative.com/. Note that Creative’s product is a hardware DVD decoder and will not work with notebooks!
