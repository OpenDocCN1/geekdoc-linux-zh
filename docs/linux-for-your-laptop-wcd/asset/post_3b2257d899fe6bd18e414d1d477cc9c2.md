
## The Ideal Linux Laptop

What makes the ideal Linux mobile computer? My definition is "The notebook that is right for your needs, at the right price, with compatible graphics and sound subsystems, and with the right mix of features." Does this mean that the ideal Linux laptop features an internal CD-ROM? Certainly not! Not everyone needs to access CDs while on the road (especially if you have a large internal drive and play .mp3 audio files). Does your Linux laptop need a floppy? Maybe, or maybe not. Floppy drives add weight, and if you need a backup medium, you might be able to use a flash memory card.

Certainly one constraint will be the keyboard. Ham-fisted keyboard pounders on the road for any length of time with a diminutive subnotebook will quickly lose their minds or resort to "hunt and peck" two-fingered typing. Another issue might be weight. Petite travelers, upon their first trip through the airport lugging a 10-lb. desktop replacement, will want to switch to a slimtop or ultralight (or build up those biceps).

Display quality and size can also affect the quality of life on the road. Dinky, poorly lit screens can destroy your eyesight, make the display unreadable in sunlight, or cramp your style during an X Window session. (Try running a terminal shell window using 5-point Agate type on a 640x480-pixel True Scan display to see what I mean.)

Some people like using an external mouse with a notebook. Some notebooks come with a touchpad, trackball, or eraser-type pointer (some come with a touchpad *and* eraser). Some of these pointers include one, two, or (even better) three buttons. If you have cold, clammy fingers or the body temperature of a reptile, a touchpad might not work for you. On the other hand, if you perspire a lot, you might want to think about a rugged notebook so that your palms' sweat glands won't short-circuit the keyboard.

Do you need to work away from an electrical outlet? Having a powerful laptop and working with a notebook sporting the power of Big Iron (mainframe computers) can be a wonderful thing. But if the battery drains in an hour and you're nowhere near an outlet (such as on a train), you're stuck with a useless paperweight. Battery time can be critical!

Choosing a notebook is about style as much as anything else. Selected manufacturers now market notebooks in different colors, a la Apple Computer's iBook (discussed in this book, and which runs Linux). Speaking of Apple, if you're a Macintosh fan, you can currently choose between an iBook or a G3 PowerBook—a much simpler choice than when you're faced with the mind-numbing array of Intel-based portables on the market.

## Main Considerations

Many components of Intel-based notebooks are compatible with Linux. For example, you shouldn’t have to worry about a notebook’s serial or parallel port compatibility. Keyboards and pointing devices such as erasers and touchpads generally do not pose a problem and are easily recognized by the modern Linux kernel. Some of the better notebooks, such as IBM ThinkPads, come with three mouse buttons—a definite bonus during X11 sessions.

For full functionality, you should pay particular attention to a notebook’s graphics chipset. Configuring a graphical interface for Linux, although not absolutely necessary (Linux works quite well in console [text-only] mode without the X Window System), can be the key to enjoying a wealth of additional applications, or *clients* (in X parlance). Unlike desktop computers, graphics subsystems cannot be replaced in a notebook, so your initial choice is critical!

To find out if the laptop you’re considering is supported by XFree86, the free graphical interface for Linux, browse to The XFree86 Project, Inc.’s Web pages at
http://www.xfree86.org/#currentre.

Follow the link to the current documentation, and then read the documentation for the graphics chipset used in the notebook you’re considering. However, if you don’t find support, you can also turn to a commercial X Window distribution that might fully support the notebook’s graphics card. Browse to XiGraphics’ Web page at
http://www.xig.com/Pages/hardwareSupportLib.html

You can then browse a list of 3-D, 2-D, and multiheaded display cards to see if the graphics chipset is supported. You’ll want to make sure that your notebook can work with X11 to support the widest range of *resolutions*, measured in horizontal and vertical pixels (such as 1024×768), and *color depth*, or number of colors. The number of colors could be 256 (8 bits per plane [bpp]), 15bpp or 16bpp for thousands of colors, or 24bpp or 32bpp for millions of colors.

If sound is important to you, again you’ll need to choose carefully. You won’t be able to change your notebook’s sound system. Careful research will tell you whether others have been successful in configuring sound on the notebook you’re considering. Linux includes support for many different sound cards. However, some newer graphics subsystems used in notebooks, such as NeoMagic’s 256AV, also include sound support, but they rely on proprietary drivers to play audio CDs and so on. If you’re unsure about your notebook, or if you have trouble configuring sound using the drivers included with your Linux distribution, check for alternative free sound drivers from the Advanced Linux Sound Architecture (ALSA) project, available at
http://www.alsa-project.org

You can also check to see if the commercial sound drivers from 4Front Technologies will work with the notebook's sound chipset. Browse to
http://www.openound.com/ossfw.html

If you enjoy listening to audio CDs, you'll definitely want a notebook with either an internal or external CD-ROM. You should know, however, that only internal CD-ROMs are directly connected to notebook sound systems. If you use an external CD-ROM (connected with a PC card interface), you'll need a patch cable to patch the sound output on the external CD to your notebook's line-in jack.

Do you want to watch a Digital Versatile Disc or Digital Video Disc (DVD) while using Linux? You can't. At the time this chapter was written, the Motion Picture Industry Association of America saw fit to support the arrest of Jon Johansen, a 16-year-old Norwegian boy, for cracking DVD's encryption and distributing the software on the Internet. However, you might want to consider a notebook with a DVD drive. The drive will work as a regular CD-ROM drive, Linux can read the DVD drive format, and some multiple-CD Linux distributions, such as SuSE Inc.'s six-CD SuSE Linux, are distributed on a single DVD disk.

The good news is that you may soon be able to watch DVD movies while using Linux. At least one company, InterVideo Inc., has announced plans to release a low-cost software DVD player for Linux sometime in late 2000.

Other features, such as IrDA ports, an internal microphone, the number and type of PCMCIA slots, audio or keyboard controls, Advanced Power Management schemes, and spindle design, might or might not be important.

Support for USB was still under development at the time this chapter was written, although Linux supports some keyboards and mice in the 2.3.X series of development kernels. Also, IrDA support for printing is in flux and might not be finalized until the release of stable 2.4 Linux kernels.

If the notebook you're considering comes with an internal modem, chances are good that it is a "Windows-only" modem. These flaccid, vestigial pieces of junk seem to have infected the majority of notebooks (and desktops) on the market as of late. One vendor, Lucent, has released a 500K+ Linux kernel module (named lucent.o) that might or might not allow limited use of the internal Lucent communications port (the module has been verified to work on a number of IBM ThinkPad models, such as the 390X, with a Linux kernel version 2.2.12 or later). You can get this driver at
http://www.linmodems.org

You'll find active discussions regarding this class of communication device (if you can call it that). Some Linux programmers feel that software-driven modems can be used for other tasks, such as voice dialing or call recording.

My opinion is that software-driven modems are a bad idea and bad engineering. This approach to providing communications ability for a notebook might save the manufacturer a few pennies, but it puts the consumer at the mercy of the operating system and the drivers' software vendor. For now, avoid any notebook with an internal modem, or order the notebook without one of these things if you can. Ironically, a very select few notebooks on the market use real modems (one being the Sony 505TX/TR series, now destined to be the "classic" Linux notebook).

In general, you'll have a better chance of success with Linux compatibility with a notebook if the computer is not the "latest and greatest" model. Older models (a year or more) have a much better chance of being supported. You should also peruse the comp.os.linux.portable Usenet newsgroup for answers to questions about compatibility.

Here are the features I look for when choosing a notebook for Linux:

- A readable screen display with a resolution of at least 1024x768. Although 800x600 is acceptable, some X11 clients require 1024x768.
- A graphics chipset supported by XFree86 that provides at least 16bpp.
- A compatible sound system with a chipset supported by the Linux kernel sound modules. Many notebooks have only one speaker, although newer models might have stereo. Nearly all sound tinny, but they should support a line-out or headphone jack.
- A hard drive with a minimum of 2GB of storage, and even more if you are doing development or want to install all the latest software and games.
- A pointing device with at least two mouse buttons (three is even better). Apple iBook users must use two function keys to simulate a middle and right button click.
- A minimum of 32MB of RAM (64MB or 128MB is even better).
- At a minimum, a PCMCIA port supporting at least one Type II card. If the notebook doesn't have an internal CD-ROM, you can at least install Linux over a network. Other alternatives include using the Parallel Line Interface Protocol (PILP).
