One interesting graphic monitor you can use if you don't use KDE for your X11 session is the asapm (AfterStep APM) monitor. You can dock this tiny client in your AfterStep icon wharf or start it from the command line of an X11 terminal window like this:

```
$ asapm &
```

After you press Enter, you'll see a tiny window, as shown in Figure 17.5.

By configuring asapm's resource file, .asapmrc in your home directory, you can have this small client launch the `apmsleep` command to put your notebook into suspend or standby mode when power reaches low levels. For example:

```
DownTo 10   /usr/bin/apmsleep -s
```

This entry causes asapm to execute the `apmsleep` command to suspend your notebook when only 10 percent power remains.

## Alternative Power Sources

If you're going on the road, you can take a DC power plug for your computer. These typically plug into a cigarette lighter in a car or on a boat. Other devices, such as AC/DC converters, may be used to provide AC power for your notebook's AC adapter and even recharge your laptop's battery.

For an incredible extended mobile session, consider using an external solar panel. A number of these panels, such as Powerline Solar's Sun Catcher, can power your laptop entirely from the sun, or at least (according to the manufacturer) triple your mobile session and battery life. These solar panels are about twice the cost of a replacement LiIon battery for most notebooks.

## Reading Books

There's nothing like curling up with a good book on the couch during a rainy afternoon or reading a few chapters at night before drifting off to sleep. If you're an avid reader, and you happen to possess one of the new breed of ultralight laptops, it's possible to swap a hard-bound book for a light notebook (and it might even be easier to read).

If you enjoy browsing a favorite bookstore or library for something to read, you can now browse the Internet and find plenty of classic and contemporary literature to read, download, install, and enjoy on your notebook.

### Online Resources

One of the best sites for full texts of books in the public domain is Project Gutenberg. This project was started in 1971 by Michael Hart at the University of Illinois, and it began with a copy of the Declaration of Independence. Since then, nearly 3,000 electronic texts (*etexts*) have been created by Project Gutenberg. The texts are plain ASCII text, which means that each book can be read by nearly every computer in existence.

Unfortunately, due to the shortsightedness of federal legislators (most of whom are in the pockets of mainstream media, the entertainment industry, and other corporate interests), copyright periods were extended from 14, and then to 28, and finally to 50 years (or more) past the life of the author. This disgusting trend reverses the original intent of copyright law, which was to ensure the sharing and availability of ideas and published works. (Very few people had access to printing presses or bookmaking technology when the laws were first enacted. Today, however, few people would readily give up the ability to share information.) These recent changes to copyright laws clearly do not favor the public interest. For a short discussion of this issue, read Richard Stallman's article, “Reevaluating Copyright: The Public Must Prevail,” available at http://www.gnu.org/philosophy/reevaluating-copyright.html

### Downloading Books

The main Gutenberg FTP site is ftp://metalab.unc.edu/pub/docs/books/gutenberg/. Browse to the site with your favorite Web browser, and then download a book to your directory. The books are stored as plain text or as a compressed .zip file. To decompress a .zip archive, use the Linux unzip command like this:

```
# unzip 12ad10.zip
```

This will decompress the archive and save H. G. Wells's *Twelve Stories and a Dream* in your directory. You can then use your favorite text editor, word processor, or pager (such as less) to read the book. You’ll find that you can store a complete home library of books on your notebook if you have enough drive space! If you use X11, you can use the xless browser to read, search, and print your books. Use the xless client like this:

```
# xless 12ad10.txt &
```

After you press Enter, you’ll see the dialog box shown in Figure 18.1. You can use it to read your book.

## Games

Many games are available for Linux. Whether you're after a challenging game of chess, or you want to vaporize hordes of mutants in the depths of some animated hell, you'll find a Linux program to satisfy your recreational needs. You'll find games that work with or without X11, games only for X11, and games that require specific graphics hardware. Some games work in less than an 80-character-by-25-line text display, and others work best at a minimum resolution of 1024x768 with thousands of colors.

This section lists several of the best Linux strategy and arcade games that work especially well on notebooks with an LCD resolution of 800x600.

### Strategy Games

If you do not use X11, try the BSD collection of games. Many Linux distributions offer this package of console-based fun. You'll find variants of board games, such as backgammon, along with old favorites, such as air traffic control and monopolyp. Each game in the BSD collection is designed to work in the text-based console. Each game is started from the command line.

One of the classic games is Adventure, in which you type instructions, directions, commands, and questions in order to explore a cave in search of treasure and trinkets. The game typically starts like this:

```
$ adventure
```
...
You are standing at the end of a road before a small brick building. Around you is a forest. A small stream flows out of the building and down a gully.
...
```

One of the most powerful and undefeatable games for the Linux console is gnuchess from the Free Software Foundation. To start a game, log in to Linux, type gnuchess, and press Enter. You'll first be asked if you want to specify a number of moves and a time limit on play. When the game starts, you'll see a board similar to Figure 18.2.

Enter moves according to piece location and desired square. For more information, read the gnuchess man page. Other variants included with gnuchess draw fancy graphics or can be used with a graphics client, such as xboard. The xboard client, shown in Figure 18.3, supports gnuchess during X11 sessions. If your notebook has a small display, be sure to use xboard, along with its -size small option, like this:

```
$ xboard -size small &
```
