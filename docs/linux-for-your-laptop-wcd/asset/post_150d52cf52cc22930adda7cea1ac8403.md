## Supported Hardware

As I mentioned, there are Linux distributions for a variety of computers and central processing units (CPUs). Although they are best known for their support of Intel-based computers, there are also Linux distributions from different vendors supporting the Digital (now Compaq) Alpha, Sun Microsystems’ SPARC, the ARM series, and Motorola’s Dragonball series of CPUs.

In general, if you have an Intel-based x86 computer or better with 16MB of RAM and 500MB of free hard drive space, you can run Linux.

### Matching Your Needs

Before you choose a notebook for Linux, you should understand the general category and variety of size and weight, or *form factors*, for mobile computers available on the market. Computer manufacturers are currently in stiff competition as notebook computers gain more and more market share. This is evident in computer store showrooms, industry trade shows, and computer hardware catalogs. Notebooks are increasingly capturing market share as consumers demand more power in a portable platform. Some industry pundits believe that notebooks will eventually replace desktop computers, as the distinction and lines blur between model generations.

The range and power of computers changes every 18 months or so, usually as the result of new manufacturing techniques. These advances include faster central processing units (CPUs), higher-density random access memory (RAM), improved monitor and Liquid Crystal Display (LCD) technology, larger-capacity hard drives, and—most important for mobile Linux users—increased miniaturization.

For example, from 1995 to 2000, standard CPU speeds on typical Intel-based computers increased more than nine-fold, from 75MHz Pentiums to the newer 700MHz Pentium IIIs. Nominal RAM installation went from 16MB to 128MB, and hard drive storage jumped from 1GB to a more-or-less standard 12 to 16GB. At least one notebook model on the market today boasts an internal hard drive capacity of 75GB!

Manufacturers have also introduced new connection hardware and protocols for computers. These improvements include Infrared Data Association (IrDA) ports, the consumer-oriented Universal Serial Bus (USB), the improved CardBus for Personal Computer Memory Card International Association (PCMCIA) Type II and less-common Type III PC cards, and the new ultra high-speed IEEE-1394 Firewire for digital data transmission. Many computers now feature graphics cards that have more memory than was routinely installed on computers only a few years ago. In some cases, it matches or exceeds the amount of installed RAM!

The point is that many of these new features and increased capabilities are now debuting on notebook computers! Whereas four years ago, mobile users had to make do with "catch-up" hardware, and notebook computing lagged behind the power of desktop computers, this game no longer applies. You can now get a laptop computer that easily matches or exceeds the power of a desktop computer.

### Consider Carefully

Sometimes you might have to make do with what you have, but if you intend to purchase a notebook for Linux, do some research! Even though many modern notebooks feature removable elements, such as a hard drive, memory, or CD-ROM drive, you're generally stuck with the installed CPU, graphics chipset, ports, and chassis features. Although there are ways to use a different pointing device or keyboard, when you're on the road you usually stick with the basic features of your notebook. You should also understand that this market is a moving target; it's always in flux and always changing. What was today's high-end notebook quickly becomes tomorrow's dinosaur hardware.

### Mobile Form Factors

Mobile computers were introduced right alongside the very first desktop personal computers. Veteran mobile users might remember the Osborne I with its 51-character display, the KayPro II with its dual floppy drives, or the NEC Startle, which all ran the CP/M operating system (the Startle used a read-only memory [ROM] operating system). The Radio Shack Tandy Model 100, with its embedded Beginners All-purpose Symbolic Instruction Code (BASIC), enjoyed large popularity. Following these first-generation models were early Disk Operating System (DOS)-based computers such as the lunch-pail Morrow Pivot and the luggage Compaqs.

Although many of these computers are still in use and continue to serve admirably today, none of them may be used with the modern Linux kernel, which requires a minimum Intel 386-based chipset. However, these early computers helped define the notebook market, and many form factors still survive.
