# Cleaning Your Screen

First, read your notebook's manual! Some manufacturers recommend that you use a mix of water and isopropyl alcohol, but many modern displays are protected with a clear plastic coating that might be damaged by improper cleaning. Do not use an anti-static spray or other monitor cleaner, and never use a paper towel!

Color LCD displays used with modern notebooks come in at least two types of form factors. Until recently, many less-expensive notebooks used a passive-matrix transistor-driven display providing up to 256 colors, sometimes called Dual-Scan Twisted Nematic (DSTN). These notebook displays are recognizable by slow screen draws and low contrast and are unusable in full sunlight. However, even with recent improvements to beef up performance and resolution to 800×600 pixels, these lower-cost displays are now relegated to sub-$1000 notebooks and might disappear from the market altogether.

Nearly all modern notebook displays use thin-film transistor (TFT) active-matrix displays that, at 800×600 pixels, pack nearly 1,440,000 transistors on the glass panel! This is the minimal desirable display to have when using Linux. Clarity is usually very good, and on many models the display rivals or exceeds traditional CRT monitors in clarity.

Most of today's notebooks rival desktop computers in power and display. This means that most premium laptops distributed by name-brand manufacturers feature at least 1024×768-pixel displays with at least thousands of colors.

# Dead Pixel(s)?

If you see a red dot on your screen when the display is black, or a blue, green, or black dot that never changes color, your display has a dead pixel with a bad transistor (which controls the pixel's color, changing it from red, blue, or green).

Although most notebook makers have strict quality assurance guidelines, the advent of 1024×768, 1280×1024, and even 1400×1050 notebook displays means that it is possible that a given screen might have 10 (or more) problem pixels. If having even one dead pixel is objectionable, check with the manufacturer before you buy! Most have guidelines regarding the number and placement of dead pixels before a replacement is warranted.
