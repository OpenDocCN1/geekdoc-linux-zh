T his chapter is about time: how Linux uses time, how you can use time, and how you can keep accurate track of time on your notebook. Time is a valuable commodity. We never seem to have enough of it, and if we lose track of it, we can get into trouble. Few books about Linux cover time issues in any detail. One of the reasons is that most users rarely have to think about or fiddle with time and time zone settings after installing Linux. Besides, most default settings work well enough.

However, there are some instances in which time and timing issues can be critical. For example, if you're on the road and have traveled thousands of miles from home, chances are good that your mobile Linux environment has the wrong time and perhaps even the wrong date. Documents you generate or e-mail messages you send can have incorrect date and time information. You can also miss appointments, scheduling, or travel arrangements.

Time is also important if your job requires you to keep close track of it. Work that is tracked in billable hours becomes very valuable (and expensive) for companies using technicians, computer consultants, or lawyers.

Network administrators know how important time is, especially when crafting network-wide backup scripts or schedules. It is critically important that locally connected computers are all set to the same date and time so that they will perform appropriately on command or in sequence during backup operations. Coordinating simultaneous processing of scripts or programs can be impossible if dates and times are not synchronized. Other issues include keeping the accurate date and time, especially on systems with password expirations.

Issues that affect mobile Linux users include APM, which might or might not offset, reset, disable, or otherwise screw up correct time settings for Linux or (even worse) the computer's hardware clock (which would be a fault of the BIOS, not Linux).

# Linux, Time, and Time Zones

There is a difference between the date and time used by Linux, known as the system time, and the date and time used by your notebook's real-time clock, or hardware clock. Many experienced mobile Linux users keep the notebook's clock set to Coordinated Universal Time, or UTC (or, in the military, ZULU). Then they set the Linux system time as an offset to UTC. This has the advantage of providing a base time value that you can easily adjust or update in the system when traveling.
