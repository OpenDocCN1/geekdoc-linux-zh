
## Chapter 1: Choosing a Notebook for Linux

Mobile computers come in many different sizes and formats, running a number of different operating systems. These form factors generally break down into several categories (they are listed here according to size):

- **Luggable.** A mobile chassis computer equal to a desktop, weighing up to 20 lbs. or more, usually offering a large (often tube glass) display, removable keyboard, and internal expansion slots. It generally is used for industrial applications or environments. It usually does not include a battery, so it needs to be plugged in. It’s capable of running Linux.

- **Desktop replacement.** A notebook with a 14-to-15-inch or greater display, weighing up to 10 lbs. or more. It usually features a larger keyboard and multiple slots or “bays” for extra batteries, hard drives, and floppy, Zip, LS-120, or CD-ROM drives. Its battery life is one to two hours or possibly more. This is a complete computer with most supported connectivity ports, such as IrDA, USB, serial, printer, video, monitor, keyboard, and mouse. It’s capable of running Linux.

- **Notebook.** A broad category of mainstream laptops, 1 or more inches thick, weighing from 4 to 7 lbs., usually with a 12-to-14-inch display. It might have a three-spindle design (hard drive, floppy drive, and CD-ROM drive). It generally has built-in IrDA, serial, printer, monitor, USB, and single PS/2 keyboard or mouse ports. It’s capable of running Linux.

- **Slimtop.** Generally weighs from 3 to 6 lbs. and might be 1 inch thick. It generally has a two-spindle design with an internal hard drive and CD-ROM. It might use an external floppy or require a port replicator or docking station to provide serial, printing, or video ports. It might offer IrDA and one or more Type II PCMCIA slots. It also has a tinny sound system. It’s capable of running Linux.

- **Ultright, or subnotebook.** Weighs 3 lbs. or less and can be less than 1 inch thick. These extremely slim computers generally have a smaller display—12 inches or less. Most, if not all, lack an internal CD-ROM drive and have a one-spindle design with an internal hard drive and external floppy. This model has a tinny sound system. It might have a built-in IrDA port and Type II PCMCIA slot. It generally requires a port replicator or docking station. Some (such as the Sony Vaio) use magnesium casing instead of plastic for rigidity. It’s capable of running Linux.

- **Mini-notebook.** Handheld, lightweight computer with a diminutive or half-size display. It has tinny sound (if any). It has a cramped mini-keyboard or keypad. It might have a built-in hard drive. Some units that run Windows CE, known as Pocket PC, are available. This model might have one or more connectivity ports, such as a serial or Type II PCMCIA slot. It might be pen-based. It might or might not be capable of running Linux. (Some of the better-known minis are the Toshiba Libretos, which run Linux quite well.)

- **Handheld, also known as a Personal Digital Assistant (PDA).** Features shirt-pocket convenience. As long as the unit isn't running Pocket PC, its battery life can be measured in weeks or months. It usually features a single connectivity or IrDA port, it's usually pen-based, and it generally weighs several ounces. Most, if not all, will not run Linux. (However, the TRG Pro Palm PDA is reported to boot and run Linux, and progress is being made on porting Linux to several PDAs, such as the Casio E-100 and E-105.)

As you can see from this list, size, weight, and expandability can be important. Other issues can also factor into your choice of platform for Linux on a notebook:

- Processor type and speed. Notebooks with Pentium III processors are now quite common. Advances from companies such as Transmeta, due to introduce extremely low-power “intelligent” CPUs for the mobile market, might provide features you've been waiting for in a laptop. Generally, the faster the CPU, the more heat it generates, requiring a possibly noisy fan for cooling. Faster CPUs generally also require more power, increasing battery drain and decreasing mobility.

- Graphics. For a long time, a Super Video Graphics Array (SVGA) display of 640×480 pixels, then 800×600 pixels, was considered standard. Today, however, many lighter-weight notebooks feature Extended Graphics Array (XGA) displays of 1024×768 pixels with color depths of thousands of colors. The IBM 770Z was the first to introduce a 1280×1024 display. The industry leader is now the Dell Inspiron SVGA+ display, providing a whopping 1400×1050 pixels!

- Expansion. After you buy a laptop, you're generally stuck with its features. Some notebooks allow you to remove or add internal components. Many offer port replicators or docking stations to add additional connectivity, sound, or networking. Adding more hard drives, CD-ROM drives, or other storage usually involves an external device that might or might not require its own power.

In most cases, by choosing a lighter and more compact device, you sacrifice features. You must decide which model and type of mobile platform provides the best price, weight, and features. One of the most often-asked questions on Internet Linux discussion groups is "What is the best laptop for Linux?"

This is a good question, and it’s much better to ask it before you buy a laptop for Linux! But it’s not easy to sort out the responses. The best notebook for one person might not be the best notebook for another person. Some notebook owners are unwilling to purchase a laptop with a single dead LCD pixel, while others won’t even notice. Some users buy a notebook with no intention of going on the road, while others need a computer that can be thrown in the back of the car at a moment’s notice.

Also remember that notebooks are quite different from desktop computers. You will need to do some research (and I’ll offer you some tips) on picking a notebook with the most compatible hardware.

### Who Makes Notebooks?

Although many name-brand computer manufacturers, such as IBM, Compaq, and Dell, market notebook computers, you might be surprised to learn that many branded laptops are actually made under contract by other companies! This is actually good news, because it means that there might be other models quite similar to the one you purchase that can share upgrades, add-ons, or additional peripherals. Notebooks are notorious for requiring specifically manufactured hardware such as batteries or RAM, or replacement items such as floppy drives or keyboards.

According to International Data Corp., in 1998 the majority of laptops sold in the U.S. were made by Toshiba, Compaq, Dell, IBM, and Gateway. The rest of the market was split among other vendors.

In some cases, you might be able to save quite a bit of money by buying a similar off-brand notebook. The trade-off may be in service, warranty, or convenience, such as being able to buy only through mail order, or only receiving technical support from a Web page. You’ll have to make that choice.
