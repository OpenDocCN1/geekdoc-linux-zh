You can also use the software and procedures outlined in Chapter 7, "Advanced Power Management," when you're on the road.

# # Docking Stations

Although this book focuses on mobile Linux computing, most notebook owners know that some laptops are made to fit into a compatible docking station. These stations are designed to easily accommodate laptop insertion and removal, and they offer the convenience of turning your mobile machine into a desktop unit.

External devices, such as a keyboard, printer, monitor, modem, mouse, audio system, or network connection, usually remain attached to the docking unit. The laptop is then just inserted into the dock before you go to work. Some docking stations also offer an additional hard drive or other form of storage.

Most likely, however, this convenience comes at a price. Stations are designed to specifically match a particular notebook model. There's no such thing as a standard docking connector in the notebook industry!

However, at least two companies build and sell what are touted as "Universal Port Replicators." These devices, which offer serial, parallel, keyboard, network, mouse, and video connections, connect through your notebook's PCMCIA port but are not compatible with Linux.
