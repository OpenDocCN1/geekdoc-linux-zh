
There might be a market for a book that details the exact instructions for bypassing the BIOS for a variety of notebooks, but you can bet many of the readers would be either independent repair technicians or disreputable characters. Nevertheless, you can probably find the information you need somewhere on the Internet. (Requests for this kind of information on Usenet in newsgroups such as comp.sys.laptops are generally met with much skepticism.)

In the case of the IBM 390 and 1700 series ThinkPads, there is no way to override the hard drive password or BIOS setup password—the hardware must be replaced.

## Stolen Notebooks

There is a lucrative market in stolen computer equipment. You can probably find many of these computers and notebooks for sale in your city. If you’re in the market for a used notebook, and you find a deal that seems too good to be true, consider this: How would you feel if your notebook were stolen? Don’t buy a “hot” notebook, and avoid potential trouble. At the least, you could lose your money, and at the worst, you could be arrested.

To avoid buying a stolen notebook, be sure to see the original receipt, and make sure the serial number matches the one on the receipt. You can also check various databases of stolen laptops. Browse to

http://home.snafu.de/wehe/stolen_laptops.html

You’ll find links to a number of corporate and commercial databases. To protect your notebook before it is stolen, you can also register with a commercial tracking service. These services, which range in cost from a one-time fee to monthly or annual charges, generally work by providing a special tag and including your notebook’s information (such as make, model, and serial number) in a database. The unremovable tag will have an 800 number, along with a serial number. The service acts as an intermediary in getting your system back to you.

The bad news is that most stolen computer equipment is never recovered. But you can protect yourself. Until laptops come equipped with fingerprint recognition, you can take some basic steps:

- Never leave your laptop unattended.
- If you’re on the road, use the in-room or hotel safe.
- Never check your laptop through the airport baggage check.
- Some thefts occur at the airport’s x-ray machine. Someone blocks you after you put your notebook on the conveyor belt for scanning, and then someone else takes your notebook after it comes out the other end.
- Use a nondescript case or bag for your laptop.
- Use a motion detector for your notebook case on the off chance you need to leave it unattended.
- Use a security cable with your notebook. Nearly every notebook made today includes a standard notebook lock port.

Want to protect your notebook and, if it is stolen, perhaps track the thief? Browse to http://savelaptop.zzzweb.com/, and register your laptop with Moonicer Salem's server. You can then download and install his laptop-tracking software for Linux. The tracker command, according to Salem, "assists another user or yourself in finding your laptop by sending vital selection information to our servers each time your laptop is connected to the Internet."

The tracker command is a small 40,000-character binary file. It uses a simple command line consisting of your username and password at Salem's server, your laptop's bar code, and a designated network interface to watch, such as eth0 or ppp0. This command should be renamed and then run at startup from an initialization script. Should someone steal your Linux laptop and then get on the Internet, you might be able to track the logon's point of origin. Versions of the software are also available for other operating systems.

## Backup Strategies

Many mobile Linux users configure their notebooks as dual-boot systems (hopefully to boot Linux or BSD UNIX). If your laptop has enough hard drive storage on the Windows or Linux partition, you can use this storage to make a complete backup of an entire filesystem. For example, when you first turn on and initialize your notebook, you might want to keep the software configuration in as close to factory "out-of-the-box" condition as possible. This can aid in restoration or help you if you intend to sell the notebook later.

### Filesystem Backup

You can easily make a backup of your entire Windows partition using Linux and a DOS-compatible compression program and then store the backup on another computer or even on unused space under Linux. For example, if you mount your Windows VFAT partition under /mnt/dos, you can use the zip command to back up Windows like this:

```sh
# cd /mnt/dos
# zip /win.zip *
```

The zip command will then create a .zip file under the root directory (/) with the name win.zip. If you ever need to restore your Windows partition, all you need to do is create a bootable DOS partition and then restore the file. You can do this from Linux by mounting the partition and then using the unzip command:

```sh
# mount -t vfat /dev/hda1 /mnt/dos
# cd /mnt/dos
# unzip /pathtomotozipfile/win.zip
```

In this example, /dev/hda1 is the DOS partition, and the win.zip file resides somewhere on your Linux filesystem. If you have a network, you can also use strategies such as using an NFS-mounted remote partition to hold your filesystem backups and then restore over a network.

You can also use this approach to make a copy of your entire Linux filesystem (although this really isn’t necessary). For example, if you have enough room on your hard drive or another partition, you can back up everything with this:

```sh
# tar cvsf /linux.tgz /
```

Be Careful!

If you want to make a copy of your entire filesystem, be sure to unmount any unwanted partitions or other filesystems, such as /mnt/dos, or you’ll back up those files, too!

Creating a single compressed file of an entire filesystem is known as archiving. You could consider a Linux distribution on CD-ROM as a permanent archive or snapshot of a filesystem. There are other approaches to backing up data that you can use with great success to reduce the amount of time, effort, and file sizes of the backup.

### Incremental Backups

The best way to back up your system is to devise a backup strategy, or schedule. You’ll have to decide whether it is worth the effort to perform backups on a daily, weekly, or monthly basis. For example, you could devise a strategy like this:

- First of the month: Full backup
- First day of second week: Backup of changed files
- First day of third week: Backup of changed files since first day of second week
- First day of fourth week: Backup of changed files since first day of third week

This approach has an advantage over backing up only once every month in that you would be able to restore a week’s worth of files and easily backtrack to your full backup. The disadvantage over a daily backup schedule is that you could lose six days of work. A daily, incremental backup schedule would look like this:

- Sunday: Full backup
- Monday: Backup of all changed files since full backup
- Tuesday: Backup of all changed files since Monday
- Wednesday: Backup of all changed files since Tuesday
- Thursday: Backup of all changed files since Wednesday
