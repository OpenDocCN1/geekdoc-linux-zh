As you can see from this list, size, weight, and expandability can be important. Other issues can also factor into your choice of platform for Linux on a notebook:

+   * Processor type and speed. Notebooks with Pentium III processors are now quite common. Advances from companies such as Transmeta, due to intro- duce extremely low-power “intelligent” CPUs for the mobile market, might provide features you've been waiting for in a laptop. Generally, the faster the CPU, the more heat it generates, requiring a possibly noisy fan for cooling. Faster CPUs generally also require more power, increasing battery drain and decreasing mobility.

+   * Graphics. For a long time, a Super Video Graphics Array (SVGA) display of 640×480 pixels, then 800×600 pixels, was considered standard. Today, however, many lighter-weight notebooks feature Extended Graphics Array (XGA) displays of 1024×768 pixels with color depths of thousands of col- ors. The IBM 770Z was the first to introduce a 1280×1024 display. The industry leader is now the Dell Inspiron SVGA+ display, providing a whopping 1400×1050 pixels!

+   * Expansion. After you buy a laptop, you're generally stuck with its features. Some notebooks allow you to remove or add internal components. Many offer port replicators or docking stations to add additional connectivity, sound, or networking. Adding more hard drives, CD-ROM drives, or other storage usually involves an external device that might or might not require its own power.
