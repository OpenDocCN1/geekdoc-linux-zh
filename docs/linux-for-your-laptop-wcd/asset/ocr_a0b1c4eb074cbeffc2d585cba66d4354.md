One potential drawback is that these can get dirty very quickly, wear out, or, if improperly installed, damage the notebook screen if the lid is closed. Manufacturer replacements, such as for the IBM ThinkPad series of notebooks, can cost $4 each!

+   * **Trackball.** An earlier notebook pointing device, this pointer relies on a rolling ball resting on internal mechanical wheels to move the cursor. Prone to dust, dirt, and other foreign materials, the trackball is no longer in common use (at least on notebooks). Abuse and subsequent failure of this component on a notebook will render the built-in pointer useless.

+   * **Touchpad.** Initially introduced to the consumer market by Apple Computer, Inc. on an early line of PowerBook notebooks, the touchpad spawned a variety of pointers from different manufacturers. Using similar electromagnetic principles as early drawing tablets, these pointers use your finger as a stylus to move the cursor. Modern variants support finger taps and scrolling. At least one line of notebooks, the Sony Vaios, includes a stylus with a built-in touchpad variant, the VersaPad. This allows you to enter signatures and even draw on the pad like a tablet. A similar device is the Cirque GlidePoint, licensed to Alps Electric Company. The touchpad type of pointing device has the advantage of being practically indestructible; barring failure of internal circuitry, a touchpad should last for the life of the notebook.

Other pointer variants include those such as the rocker pad, found on Toshiba's now-defunct line of Libretto subnotebooks. Pointer motion is achieved by pressing the small (half-inch) rubber pad in different directions. Infrared (IrDA) pointers have been around for several years and are growing in popularity.

# Cursor Drifting?

If you have an IBM ThinkPad and you notice your cursor occasionally drifting a quarter of an inch across the screen, don't worry! Leave it alone, and it will stop. The cursor is recalibrating!
