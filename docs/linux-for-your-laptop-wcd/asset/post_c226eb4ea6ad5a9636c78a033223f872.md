
before Linux writes data to disk. For example, you could change the /etc/inittab entry for update to this:

```
id:once:/sbin/update -S 1800 -f 1800
```

This will cause updates only once every half hour. Be warned, however: If you lose power and your disk is not updated, you could lose data!

This is the best option to use before making any changes to your drive settings. Be careful! Other than changing the standby and a few other settings, you can cause your system to become unbootable by using incorrect settings!

You can set your hard drive to immediately go to standby mode by using the `-y` option like this:

```
# hdparm -y /dev/hda
```

```
/dev/hda:
	drive state is:	standby
```

To check your hard drive's power consumption mode, use the `-C` flag:

```
# hdparm -C /dev/hda
```

```
/dev/hda:
	drive state is:	standby
```

You can put your hard drive to sleep (to use no power) with the `-Y` option:

```
# hdparm -Y /dev/hda
```

```
/dev/hda:
	issuing sleep command
```

Use the `-s` option, followed by a number from 1 to 240, to set the timeout before your hard drive goes to standby mode. The numbers are multiplied by 5, so using a value of 6 would cause your hard drive to spin down after 30 seconds of inactivity:

```
# hdparm -S 6 /dev/hda
```

```
/dev/hda:
	setting standby to 6 (30 seconds)
```

By using a combination of hard drive inactivity and software inactivity (see the next section), you can craft a safe and very power-efficient mobile Linux system.

## Maintaining Your Battery

Proper maintenance of your notebook's battery is essential if you want to get the most life out of it and avoid early replacement. Of course, you should never let your battery get wet or try to take it apart. Batteries that are no longer any good should be disposed of properly. Many of the general guidelines for batteries, aside from recharging, also apply to your notebook. Here's a list of do's and don'ts for battery treatment:

-   Do charge your battery in an environment between 60 and 80 degrees Fahrenheit.
-   Do drain and store your NiMH battery if you aren't going to use it for several weeks.
-   Do drain your LiIon battery to 50 percent and store it if you aren't going to use it for more than several weeks.
-   Do expect shorter battery life if the battery is cold. (Always allow a computer to come up to room temperature if it has been stored in a cold environment.)
-   Do fully charge any new battery and then discharge it. Repeat this three times.
-   Don't bang or drop your notebook's battery.
-   Don't expose your battery to heat by leaving your notebook and battery in the sun or inside a parked car during the summer.
-   Don't forget to condition your battery every 60 days by fully draining and recharging it.
-   Don't store your notebook with an inserted battery for long periods of time.
-   Do recharge your battery completely. (LiIon batteries are not as prone to “memory” problems as NiCads.)
-   Do recharge your battery if it has not been used for a long time.
-   Do replace your battery if it does not hold a charge.
-   Do understand that a battery will heat up if it is discharging or in use.
-   Do realize that battery life during usage depends on the number of peripherals in use (such as hard drive, audio, CD-ROM, modem, or networking port).
-   Do use Advanced Power Management when using Linux on your notebook.
-   Do use your battery until it is depleted, and then recharge it. To avoid corrupting your Linux filesystem when draining your battery, use a DOS boot disk, or boot a Linux distribution on a floppy. (Use a single-floppy Linux distribution, such as HAL9; see Appendix A, “Linux Laptop Resources,” for details.)

## Getting Power Information

APM information is stored in the file `apm` under the `/proc` directory. This file is continually updated and can be used to provide a running commentary on the power state of your notebook. For example, you can get a quick snapshot by using the `cat` command:

```
$ cat /proc/apm
1.9 1.2 0x03 0x01 0x00 0x01 100% 200 min
```

Many different Linux commands and clients use this information to graphically display the state of your notebook's power or battery. See Chapter 7, “Advanced Power Management,” for more details about using APM and the structure of the data returned from the process directory.

The important task to keep in mind when configuring your notebook for the road is to enable APM for Linux by making sure the system starts the `apmd` daemon when you boot. You should then be familiar with the `apmsleep` command or other graphic APM utilities if you want to put your notebook into standby, sleep, or hibernation mode.
