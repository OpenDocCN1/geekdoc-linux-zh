### CPU Bugs

On the other hand, other CPUs such as the early Cyrix 6x86 failed to work with Linux unless APM was disabled in the computer's BIOS. Many Pentiums, including the MMX series, still suffer the Pentium “f00f” bug, which can crash a system through an invalid instruction. Another bug, the “f0” invalid instruction, affects early Pentium and MMX Pentium CPUs. Some versions of the Pentium II and Pentium Pro series also suffer a potentially catastrophic floating-point problem. And many Intel-based computer users might remember the now-classic early Pentium floating-point bug from 1993.

Fortunately, all modern Linux kernels contain code to handle these problems, so you should not have to worry about the stability of your system. You can look for some of this output when Linux boots by *piping* (with the bar operator) the output of the `dmesg` command through the `fgrep` command:

```
# dmesg | fgrep bug
Intel Pentium with F0 0F bug - workaround enabled
```

This shows (from a boot on a Pentium 300MHz MMX laptop) that Linux determined a CPU bug type and enabled a fix.

### How Does Linux Handle CPU Bugs?

One of the benefits of using Linux is that you get the source code to the kernel of the operating system. One of the places to see specifically how Linux attempts to detect and protect your system against CPU instruction set errors is the file `traps.c` under the `/usr/src/linux/arch/i386/kernel` directory. Note that this applies only to Intel-based computers!

Linux also runs quite handily on older 486-based notebooks. However, with prices of used 75 to 150MHz Pentium laptops in the $200 to $400 range, you’ll get much better performance with a faster CPU.

You can also use Linux to determine the exact type of CPU in your notebook. Again, use the `dmesg` command, but pipe the output through the `less` text reader (known as a *pager*):

```
# dmesg | less

Detected 501145385 Hz processor.

CPU: Intel Pentium III (Coppermine) stepping 01
```

This shows the output when booting Linux on a Pentium III 500MHz notebook. If you use the K Desktop Environment (KDE) for your X11 sessions, use the KDE Control Center client Information menu’s Processor item, as shown in Figure 2.1, to display details about your notebook’s CPU.

### Your Notebook's Display

Although your notebook's CPU is an important internal component, your laptop's display can be a critical factor in your productivity. Early luggable computers used traditional *cathode ray tubes* (CRTs) to provide a display. However, the included wiring and glass added many pounds. Early notebooks, such as the Tandy Model 100, used a simple *Liquid Crystal Diode* (LCD) display to draw characters and graphics on the screen. Major advancements in display technology have been made since then, but like the CPU, the display is one of the most power-consuming components of a notebook.

The next generation of notebooks used LCD displays, with improvements slowly being made to improve density, clarity, and appearance. Because LCDs do not generate light, early improved notebooks used some form of backlighting to improve readability. Further improvements came along with Twisted Nematic or Super Twisted Nematic (SuperTwist) displays, albeit in monochrome. The next leap forward for the notebook industry was providing color LCD display. Early models were quite expensive, but as manufacturing techniques improved, along with increased volume sales and industry demand, in the last eight years, color has become a standard feature of any desirable notebook.

### Cleaning Your Screen

First, read your notebook's manual! Some manufacturers recommend that you use a mix of water and isopropyl alcohol, but many modern displays are protected with a clear plastic coating that might be damaged by improper cleaning. Do not use an anti-static spray or other monitor cleaner, and never use a paper towel!

Color LCD displays used with modern notebooks come in at least two types of form factors. Until recently, many less-expensive notebooks used a passive-matrix transistor-driven display providing up to 256 colors, sometimes called Dual-Scan Twisted Nematic (DSTN). These notebook displays are recognizable by slow screen draws and low contrast and are unusable in full sunlight. However, even with recent improvements to beef up performance and resolution to 800×600 pixels, these lower-cost displays are now relegated to sub-$1000 notebooks and might disappear from the market altogether.

Nearly all modern notebook displays use thin-film transistor (TFT) active-matrix displays that, at 800×600 pixels, pack nearly 1,440,000 transistors on the glass panel! This is the minimal desirable display to have when using Linux. Clarity is usually very good, and on many models the display rivals or exceeds traditional CRT monitors in clarity.

Most of today's notebooks rival desktop computers in power and display. This means that most premium laptops distributed by name-brand manufacturers feature at least 1024×768-pixel displays with at least thousands of colors.

### Dead Pixel(s)?

If you see a red dot on your screen when the display is black, or a blue, green, or black dot that never changes color, your display has a dead pixel with a bad transistor (which controls the pixel's color, changing it from red, blue, or green).

Although most notebook makers have strict quality assurance guidelines, the advent of 1024×768, 1280×1024, and even 1400×1050 notebook displays means that it is possible that a given screen might have 10 (or more) problem pixels. If having even one dead pixel is objectionable, check with the manufacturer before you buy! Most have guidelines regarding the number and placement of dead pixels before a replacement is warranted.

### Display Chipsets

The sad truth at this point, even with the increasing popularity of Linux, is that most Intel-based notebook manufacturers still cater to Microsoft's software monopoly and build notebooks to run only one (and, in rare cases, two) variant(s) of Microsoft's operating systems: Windows 98 and Windows NT. At the time this chapter was written, the industry was poised for the deployment of Windows 2000, and several notebook manufacturers have certified laptops for this operating system. The fact that most, if not all, of these laptops are supported by Linux (at least in console, or text-only, mode) is an amazing testament to the determination and process of Open Source programmers.

Another negative factor is that makers of the graphics chipsets that drive modern notebooks are loath to release specifications regarding how the displays are driven. This poses severe constraints on Open Source programmers struggling to craft display driver support for new notebooks. Most, if not all, of these manufacturers are concerned only with supporting the Original Equipment Manufacturer of notebook PCs and not the end user.

With the increased power, speed, and display resolution of modern notebooks, these chipset manufacturers are in stiff competition to provide hosts of additional features, such as ever-faster three-dimensional image rendering, graphics acceleration, and incorporation of other notebook subsystems, such as audio. Chances are good that if you buy a recently introduced laptop with a new graphics chipset, you will run into display compatibility problems.

The graphical interface for Linux is the X Window System. Most Linux distributions come with the XFree86 X11 distribution from The XFree86 Project, Inc. Although XFree86 is free, you can also purchase a commercial X11 distribution from other companies, such as Xi-Graphics or MetroLink, Inc. You'll improve your chances of compatibility if you research support for your notebook's display before purchase. However, most Linux notebook users first try XFree86 to see if it works.

### The iBook and X11

Apple iBook users need to use a special Linux kernel (at least version 2.2.14 or greater) with frame-buffer support, along with a special X11 server application named XF86_FBDev. The maximum resolution is 800×600 pixels with a color depth of at least 16bpp.
