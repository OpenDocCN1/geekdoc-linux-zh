
This is an easy way to enable roaming access (within the limits of the wireless cards) while using a laptop. Other commercial cards might have additional distance capabilities, but by using this approach, you can build a small wireless network for less than $170 (U.S.).

## Wireless Modems

If you need to connect to your ISP while on the road, you can also try using Metricom’s Ricochet modem. Although it currently supports only wireless connection speeds of 28.8Kbps, the service is slated for an upgrade to 128Kbps in late 2000. The Ricochet modem is about the size of two decks of playing cards and attaches to your notebook’s serial port. The device weighs about 14 ounces and uses a NiMh rechargeable battery for about six hours of connect time.

Mobile Linux users report great success using the Ricochet device, but service areas are limited to several major U.S. cities, airports, colleges, and corporate campuses. You’ll also need to sign up for service with Metricom, because the Ricochet is designed to connect only with Metricom’s network and servers. The service provides dynamic IP addressing, e-mail, and news feeds. Dial-in access via land-line to Metricom’s servers is provided as an option.

## Wireless Cellular Connections

If you need wireless access to the Internet while on the road and you can’t find a phone jack, you can use one of several cellular-based systems and cell phones to dial your ISP. The type of technology, service, and cell phone you use might be dictated by your local phone service provider, service area (geographic) coverage, features, and price. Basically, four types of services are generally available to the casual mobile user, and each has advantages and disadvantages.

### CDMA

Code Division Multiple Access (CDMA) is another cellular technology that combines spread-spectrum frequency management with small cell radius to provide data transmission. Slower than TDMA or GSM (discussed in a moment), this digital service has an advantage in that you can use your phone as a modem and use a connector that plugs directly into your notebook’s serial port. Although there’s no actual modem inside the phone, dialing from your computer dials the phone hands-free, and a modem on the connecting network does the connection and transmission for you, usually at 19.2Kbps.

### IDEN

Integrated Digital Enhanced Networks (IDEN) combines digital cell phone, radio, pager, and modem technology. This service uses phones that include built-in modems, and it can be used directly with your laptop.

### GSM

Originally named in 1982 for Groupe Speciale Mobile, GSM now stands for Global System for Mobile Communications. It also allows global roaming. This service allows the use of a GSM phone directly with your notebook’s serial port or through a PCMCIA cellular modem.

### TDMA

Time Division Multiplexing (TDMA) uses different time slots for transmission of multiple data streams. You can generally get higher transmission speeds using this type of analog service, but most phones require an external PCMCIA modem card with a cellular connector and cord.

For example, using Spring PCS service (CDMA-based) and a Motorola StarTAC and connector cable, you can set up kppp to dial into your ISP at 19.2kbps. However, when configuring modem speeds for cellular connections made directly through your notebook's serial port, you must use the setserial command to ensure that strict transmission rates are used, or the call might not go through.

To do this, use the setserial command’s spd_normal option, along with the device name of the serial port you will use. Connect your phone and laptop, turn on your phone, and use setserial like this:

```bash
# setserial /dev/ttyS0 spd_normal
```

In this example, the phone is attached to the notebook’s COM1 port. You would then configure kppp (perhaps creating a "roaming" account!) to use /dev/ttyS0 and the proper connection speed.

### Net Connection Strategies

If you’re on the road or away from the LAN and you need to get connected, first check to see if your ISP has an 800 number. If it doesn’t, check your ISP’s coverage area and get a list of local phone numbers to match your itinerary before you go on the road. There’s nothing like wasting time trying to find a local access number when you can’t get on the Internet!

If you need access to your LAN, and you don't mind paying for long distance (or if your cellular service provider has free long distance), you can configure your Linux system for dial-up access.

## Setting up for Dialing In

Setting up a Linux system for dial-in capabilities is very easy. You need a computer with a modem and a phone line to answer the phone. If you have two phone lines attached to the computer, after you log in you can dial out and connect to your ISP to send and receive software.

Edit your system's initialization table, /etc/inittab, and look for these lines:

```bash
# Run getty in standard runlevels
1:2345:respawn:/sbin/mingetty tty1
2:2345:respawn:/sbin/mingetty tty2
3:2345:respawn:/sbin/mingetty tty3
4:2345:respawn:/sbin/mingetty tty4
5:2345:respawn:/sbin/mingetty tty5
6:2345:respawn:/sbin/mingetty tty6
```

Each line represents a different Linux runlevel. When you use Linux without X11, you run Linux at runlevel 3. When you use X11 with Red Hat or Mandrake Linux (note that other distributions might use different runlevels), you use runlevel 5. One of the first lines in your /etc/inittab file shows the default runlevel; it might look something like this:

```bash
id:5:initdefault:
```

To have Linux answer the phone and allow you to log in, you need to use the `agetty` command instead of the `/sbin/mingetty` command to monitor runlevel 3. You also need to specify the serial port used by your modem. Change this line:

```bash
3:2345:respawn:/sbin/mingetty tty3
```

to this:

```bash
3:2345:respawn:/sbin/agetty -w -I "ATE0Q1&D2&C1S0=1\015" 115200 ttyS0 vt100
```

The `agetty` command automatically initializes your modem to answer the phone, using your modem's S0 register (set to answer after the first ring in this example). Note that the modem is attached to the computer's COM1 port.

### Need to Find agetty?

If agetty is not included with your Linux distribution, you can quickly find a copy by browsing to

```
ftp://metalab.unc.edu/pub/Linux/system/serial/getty/
```

Alan Wendt’s agetty command is very simple but works well. If you don’t want to use agetty, you can also use mgetty or ugetty. Look under /usr/doc/mgetty+sendfax for details about setting up for dial-in. If you need additional information, you can also check the Serial-HOWTO, usually found under the /usr/doc/HOWTO directory.

Save your changes, and then restart your system using the shutdown command and its -r (reboot) option, like this:

```bash
# shutdown -r now
```

Linux will start the agetty command after booting, and agetty will monitor your /dev/ttyS0 serial port for incoming calls. When you dial in, agetty will pick up the phone, and after you (perhaps) press Enter, you’ll see a login: prompt. Be sure to test your setup before you hit the road!

### Additional Service

By creating a special user account for PPP or by editing a getty entry in /etc/inittab, you can also offer PPP over a dial-up connection. This has the benefit of allowing you to use FTP directly over the phone, or to transmit e-mail directly back to your laptop. First, enable dial-in service, and then create a ppp user. However, you’ll need to change the default /etc/passwd entry from this:

```
ppp:x:501:501::/home/ppp:/bin/bash
```

to this:

```
ppp:x:501:501::/home/ppp:/usr/sbin/pppd
```

You should then create and edit a file named .ppprc in the /home/ppp directory to contain this:

```
detach
modem
crtcts
lock
192.168.2.50
```

Save the file and restart your computer. When you log in, the pppd server will assign the IP address 192.168.2.50 to your notebook (make sure your server has a different IP address!). You can then retrieve e-mail, use FTP, or, if enabled, telnet into the server to get to your home directory.

## Free Services

Another approach to staying connected while on the road is to sign up for a free Internet mail account using a Web browser, such as at http://www.yahoo.com. You can then use any open terminal, such as at a public library or cyber-cafe, to send and receive mail through your Web-based e-mail account.

If you have access to a phone and are traveling in or around most major cities in the U.S., you can sign up for free Linux-oriented dial-up PPP Internet service at http://www.teledyn.com/products/PreseMD/

You’ll need to register, but after that you’ll get a local access number. (If you need other numbers, write them down during registration.) This is a full, nationwide ISP service!
