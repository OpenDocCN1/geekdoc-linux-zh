# Main Considerations

Many components of Intel-based notebooks are compatible with Linux. For example, you shouldn’t have to worry about a notebook’s serial or parallel port compatibility. Keyboards and pointing devices such as crasers and touchpads generally do not pose a problem and are easily recognized by the modern Linux kernel. Some of the better notebooks, such as IBM ThinkPads, come with three mouse buttons—a definite bonus during X11 sessions.

For full functionality, you should pay particular attention to a notebook’s graphics chipset. Configuring a graphical interface for Linux, although not absolutely necessary (Linux works quite well in console [text-only] mode without the X Window System), can be the key to enjoying a wealth of additional applications, or *clients* (in X parlance). Unlike desktop computers, graphics subsystems cannot be replaced in a notebook, so your initial choice is critical!

To find out if the laptop you’re considering is supported by XFree86, the free graphical interface for Linux, browse to The XFree86 Project, Inc.’s Web pages at
http://www.xfree86.org/#currentre.

Follow the link to the current documentation, and then read the documentation for the graphics chipset used in the notebook you’re considering. However, if you don’t find support, you can also turn to a commercial X Window distribution that might fully support the notebook’s graphics card. Browse to XiGraphics’ Web page at
http://www.xig.com/Pages/hardwareSupportLib.html

You can then browse a list of 3-D, 2-D, and multihedead display cards to see if the graphics chipset is supported. You’ll want to make sure that your notebook can work with X11 to support the widest range of *resolutions*, measured in horizontal and vertical pixels (such as 1024\u00d7768), and *color depth*, or number of colors. The number of colors could be 256 (8 bits per plane [bpp]), 15bpp or 16bpp for thousands of colors, or 24bpp or 32bpp for millions of colors.

If sound is important to you, again you’ll need to choose carefully. You won’t be able to change your notebook’s sound system. Careful research will tell you whether others have been successful in configuring sound on the notebook you’re considering. Linux includes support for many different sound cards. However, some newer graphics subsystems used in notebooks, such as NeoMagic’s 256AV, also include sound support, but they rely on proprietary drivers to play audio CDs and so on.
