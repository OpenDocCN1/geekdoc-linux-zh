You can then confirm the change by using `setserial`'s -a option:

```
# setserial -a /dev/ttyS1

/dev/ttyS1, Line 1, UART: 16550A, Port: 0x02f8, IRQ: 4
    Baud_base: 115200, close_delay: 50, divisor: 0
    closing_wait: 3000, closing_wait2: infinite

    Flags: spd_normal skip_test
```

The `setserial` command will print as much information as it can determine about the specified serial port.

If you need additional serial ports for your notebook, consider getting a serial-port PCMCIA card.

# ## Internal Modems

Most notebooks, including the Apple iBook, come with an internal modulator/demodulator, known as a *modem*. Having a working internal modem in your notebook when using Linux is a wonderful thing. You don't have to carry an extra PCMCIA card or use a large, bulky external modem with its associated cables and power supply.

That said, here's the bad news: In an effort to cut manufacturing costs, save money, and reduce chip count, most Intel-based notebooks on the market today use proprietary "software-driven" modems that depend on closed-source drivers and that work only with operating systems from the PC software monopoly.

These modems are, in my opinion, simply bad engineering even though today's CPUs have more than enough horsepower to handle the work with software. As much as I'd like to say that you should not buy a laptop with an internal "controllerless" modem, the truth is that many so-equipped notebooks work well with Linux—with the exception of these useless modems. Even worse, no manufacturer (with the possible exception of one at the time this chapter was written) will release the specifications so that an Open Source solution may be developed.

This puts you, the consumer, at the mercy of proprietary software and a slow-to-respond industry. The modem controller chip manufacturers do not live in Internet time, but mobile Linux users do. The industry's sloth-like response to consumer needs, bug fixes, and other problems is only exacerbated by the notebook manufacturing industry's acquiescence in continuing to build notebooks with these devices. This situation won't change until the Linux notebook user base grows and consumer demand forces reevaluation of the hardware or software design strategy.
