### Modem and connector cable (RJ-11)

What? Your notebook doesn't come with an internal modem that works with Linux? You must have a notebook with one of those worthless software-driven modems that currently plague the notebook industry. The good news is that eventually the number of Linux notebook users will force the industry to reconsider the use of these evil little items, open up the specs, or revert to using non-CPU-burdening communication devices. In the meantime, choose a notebook that has a real modem. If your notebook has only one Type II slot, get a combination Ethernet/modem PC card. If your notebook has two slots, you should still get a combination Ethernet/modem card, but one that doesn't take up two slots (such as a Linksys EtherFast 10/100 + 56K V.90). If you need only a modem card, don't buy a winmodem PC Card (yes, these pieces of junk have infected PC Card modem lines, too). Buy a PC Card "real" modem, such as a Viking 56K V.90, or a modem with an XJACK connector. XJACK and similar connectors help you avoid proprietary dongles that can break, and you need only a spare phone cord.

### Notebook car power adapter (12VDC to 120VAC)

This is very handy if you're traveling a long distance by car or work out of your car. Many notebook manufacturers offer these adapters, which usually plug into a cigarette lighter or, if you drive an SUV, the "data power port" (which can probably still light cigarettes).

### Notebook computer

Lighter, faster, more memory, more hard drive space, extended battery life, overnight warranty replacement, and more features using open-spec hardware—this is what mobile Linux users demand! But don't forget yours when you go on the road!

### Notebook printer cable

As notebooks and printers get smaller and smaller, some things stay the same, such as thick DB-25 Centronics printer cables. However, if you're going on the road, you don't have to lug a 6-foot printer cable! You can buy shorter 24- to 30-inch printer cables at most computer stores. Some printers, such as the Canon BJC-50, include a proprietary lighter-weight cable (albeit 62 inches long). But if you have an infrared printer such as the BJC-50, the only reason to carry a printer cable is to hook up to someone else's printer.

### Pager

Cheaper and lighter than most cell phones, pagers are a convenient way to be notified of phone calls, news, important events, sports scores, or stock reports. However, a pager can be a modern-day ball and chain, especially if you're shackled to one because of work. Many paging features are now being incorporated into new cell phone technology, so you might want to invest in a higher-end cell phone and ditch your pager.

### Passport

If you're traveling overseas, you'll need a passport. Don't let yours lapse or lose it! If you need a new passport or visa to another country, you can try one of the many new travel document services, such as http://www.traveldocs.com, to help expedite the paperwork. U.S. citizens can renew passports through any post office (ask for form DSP-82). You'll need two professional passport photos, your lapsed passport, and possibly other documents.

### PDA

If you have one, don't forget it. If you're going to buy one, get a monochrome Palm PDA and ignore any Windows CE device (many manufacturers have dropped their CE device lines).

### Phone-line tester

If you're worried about damaging your notebook's modem by inadvertently plugging it into a digital phone line, practice safe computing and get a cheap tester. These typically have an LED indicator to tell you if the line is safe.

### Removable floppy drive

Many mini-notebooks do not come with an internal floppy drive. A good alternative might be an LS-120 drive, which can also be made into a bootable Linux 120MB filesystem. You'll probably need to treat the device as an IDE drive and change the boot order in your notebook's BIOS. These drives work with Linux, but the status in the USB versions is unknown at this time.

### Rescue CD-ROM

One of the biggest hits at LinuxWorld in March of 1999 in San Jose, California was Linucare's distribution of emergency bootable Linux CD-ROMs in the shape of a business card. Stuff one of these babies in your notebook, reboot, and you'll boot to a 38MB Debian system loaded with software recovery tools (and a slide show of the developers). See http://www.linucare.com.

### Ricochet modem

If you want to avoid juggling a modem, phone cord, or cell phone just to get your e-mail, take a look at the Ricochet modem at http://www.metricom.com. These wireless modems, which use one of your notebook's serial ports and include a rechargeable three-hour battery, currently provide a 28.8Kbps connection with 128Kbps in the works for mid-2000. More than 40 college campuses across the U.S. have full coverage. Several hundred cities and airports are also covered. Linux works well with the Ricochet network.

### RJ-11 inline coupler

These handy items allow you to extend your reach from the wall's phone jack. Many PC card modems with proprietary dongles provide only 3 feet of length. Extending your reach might allow you to browse the Web or do e-mail while sitting on your hotel room's balcony.

### RJ-11 phone cord

You can get these just about anywhere for a few bucks, but a much better product is a retractable phone cord that fits into its own credit-card-sized holder. Products from Targus and Fuji typically store 8 feet or more of phone line in an extremely compact package.

### RJ-11 phone splitter

This is very handy if you need to share a single phone line but want to have your phone and notebook plugged in at the same time. Be aware that some splitters, especially those that plug into the wall, are designed to provide two separate phone lines, not split a single line. (Most homes are wired for two lines by default. Only two wires of the four-wire RJ-11 are used for a single line.)

### Spare batteries for cell phone

This is a good idea if you're going to be on the road for a while but don't want to take the recharging unit.

### Spare batteries for PDA

Fortunately, Palm PDA users don't have to worry about replacing batteries very often, unless they go on month-long road trips. Users of WinCE (makes you "wince," doesn't it) had better stock up, because battery life on most supporting hardware platforms is measured in hours.

### Spare battery for pager

Beep, buzz, or vibrate, pagers require batteries. Pagers with LCD screens, Web browsing, and news and stock quotes require even more juice.

### Spare dongles

I'm not talking about the evil software-enabling dongle (you deserve what you get if you buy software that requires a hardware dongle), but a spare dongle for your modem or network card. See http://www.thesupplynet.com for replacement dongles for nearly any manufacturer's PC card.

### Spare notebook battery

If your notebook doesn't support the use of two batteries at once, you can take along a spare. Many laptop batteries (especially NiMh) last only a year with daily use and recharging. Li-Ion batteries are generally rated for 400 discharge/charge cycles (for at least a 50 percent charge rate), so if you plan to use your notebook for a couple years, a spare battery is a good idea.

### Spare pointer eraser

ThinkPad and some Toshiba and Hitachi users will definitely need to replace the eraser-type pointer caps at some point in time. Be forewarned that they are not cheap, and usually cost $3 to $6 each. Although these pointing devices work well, getting stuck out on the road with a broken pointer cap can be, well, somewhat embarrassing. ThinkPad TrackPoint tips are red (part #84G6537), although a blue variant is listed on IBM's ThinkPad accessories Web page (part #12J4552). You can order the red tips (four for about $22 with shipping) from http://www.compu-lock.com/default.html.

### Surge suppressor

You'll find a number of smaller AC surge protectors available for mobile users with laptops. You should always use one to protect your computer equipment, especially while you're on the road.

### Three-pronged adapter

Always carry a three-prong to two-prong AC adapter when traveling. Many old (two-prong) outlets are still in use, and many notebook AC adapters use three prongs.
