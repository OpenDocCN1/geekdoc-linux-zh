
## Universal Serial Bus

Universal Serial Bus (USB) was originally developed as a standard in 1995 by several large computer manufacturers, such as IBM, Intel, Compaq, NEC, and Northern Telecom. Designed to promote the use of inexpensive, low-power, yet high-speed peripherals, USB supports data transfer rates of 12Mbps and supposedly allows up to 127 devices to be chained or supported via USB hubs. Cable lengths are generally limited to 5 meters, with shorter cables preferred to avoid drops in voltage.

Most laptops (and many desktops) manufactured since 1997 support USB by providing a small port through which to attach a hub or other USB peripherals such as a keyboard, mouse, CD-ROM drive, modem, printer, scanner, or digital camera. One of the benefits of using USB is supposed to be the reduction of expensive bus connectors and add-in cards; another is the elimination of system resource conflicts (such as IRQs). USB devices are also supposed to be hot-swappable, much like PCMCIA cards, and many offer pass-through connectors to allow chaining of multiple devices (although most USB users will want to use self-powered hubs to support multiple devices for best performance).

USB support for Linux first appeared in the 2.3 development series of the Linux kernel, although patches were available in the Linux source tree for later 2.2 kernels using loadable modules and start and stop scripts. The first devices supported included various keyboards and mice. Native USB support will become a reality with the advent of the 2.4 series of Linux kernels. Table 2.8 lists a number of devices that currently work with Linux.

To find out how to use USB, the status of USB, or how to make USB work with older Linux kernels, browse to [http://www.linux-usb.org/](http://www.linux-usb.org/).

Manufacturer and protocol information about USB may be found at [http://www.usb.org/](http://www.usb.org/).

## IEEE 1394 (FireWire)

One of the newest high-speed data transfer protocols is IEEE 1394, also known as *FireWire*. First championed by Apple Computer, FireWire is much faster than USB, with speeds of up to 400Mbps. Perhaps intended as a replacement for the Small Computer Systems Interface (SCSI), FireWire is also being touted as the ideal way to transfer digital video, or perhaps to enable Local Area Network (LAN) connections.

## Your Notebook's Battery

Without a doubt, mobile Linux users on the go are held hostage by one of the most critical notebook elements: the battery! Battery life is crucial to the success of a road warrior, and it determines the amount of productivity and time before a working piece of equipment turns into a paperweight.

Although there are many different types of batteries, such as alkaline, carbon zinc, silver oxide, zinc air, and manganese dioxide, only three types of batteries are commonly in use with portable computing equipment:

- NiCd (nickel cadmium). One of the heaviest of the rechargeable types, these batteries have fallen out of favor because of environmental concerns (cadmium) and the development of improved battery technology resistant to the "memory" effect of residual charging. This means that these batteries "remember" low-charge states. Although inexpensive, these batteries do not handle abuse, such as overcharging or undercharging.
- NiMH (nickel-metal-hydride). Used in many different notebooks, these batteries can have problems with temperature extremes and overcharging. They are more expensive than NiCd and might not last as long.
- LiIon (lithium-ion). These batteries are the latest darling in notebook technology. They are durable, hold a charge, provide higher power, and are not prone to the memory effect. Although more expensive, these batteries are somewhat more "environmentally correct," but like all batteries, they should be recycled.

Battery usage and capabilities vary between notebooks. Some models can use two batteries for extended life, and some manufacturers, such as Sony, offer quad-capacity LiIon batteries for 10 or more hours of full-throttle, open-source mobile computing!

Some obvious steps to increase your mobile activity include the following:

- Spinning down the hard drive
- Turning off the LCD backlight (if possible)
- Not using external devices
- Ejecting unused PCMCIA cards

You can also use the software and procedures outlined in Chapter 7, "Advanced Power Management," when you're on the road.

## Docking Stations

Although this book focuses on mobile Linux computing, most notebook owners know that some laptops are made to fit into a compatible docking station. These stations are designed to easily accommodate laptop insertion and removal, and they offer the convenience of turning your mobile machine into a desktop unit.

External devices, such as a keyboard, printer, monitor, modem, mouse, audio system, or network connection, usually remain attached to the docking unit. The laptop is then just inserted into the dock before you go to work. Some docking stations also offer an additional hard drive or other form of storage.

Most likely, however, this convenience comes at a price. Stations are designed to specifically match a particular notebook model. There's no such thing as a standard docking connector in the notebook industry!

However, at least two companies build and sell what are touted as "Universal Port Replicators." These devices, which offer serial, parallel, keyboard, network, mouse, and video connections, connect through your notebook's PCMCIA port but are not compatible with Linux.
