# The Earth or Atomic Time?

Most people believe that GMT—or, more properly, UTC—is based on the time kept at Greenwich, England. However, UTC is based on atomic clocks kept around the world and distributed by radio. Another variation of time is UT1, which is based on the rotation angle of the Earth. These times can be different! When this happens (about every 18 months), according to the U.S. Naval Observatory, a leap second is introduced into UTC. For a detailed discussion, browse to http://tycho.usno.navy.mil/leapsec.html

As you know, the world is split in many different time zones. These zones run in increments of plus or minus hours (and in some cases, 30 minutes) east and west of longitude 0, which runs through Greenwich, England (thus the common, but not technically used, term Greenwich Mean Time, or GMT). For example, U.S. time zones are negatively offset in one-hour increments (because they are west of longitude 0).

Linux keeps a database of time zone information under the /usr/share/zoneinfo directory. This information, along with the system time, may be manipulated and used by various commands included with nearly every Linux distribution. These commands are listed in Table 16.1.

Linux system time is based on the number of seconds elapsed since January 1, 1970. The number of seconds is currently stored as a long data type (a 32-bit unsigned value), but this value (unlike the MacOS; see the following sidebar) will not be large enough to hold the correct number of seconds by January 19, 2038. Although most time calculations won't be affected, some will, and this might cause problems due to incorrect assumptions made in computing the difference between dates.

# Macintosh Time

The Apple MacOS is immune to Y2K problems because time is stored in a 64-bit value, represented by the number of seconds elapsed since midnight of January 1, 1904. Geographic and time zone data is stored in the Mac's hardware (PRAM). This information includes latitude, longitude, daylight saving time (DST), and UTC (colloquially designated as GMT in Apple's programming manual).

Red Hat, Inc. has certified that at least on Intel-based Linux systems, its distributions' core 15 time-related commands, six time-related daemons, and six related software libraries are Y2K-compliant.
