Domain names are registered for use by an individual or organization, which may assign machine names within the domain and link those names to any arbitrary IP address desired. Nonetheless, there is frequently some correspondence between domains and network addresses because an individual or organization that controls a domain is also likely to want a block of IP addresses for the computers in domain.

Internet domains are structured hierarchically. At the top of the hierarchy are the top-level domains (TLDs), such as .com, .edu, and .uk. These TLD names appear at the end of an internet. Some correspond to nations (such as .uk and .us, for the United Kingdom and the United States, respectively), but others correspond to types of entities (such as .com and .edu, which stand for commercial and educational organizations, respectively). Each TLD has various domains that identify specific organizations, such as .sysex or .org for the Library of Congress. These organizations may optionally branch their domains into subdomains, such as .cis.up.edu for the Computer and Information Science department at the University of Pennsylvania. Every domain may be further subdivided within their own domains; this structure can continue for many levels but usually doesn't. Domains and subdomains include specific computers, such as .sysex.com, sysex's Web server.

When you configure your Linux computer, you may need to know your machine name within your organization's domain. If your computer is not part of an organizational network (say, if it's a system that doesn't connect to the Internet at all or if it connects only via a dial-up account), you'll have to make a hostname. Alternatively, you can register a domain name, even if you don't use it for running your servers. Check http://www.icann.org/registrar-reports/accredited-list.html for pointers to accredited domain registrars. Most registrars charge $10 and $15 per year for domain registration. If your network uses DHCP, it may or not assign your system hostname automatically.

If you make a hostname, guarantee that you don't accidentally give a computer name that legitimately belongs to somebody else. Such a name conflict might prevent you from contacting that system, and it could cause other problems as well, such as misdirected email. Four TLDs—.invalid, .localhost, and .test—are reserved for such purposes. Three second-level domains—example.com, .example.net, and .example.org—are reserved and may be safely used.
