# USB Basics

USB is a protocol and hardware port for transferring data to and from devices. It allows for
many (and varied) devices per port other than ATA or SCSI and gives better
speed RS-232 serial and parallel ports. The USB 1.0 and 1.1 specifications allow for
u to 127 devices and 12Mbps of data transfer. USB 2.0 allows for much higher transfer
rates—480Mbps, to be precise. USB 3.0, introduced in 2010, specifies a theoretical maxi-
speed of 4.8Gbps, although 3.2Gbps is a more likely topic to speak in. USB 3.0
devices require a new physical connection.

> Data speeds may be expressed in bits per second (bps) or
tples, such as megabytes per second (Mbps) or gigabits per
second (Gbps); or in bytes per second (B/s) or multiples thereof, such as
megabytes per second (MB/s). In most cases, there are 8 bytes per byte, so
multply or dividing by 8 may be necessary if you are trying to compare
speeds of devices that use different measures.

USB is the preferred interface for many external devices, including printers,
scanners, mice, digital cameras, flash drives, and music players. USB keyboards, Ethernet
adapters, modems, speakers, hard drives, and other devices are also available, although
USB has yet to dominate these areas as it has others.

Most computers ship with four to eight USB ports. (A few years ago, two USB ports
were more common.) Each port can handle one device by itself, but you can use a USB hub
to connect several devices to each port. Thus, you can theoretically connect huge numbers
of USB devices to a computer. In practice, you may run into speed problems, particularly if
y're using USB 1.x for devices that tend to transfer a lot of data, such as scanners, print-
ers, or hard drives.
