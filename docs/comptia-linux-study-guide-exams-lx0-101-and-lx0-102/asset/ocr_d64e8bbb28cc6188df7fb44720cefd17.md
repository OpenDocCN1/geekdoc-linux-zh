At its core, network hardware is hardware that facilitates the transfer of data between computers. Hardware's most used for networking includes features that help this this transfer in various ways. For instance, such hardware may include address data intended for specific computers, as described later in the section “Addressing Hardware.” When basically non-network hardware is pressed into service as a network, the lack of such features may limit the utility of the hardware or require extra software to make up for the lack. If extra software is required, you're unlikely to notice the deficiencies as a user or system administrator because the protocol drivers handle the work, but this makes the hardware more difficult to configure and more prone to sluggishness or other problems than dedicated hardware.

# Investigating Types of Network Hardware

Linux servers support several types of network hardware. The most common of these is Ethernet, which comes in several varieties. Most modern Ethernet hardware uses twisted-pair cabling, which consists of pairs of wires twisted around each other to minimize interference. Varieties of Ethernet are identified by a -T suffix to the Ethernet variety, as in 10Base-T or 100Base-T. The numbers denote the speed of the protocol in megabits per second (Mbps). In the late 90s, 1000Base-T took over from 10Base-T as the standard in office and home networks. More recently, 1000Base-T and Ethernet variants that use optical cabling and are capable of 1000Mbps speeds (that is, gigabit Ethernet) have become the standard, with 10-gigabit Ethernet the new emerging standard.

Other types of network hardware exist, but are less common than Ethernet. These include Token Ring, LocalTalk, Fibre Distributed Data Interface (FDDI), High-Performance Parallel Interface (HIPPI), and Fibre Channel. Token Ring was once common on some IBM-dominated networks in the 1990s but has been steadily losing ground to Ethernet. FDDI, HIPPI, and Fibre Channel are all high-speed interfaces that are used in high-performance applications. Some of protocols support significantly greater maximum cable lengths than does Ethernet, making them suitable for linking buildings that are many, or even miles, apart.

Wireless networking (aka Wi-Fi) is an exception to Ethernet dominance. Wireless protocols use 802.11a, 802.11b, 802.11g, and 802.11n. These protocols support maximum speeds of 11Mbps (for 802.11b), 54Mbps (for 802.11a and 802.11g), and 300 Mbps (for 802.11n). With the exception of the rarely used 802.11a, Wi-Fi protocols are compatible with one another, albeit at the speed of the slowest protocol in use. Wireless networking is particularly useful for laptop computers, but it's even handy for computers in homes and small offices that don't have adequate wired network infrastructures in place.
