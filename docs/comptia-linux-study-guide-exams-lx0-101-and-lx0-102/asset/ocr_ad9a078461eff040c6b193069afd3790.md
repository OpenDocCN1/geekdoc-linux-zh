# # Configuring PATA Disks

PATA disks once ruled the roost in the x86 PC world, but today SATA disks have largely supplanted them. Thus, you're most likely to encounter PATA disks on older computers—say, from 2005 or earlier. PATA disks are still readily available, though.

As the full name implies, PATA disks use a parallel interface, meaning several bits of data are transferred over the cable at once. Thus, PATA cables are wide, supporting a total of 48 bits, depending on the variant of PATA. You can connect to two devices to each PATA connector on a motherboard or plug-in PATA controller, meaning that PATA disks typically have three connectors—one for the motherboard and two for disks.

PATA disks must be configured as masters or slaves. This can be done via jumpers on the disks themselves. Typically, the master device sits at the end of the cable, and the slave device resides on the middle connector. All modern PATA disks also support an option called select. When you set this option, the drive configures itself automatically based on its position on the PATA cable. Your easiest configuration is to set all PATA devices to the select-cable option; you can then attach them to whatever position is convenient, and the drives configure themselves.

For performance, disks should be placed on separate controllers rather than master and slave on a single controller, because each PATA controller has a limited throughput that may be exceeded by two drives. Until recently, most motherboards had at least two controllers, so putting each drive on its own controller is not a problem until you install more than two drives in a single controller.

In Linux, PATA disks have traditionally been identified as /dev/hda, /dev/hdb, and so, with /dev/hda being the master on the first controller, /dev/hdb being the slave on the first controller. Thus, gaps can occur in the numbering scheme—if you have master disks on the first and second controllers but no slave disks, your system will contain /dev/hda and /dev/hdc instead of /dev/hdc. Partitions are identified after the main name, as in /dev/sda1, /dev/hda2, and so on.

The naming rules for disks also apply to optical media, except that these media typically are not partitioned. Most Linux distributions also create a link to your optical drive under the name /dev/cdrom or /dev/dvd. Removable PATA disks, such as Zip disks, are given identifiers if they were fixed PATA disks, optionally including partition identifiers.

Most modern Linux distributions favor fewer PATA drives that treat PATA disks as if they were SCSI disks. Thus, you may find that your drive filenames follow the SCSI rules rather than the PATA rules even if you have PATA disks.
