
## Partition Peculiarities in Linux

In Windows, partitions and hard drives are represented using *drive letters*. The first drive or partition is labeled "C:" and the letters climb alphabetically from there.

This is not the case in Linux. Linux recognizes each partition or hard drive as a *directory*, so the inclusion of various hard drives or partitions on your system is seamless. This may seem a little different to you at first, but you'll find that it's sometimes quite useful. It allows for one root file system (a root file system is the very top of the directory tree—it's like C:\ in Windows or MS-DOS, not several, for instance).

It also allows you to have hard drives on other computers integrated seamlessly on yours, so accessing someone else's computer appears to look exactly like accessing your own. All in all, it provides a balanced look and feel to your computer.

## How Partitions Are Named in Linux

This is a relatively important part that you probably don't want to skip reading. The inner workings of the computer can sometimes be confusing, and this is what Linux uses to decide what to call your hard drive.

Most desktop computers come outfitted with IDE or ATA hard drives. The alternative to IDE is known as SCSI, and we'll talk about that in a moment. IDE hard drives are connected to the *IDE Controller* card in your computer. The controller card does just what it says—it controls all of the IDE devices, like your hard drives or CD-ROM attached to it.

Each of the devices on an IDE controller is named by using a combination of letters and a number. Each device profile is stored in a directory on your system called /dev. And each device is given a name. IDE devices all begin with the letters “hd.” Each is then followed by an additional letter, determined by where the drive is on the controller, and a number, arbitrarily assigned when you create the partition.

During the installation process, you're going to need to know which your drive is called under Linux so that you know which parts to format and which to leave alone. This is of particular interest if you're dual booting. Here is a rough idea of how you can know which drive on your system is which.

There are both primary and secondary IDE devices. Each of these two classes is broken down into a “master” device and a “slave” device. A drive is primary or secondary, based upon where it is plugged in on the motherboard (which is what handles most of the information traffic in your computer).

Each of the four possible devices that can connect to your IDE controller is assigned a letter; the primary master drive is given the letter “a,” the primary slave the letter “b,” the secondary master the letter “c,” and the secondary slave the letter “d.” Because the CD-ROM often has two different names (dev/cdrom and dev/hdc), you may find that the drives skip a letter—that is, you could have a “hdb” drive and then “hdd.”

If you have two hard drives on your computer, the primary master is probably running Windows. If you plan on dual booting, make sure that you don’t modify the “hda” drive during the course of the installation.

If you’re still not really certain by what name Linux knows your drive, you can gain additional hints by how large the hard drive is. A 10-GB hard drive might come up as having something like 9700 MB of hard drive space. If you want to install Linux on one that is substantially smaller, say 4 GB, you’ll notice one with 3900 MB of space—that’s the one you want to go for.

Each of your SCSI (often called “scuzzy”) devices is named with a similar convention. However, a SCSI controller operates by *chaining* your devices together. When you chain devices, you simply connect the first device in the chain (usually given the number 0) to the controller card. The second device is connected to the first and so on. Most SCSI controllers allow for eight devices on a chain, numbered 0–7.

SCSI devices are named with the prefix “sd” and the position it occupies on the chain. The first SCSI device is called “sda.” The second is known as “sdb,” and so on. These devices will also have numbers at the end.

Again, if you’re not familiar with how your computer is put together, you can gain clues to which hard drive you want to format based upon the size of the drive. You can also open up the computer to see where everything goes together. As long as you don’t change anything, you won’t damage anything, and it will provide you with some good experience.

## Understanding the Partition Table

Now that you’ve either skipped over the part which helps explain partitions or read it, we can explain the partition table. As always, the friendly people at Red Hat have included their own advice on the left side of the screen. It's usually pretty good, so don't be afraid to follow it.

The right frame is where most of our concentration will lie. At the top of the screen is a partition summary, and below it is a hard drive summary.

### The Partition Summary

The partition summary displays information about partitions already created on your computer. It is located at the upper left side of your screen and usually takes up most of it. There are a number of different categories here that you should understand and know about.

- **Mount Point** *A mount point* is the directory that Linux assigns to a partition or hard drive. When creating partitions, you must specify a mount point to locate the partition on the directory tree. If you include a Windows disk, for instance, you might use the mount point /mnt/win. Linux can use many, many different partitions, and we'll discuss many of them when we begin creating partitions.
- **Device** *The device* is the device name that we talked about in the previous section. Device names are supplied arbitrarily based upon a device's connection inside the computer, and you will not need to supply them.
- **Requested** When you create a partition, you must specify how large you want it to be. *The requested* category tells you how much space you've requested for use with the partition.
- **Actual** After the partition is created, the actual partition itself may take up more than the space you request. This is not always the case, however. *The actual* field will tell you how much space the partition presently takes up on the hard drive. The number in this field and that in the requested field usually do not differ by more than one megabyte.
- **Type** *The type* of the partition is the format of the partition. You have the opportunity to supply four different partition types: Linux native, Linux swap, FAT16, and FAT32. Linux native is the type that you will use for all of your Linux data. A swap partition is a partition that “mirrors” the amount of random access memory on your computer. It allows you to use your hard drive in case your RAM becomes overloaded. The FAT options refer to the File Allocation Table systems used by Windows and MS-DOS. If you want to include a partition of greater than 2 GB that's compatible with Windows 98, make sure to use FAT32. If you're running Windows 95 or lower, you should use FAT16.

### The Drive Summary

The drive summary contains information about the physical hard drives presently installed on your computer. It will list them in order, hda first, hdb second, and so on. You will notice in this list that you can skip letters, so hdd may follow hdb or even hda. The drive summary is under the partition summary. There are several categories that you should understand here, too.

- **Drive Geometry** This information is fairly technical and relatively unimportant to your understanding of the installation. The drive geometry determines how much space is allocated for your hard drive. The drive geometry is composed of cylinders, heads, and sectors.
- **Total** The total category tells you how much information your hard drive can hold in megabytes.
- **Used** The used category tells you how much of the total space your partitions are taking up.
- **Free** The free category is simply the difference between the total and the used space. You should use this category to decide how much space to allocate for partitions.

### Working with the Partitions

Directly below the partition summary is a group of buttons. One says “add,” another “delete,” and yet another “edit.” In the course of working with the partitions, you're going to be deleting some partitions, adding others, and so on and so forth. These are the buttons that we use to accomplish these tasks.

Your first task is to identify which hard drive you're going to install Linux on. If you plan on dual booting using two hard drives, you'll probably be installing it on either hdb or hdd—the primary slave hard drive or the secondary slave hard drive. If you do not plan on dual booting or if you plan on dual booting on a single drive, you'll use the drive labeled hda. Preparing for each of the three procedures is described below.
