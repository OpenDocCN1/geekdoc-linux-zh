# ## Logging In and Out of Your Account

Linux is a true multi-user system, and therefore makes use of many different user accounts. We use the word “account” virtually the same way you would apply it to your various bank accounts. Each bank account has its own features specific to it. Similarly, each user has his own account, which he can modify to fit his own tastes. (Though you have a little more latitude with a user account on your Linux machine than you do with your bank accounts.)

Windows is also a multi-user operating system, but it lacks a number of the security features that Linux provides. Rather than provide a genuine account, Windows basically provides you with a way to select different user profiles to tailor your experience with Windows.

To access your account, you must log in to your computer. This is a fairly simple process. After the computer has rebooted, a screen will appear asking for your user name. Simply type in the one that you created during the installation and press the enter key. Then a new window will pop up asking for your password. Type this in, too, and press enter. Voila! You’ve just logged in. Pretty simple.

**Note**: If you didn’t create your own user account, consult Chapter Five for the process. There are some basic systems administration procedures detailed there, among them how to create a new account.

When you’ve logged in, you’ll see a pretty sparse desktop laid out before you and a relatively boring panel at the bottom of the screen. This is akin to the Windows “Start Bar.” In the next chapter we make this look a lot more exciting, but for the moment all we want to do is follow the procedure for logging out of the system.

To log out, click on the stylized foot at the far left of the bottom panel. Near the bottom of the menu that appears is an option labeled “Log Out.” If you click it, a new menu will appear. You can just click okay.

The screen that appeared when you booted up will reappear with a prompt asking you to please input your user name. That’s not necessary at the moment, since none of our tasks in this chapter require logging in.
