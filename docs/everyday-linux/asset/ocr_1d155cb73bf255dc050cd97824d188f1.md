The only two required fields are the Login Name and the Full Name. The remaining page will have a list of the current users on the system. To clear this, you can click "quit." First, however, we want to add your regular user to the wheel group, which will allow you to use the su command.

# # Adding Your User to the Wheel Group

To add your personal account to the wheel group you need to have the list of users on the screen. Find your own account and click on it. This begins the “user editing” process. You’ll notice that it looks quite similar—almost exactly the same, in fact—to the user creation page.

To place yourself in the wheel group, you should modify the “Supplementary Groups” field. Type “wheel” in the field and then click on accept. That’s all it takes to add your personal account to any group on the system, not just the wheel group. A list of groups can be found by clicking on the arrow button next to the “Group (opti)” field.

Now that we’ve added your user to the wheel group, we’re more or less finished with account editing. Click “quit” to close the user accounts page. Our next task is to make your floppy drive and your CD-ROM drive user-mountable so that any user can use them.

# # Making Drives User-Mountable

Before we actually explain the process, it would be a good idea to introduce the concept of mounting and unmounting drives. When we installed the Linux operating system, we had to specify *mount points* for each one of the partitions we created, and we explained that Linux recognizes each drive (be it a floppy drive, CD-ROM, or hard disk drive) as a part of the directory tree.

# ## Mounting and Unmounting Drives

When you make a drive accessible, this is known as *mounting* a drive. When you mount a drive, the files on it become available for general access (depending on what permissions you set on the drive when you mount it). Conversely, when you *unmount* a drive, you deny access to the drive—it’s as though it doesn’t actually exist on your computer.
