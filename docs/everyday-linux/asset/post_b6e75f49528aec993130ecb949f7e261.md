## Netscape

Netscape for Linux is almost exactly the same as it is for Windows. The look and feel is just a little different, but the preferences and other properties are fundamentally the same. For that reason, we won't cover too much material with Netscape other than setting up mail and news preferences.

To configure Netscape Mail and News, you first need to open up Netscape. When it has finished loading, click on the top menu item labeled "edit." Then click on the menu option "Preferences." This will open the preferences window in Netscape, which is where you can change what your mail options are.

There is a rather long list of preferences. We simply want to look at the mail and newsgroup preferences for now. Figure 9-1 shows the preferences menu with the options open to mail and news groups. The first item we want to look at is configuring the mail servers.

## Netscape Mail

When you look at the mail preferences, there are essentially four different areas that you need to fill in. One is the incoming mail server. You also need an outgoing mail server. Finally, you should fill in your username and password.

- **Incoming Mail Server**—The incoming mail server usually operates using a POP3 or IMAP protocol. You might see "POP server" instead of incoming mail server used in some versions of Netscape or during your travels on the World Wide Web. The incoming mail server is the server that receives your e-mail.

What this means in plainer English is that there is a computer used to receive e-mail from the Internet. This is known as the incoming mail server, and it will store your e-mail until you pick it up and delete it from the server. It is essentially a post office with many different post office boxes. When you check your e-mail, your username acts as the post office box number and your password acts as the key. You take your mail out of the box, close it, and lock it. This is the computer equivalent to downloading your mail and logging out of the incoming mail server.

You can find out what your incoming mail server is by talking to a customer service representative at your Internet Service Provider.

- **Outgoing Mail Server** — On smaller systems, the outgoing mail server may have the same name as the incoming mail server. This simply means that the same computer handles both outgoing and incoming mail. The outgoing mail server is sometimes called the “SMTP Server,” and you may see it referred to as such in your version of Netscape or on the World Wide Web.

The outgoing mail server handles all of the routing necessary for your e-mail to make it from your post office (the outgoing mail server) to another (another incoming mail server). It ensures that the e-mail you send makes it to the address that you specify.

If you’re not sure what your outgoing mail server is called, you should call your ISP to find out. They’ll be able to tell you what you need to know.

- **Username and Password**—Your username and password are your “box number” and “key” for your electronic mailbox. It’s how you access your email.

In Netscape, you must supply your username. If you don’t want your password to be remembered, you should leave the password field blank. You’ll be prompted for your password every time you try to download your mail. Make sure that you don’t share your username or password with anyone, unless you really want someone else going through your e-mail.

## Netscape News

Many users who aren’t familiar with the Internet do not realize there is a vast array of newsgroups available on the Internet. Topics range from flame wars (see alt.flame), religion, abortion rights, to areas we’d rather not tread. You’ll be able to find almost anything you could wish to read on newsgroups. Music and TV fans will be delighted with the plethora of groups devoted to specific actors and actresses. You can even find a number of groups devoted to teaching you how to use programming languages or helping you with your new Linux system. This system of newsgroups is more commonly referred to as Usenet.

Your ISP will have what is known as a *news server* to which you can connect and download messages available on newsgroups to which you are subscribed. All you need to supply is the name of the news server, which you can obtain by calling your ISP.

To add a news server, simply click on the “Add” button. A new window will pop up, asking you for the address of the news server and which port you should connect to. Unless otherwise instructed by your ISP, you should leave the port at 119—this is the standard news port.

When you first configure your newsgroup preferences, Netscape will assume that you have a news server attached to your machine, and will call this server “news.” You should add a new one, and set the new one as default. Then remove the one labeled “news,” unless you have set up newsgroups on your computer. You can use the edit button to correct any mistakes in entries that you’ve created.

If you find that it takes too long to download messages from a newsgroup, you can tell Netscape to ask your permission before downloading more messages than you specify. In my news preferences, this number is set to fifty, since downloading fifty messages usually doesn't take me too long.

“Netiquette”—“Netiquette”is a term for the proper etiquette exercised on the Internet. This applies to all aspects of Internet use, but when dealing with Usenet, there are some specific things to know.

You should keep in mind that in a public forum there are rules, even if they are broken quite often. It is never wise to engage in public flame wars unless the forum is specifically devoted to doing so (such as the alt.flame newsgroup I mentioned before). A flame war is a large series of messages between two or more people which are inflammatory or composed of personal attacks. Flame wars scream, "I'm a pathetic Internet junkie who has no life and plenty of time to defend myself in front of people who couldn't care less." Besides that, for those people who are truly interested in reading the newsgroup, flame wars take up quite a lot of space (it's aggravating to download fifty messages and get only ten that are worth reading) and annoy almost everyone on the group.

It is also wise to take some time to get the "feel" of a newsgroup before posting. This is known as "lurking," since you read messages but don't post any to a newsgroup. Oftentimes, the posters to a group are "regulars" and have established a rapport that a clueless newbie poster might unbalance. This tends to upset people, and that poster might find himself ostracized for some time. For instance, I joined a mailing list some time ago that is devoted to discussing the writing of author Stephen Lawhead. The administrator and some others were engaging in some discussion about Greek grammar; knowing a little bit of it myself, I thought an appropriate introduction to the group would be to join the discussion. I was wrong; the administrator quickly reprimanded me via personal e-mail, and I have never posted there again, despite being a member of the list for several years.

## Moderated vs. Unmoderated Newsgroups

Generally speaking, you should read moderated newsgroups.

A moderated Usenet newsgroup is one that has a person filtering the various posts that come through the group. This person is known as the moderator, and he usually prevents unnecessary messages from getting through. This greatly reduces the amount of "Spam," or unsolicited commercial e-mail (UCE) and posts with no content other than flames.

If you're more daring, you can read and post to an unmoderated newsgroup. These groups tend to be far more rowdy and less organized. People are less willing to engage in thoughtful conversation and more apt to produce what are known as "clueless newbie posts." These involve pointless polls and one- or two-sentence opinions designed to generate debate. You can find the occasional jewel on an unmoderated newsgroup, and they can prove to be just as interesting (if not more so) as the moderated newsgroups.

Some types of unmoderated newsgroups attract less Spam and flame wars than others, fortunately. You'll be able to readily distinguish these, but a general clue is whether a newsgroup is devoted to helping the readers or to tending debates. Unmoderated debates often become flame wars.
