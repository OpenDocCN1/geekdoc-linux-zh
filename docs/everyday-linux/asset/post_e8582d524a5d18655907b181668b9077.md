## Done

When you’re finished making the configuration changes, click on the button labeled “Done”. A new window will ask you if you want to save this configuration, and you should click Yes. For good measure, click “Save” at the bottom of your Network Configurator window.

You must then activate your connection in order to use it.

## Activating Your PPP Connection

To activate your PPP connection, highlight the device you just created (probably ppp0). Then click on the “activate” button at the bottom of the window. Your modem will immediately dial the number that you specified in the setup.

Once you’ve connected, you won’t really know if your PPP connection is functioning properly because nothing happens. To check that you are connected correctly, we will run a simple test. Click on the terminal icon on your panel. When the window comes up, type yahoo.com. If data begins appearing on your screen, you are successfully connected. To exit the program, hit CTRL-C.

What this program does is send small data packets (around 64 bytes apiece) to the Internet address you specified. If these packets are returned to you, ping will tell you that you've received the packages and include how much time it took for the transfer to take place. If your PPP connection is working properly, this transfer will occur. If not, when you abort the program, you will see a message telling you that some number of the packets were sent, and none were received.

You can deactivate the connection by clicking on deactivate at the bottom of the Network Configurator window. Deactivating the interface will cause the modem to hang up.

Connecting this way every time would be rather annoying. However, you don't have to. GNOME has provided an applet that you can use to turn PPP on and off. It's under the "Network" category on the "add an applet" menu. Alternatively, you can click on the main menu, go to "Internet" and click on "RH PPP Dialer," which will allow you to dial from whatever PPP devices are defined.

## Other Connections

Most Linux machines are also equipped for dialup access without using PPP. The most popular program that comes with Linux to do this is called "Minicom." Minicom operates much like Procomm Plus, which has been "out of print" for many years now. It's not difficult to get used to the key commands, and the interface is reasonably user-friendly.

Minicom also makes use of several security features, so that you can specify which users are permitted to access Minicom, for instance.

To run Minicom, click on the main menu and go to "Internet." "Minicom" is an option there; click it and it will start up. To get help, hit CTRL-A and then Z. CTRL-A tells Minicom to come to attention and Z is the command for help. A help screen will appear which supplies you with the proper command set to use to open a dialing directory, edit entries, and dial numbers.

While we could spend a bit of time working with Minicom, it is in your interest to learn to explore your system and learn a few things about it. Text-based dialup connections these days are rare and not often used, so spending a lot of time dissecting each individual command in Minicom would take up time better spent elsewhere. However, in the event that dialup connections are still in use in your area, you now have the chance to get used to using a program and experimenting a little bit with your computer.

If you want to make Minicom available to all users, you need to do some extra finagling. In Chapter Eight, we discuss the command-line interface and how you use it to set up Minicom for all users.

## Troubleshooting Your Modem

There are invariably some modems that have problems with Linux. These are usually compatibility problems, though some are due to the default settings that Linux associates with the modem (using ATZ as an initialization string, for instance).

The subject of modem troubleshooting would require another book entirely, however, we can leave you with a few pointers.

If you're using an external modem, make sure that it is on. Sounds stupid, yes, but a lot of problems can be caused this way. If you have lights on the front of your modem, consult your modem manual to see what they mean. There are some that should be lit when the modem is working properly. Your manual will be able to tell you which.

Your modem manual may also supply you with some standard initialization strings to try in case ATZ doesn't do the job. Every modem will respond to AT&F, which resets the modem to factory defaults (hence the "F"). However, that may not be enough for your modem to work; extra settings may need some tweaking. Your modem manufacturer can usually supply you with two or three strings tested to work with most operating systems and applications.

Your modem manual will also have most of the “AT-commands” that you can use with your modem, and it will explain the meaning of each. More adventurous users can invent their own initialization strings to see if they'll work.

## Adding a Printer to Your Computer

Oftentimes the most inexpensive printers are those which are built exclusively for Windows 95 or 98. This is because most of the printer commands are interpreted by software programs that come with the printer, not by the printer's hardware. In the event that you can't find compatible software for your printer that will work on Linux, you will need to install another printer.

Fortunately, this process is easy. Remember, the control center is your friend. Click on the printer icon in the control center. You'll see a box that looks something like this:
