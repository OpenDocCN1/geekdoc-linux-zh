
# GETTING STARTED

LibreOffice Writer

## A styles-based word-processing system
### Style Guru

![](img/e39350c3a7c0d527d71b0e4097a0ba66_13_0.png)

Writer, LibreOffice’s word-processor component, is more than just a text editor. With its powerful styles system, you can build detailed and elegant documents for business and personal use. By *Mike Saunders*

Back in the 1980s, a word processor was essentially a text editor with some extra features – in other words, a tool for processing your words. Today, however, everyone expects much more from a program like this: rich layout features, high compatibility with other office software, and the ability to structure and navigate complex documents. Writer, LibreOffice’s word processor, is an exceptionally powerful tool with a styles system that really helps to maintain consistency in your documents – but more on that later. First of all, let’s get familiar with the basics! When you launch LibreOffice and choose a new Writer document (or if you’re already running the app, go to *File | New | Text Document* in the menu), a window (Figure 1) will open with the following features:

- 1. **Menubar:** You can access almost all of the features from here. Click, explore, and see what Writer can do!
- 2. **Toolbar:** Provides quick access to commonly used features. Click the arrows on the right-hand side to show more icons, or right-click on the toolbar to choose which icons do display.
- 3. **Styles:** Shows a list of predefined styles, to which you can add more. It’s a good idea to use styles rather than manually formatting each piece of text in terms of font, size, character style, etc.
- 4. **Sidebar:** LibreOffice’s user interface is very flexible, and the sidebar lets you adjust settings for various parts of the document. Click and drag the arrow to expand it.
- 5. **Status bar:** Shows the page count, word count, language being used (click for other languages), and whether the document has been modified.
- 6. **View mode:** These icons let you change how the document is viewed (e.g., as single pages, or side-by-side).
- 7. **Zoom level:** Click and drag the slider (or use the plus and minus buttons) to zoom in and out of your document.

# CUSTOMIZING THE INTERFACE

I'm not going to explain what every single toolbar button does here; you can explore the buttons by simply mousing over them and then reading the tooltip. There are a couple of things to note, though: The undo and redo buttons have drop-down lists beside them, so you can choose how many actions to undo/redo in a single click (this is often a big time-saver). Similarly, the lists (bulleted and numbered) have different styles from which to choose, as well.

![](img/e39350c3a7c0d527d71b0e4097a0ba66_14_1.png)

Figure 2: Writer offers three different user interface layouts.

Figure 1 shows the standard layout, with a menu, two toolbars, the sidebar, and a status bar at the bottom. However, the LibreOffice developers don't think that end users should be forced to adopt any specific interface, so they've packed in plenty of flexibility. For instance, go to View | Toolbars in the menu, and you can see that many extra toolbars can be enabled or disabled.

Even better, go to View | User Interface, where you can choose three options (Figure 2): Standard Toolbar (the default mode), Single Toolbar (useful if you don't have much vertical space on your screen), and Sidebar (a good choice if you're on a super-wide monitor and want to make better use of horizontal space). Try them all out to see which works best for your screen size and workflow.

As mentioned earlier, you can click and grab the arrow handle next to the sidebar to resize it. When you have it open, try mousing over the icons on the right-hand side of the sidebar to show tooltips. The Properties panel in the Sidebar is context-specific; in other words, it changes depending on what you're doing in a document (editing text, manipulating an image, working on a table, etc.). So if you want to perform a specific action or modification to an element in your document, but aren't sure where to go, click the element and then open the Properties panel.

Some additional customization options are available via Tools | Options (on Windows and Linux) or LibreOffice | Preferences on macOS. Open the LibreOffice Writer section on the left, and then explore the various options underneath.

# TOP 10 FEATURES

Here are the 10 most useful things to do in Writer:

- 1. **Headers and Footers:** These are the sections at the start and end of each page, and they mostly stay the same across pages, although you can introduce changing elements. The most common use case for headers and footers is titles. To add text to a header or footer, you first need to create space for them; simply click the mouse in the section above or below the main part of the page, and you’ll see a blue box. Click it to add space for a header or footer, and the blue box will then change into a menu (Figure 3). From here, you can directly add text into the header or footer, which will be consistent across all pages, and add page numbers and page counts (as you’d typically find in the footer in books).

- 2. **Images and Text Boxes:** If you want to use Writer as a desktop-publishing tool, then you’ll want to add floating elements onto the page, such as images and text boxes. You can find most of these under the *Insert* menu.

With images, you’ll be prompted to select an image from your computer, which will then appear on the page. Click and drag to move it around, or use the blue handles on the edges and corners to resize it.

If you want to change how the image interacts with the surrounding text, right-click on it and select the Wrap menu. *Optimal page wrap* is the best choice for images to make the text flow nicely around them.

Also in this right-click context menu, look at Properties for more options, including image borders and exact positioning.

Text boxes work in a similar way: Click *Insert | Text box*, and then click and drag to position it on the page. From there, you can use the handles to drag it around and click inside to add text.

- 3. **Change Tracking:** This is an essential feature if you’re collaborating on a document with others. First, go to *Tools | Options* (or on macOS, *LibreOffice | Preferences*). Under *LibreOffice | User Data*, you can enter your name – this isn’t mandatory, but it means that your name will be shown alongside changes and comments. This helps other people working on the document to see exactly who changed what. Click OK, and then under the *Edit* menu, go to the *Track Changes* submenu and make sure that *Record and Show* are both enabled.

Now try typing; you’ll see that your updates to the document are marked with colors and underlines. Mouse over a change and a tooltip appears, providing information on who made the change and at what time. If someone sends you a document, and you want to give some constructive feedback, detailing exactly which changes you made, now you know what to do!

- 4. **Comments:** Related to the previous feature, this option is important for collaboration. Place the cursor inside the line or paragraph where you want to leave a comment, and then click *Insert | Comment*. A box will appear on the right-hand side of the document (Figure 4), in which you can add some comment text. Your name (and the time you made the comment) appears as well. Also, click the drop-down icon in the comment box for some extra options (e.g., deleting the comment).

- 5. **Spell Checking:** Depending on your operating system setup, this may be enabled already, but if not, click *Tools | Automatic Spell Checking.* As you’d expect from any modern word processor, misspelled text is underlined in red. If you’re working with some words that aren’t part of the standard dictionary, but you don’t want to keep seeing a "this is wrong" red underline for them, just right-click on the word and add it to the dictionary (or ignore it).

If you’ve installed multiple dictionaries from the LibreOffice website and want to change the one that is being used for the spell checking, click on the language in the status bar at the bottom. (You can also change on a per-paragraph level in *Tools | Language.*) For further language options, go to *Tools | Options, Language Settings and Languages* where you can set the default language for all documents.

- 6. **Page Layout:** You’ve already seen the basic formatting options in the toolbar (font, size, bold/italics/underline, colors, etc.), but for broader page options, go to *Format | Page* in the menu. Here you can switch between portrait and landscape mode, choose different page sizes, adjust margins and borders, or even provide background gradients or images under the *Area* tab.

- 7. **Navigator:** As your documents increase in complexity, with multiple elements, styles, headings, and so on, you might find them more difficult to navigate. Fortunately, you can use the handily-named Navigator to help here: click *View | Navigator* to enable it, or find it in the sidebar (Figure 5). Click the arrows next to the different elements to expand them.
