# THE EVOLVING SPREADSHEET

When spreadsheets started being used in the early 1980s, function was everything. Even formatting touches like text wrap were not included, which meant that long pieces of content obscured adjacent cells, often making a spreadsheet unreadable at a glance.

Today, spreadsheets are as functional as ever, although the number of features has increased slowly in the last couple of decades. Where spreadsheets have really evolved is in the rise of formatting options. Thanks to the influence of information designers such as Edward Tufte, spreadsheets have also started emphasizing the importance of presentation, highlighting and coloring cells to create visual impressions and guides on top of the functionality.

These visual impressions are not an end in themselves. Should you find yourself treating your spreadsheets as works of art rather than as aids to comprehension, you will need to reassess your priorities. However, at their best, the combination of functionality and visual aids makes spreadsheets more accessible than they were 40 years ago. With its use of styles, Calc is well-equipped to meet these increasing demands for spreadsheets.

Presentation software dates back to the early 1980s, with tools such as VCN ExecuVision, which let users add images to text and display them in a slideshow format. LibreOffice’s presentation component is called Impress, and it’s an immensely versatile tool with features and special effects galore. So let’s dive into it!

Start LibreOffice and choose a new *Presentation* (or if you’re already running the app, go to *File | New | Text Presentation* in the menu). You’ll be presented with a list of preinstalled templates, so choose one you like, and a new Impress document will open displaying the following features:

# THE ART OF THE PRESENTATION

Regardless of your opinion of Steve Jobs, there’s no doubt that he was excellent at presenting new Apple products. If you’ve never seen any of his presentations before, search onto YouTube and do some searching – the original iPhone and MacBook Air are two classic examples. Jobs’ presentations weren’t packed with text and excessive detail, but instead showed individual features, images of the devices, and occasional specs. He elaborated on the slides with his words, and it all came together into an impressive presentation.

Of course, Jobs’ presentation style isn’t appropriate in every situation. Demonstrating flashy new gizmos to salivating tech journalists is very different from updating your boss on sales or showing some holiday photos to family and friends (see the “Creating a Photo Album” box). But here are some general tips to always keep in mind:

- **Get off to a quick start.** No one likes a long-winded presentation. Instead of starting with “In this presentation I’m going to...”, begin immediately with an interesting fact, stat, or discovery. This catches your audience’s attention straight away and gives them hope that the rest of the presentation will be interesting.
- **Keep your focus on the audience.** It’s easy to keep turning around to look at your slides, but that only encourages you to read or describe what’s on the screen, which adds nothing to the presentation. Look at your audience, and get a feel for how they are responding.
- **Add anecdotes and stories.** If you’ve ever seen a TED talk, you’ll know that they’re full of anecdotes. Sometimes this can seem a bit cheesy, so don’t overdo it, but one or two humorous digressions in a presentation help to break it up – and wake up anyone who is about to nod off!
- **Use the “10-20-30” rule.** Also from an Apple employee, this basically says that presentations should generally be no longer than 10 slides, 20 minutes in length, and use a font size of 30pt or more.
- **Replace text with talk.** If your slide has lots of slides full of text, try reducing them to some key points or images, and then tell your audience the rest. You’re there, giving the presentation, so make sure you’re adding value!

# CREATING A NEW PRESENTATION

So let’s go ahead and make a presentation! For general tips on how to engage your audience and shape your presentations for maximum effectiveness, see the box “The Art of Presenting.” For now, focus on the software itself.

Most of the preinstalled templates include two or more variants of a design. For instance, in the Allianz template that I chose in Figure 1, you can see two slides were created automatically, on the left-hand side: The first is suitable for an introduction (or ending), whereas the second is for regular slides with text and images.

So, add some text to the first slide, then select the second slide from the left-hand panel, and add some text (and maybe an image) as well. Before you do any more work, check out your super quick two-slide presentation! Hit F5 (or go to Slide Show | Start) and you’ll see how it works. With the first slide displayed, click anywhere on the screen – or press the Space bar – to move on to the second slide. After that, the presentation ends. If you accidentally interrupt a presentation by pressing the Esc key, you can resume it from the current slide with Shift + F5 (or *Slide Show | Start from Current Slide*).

To change the layout of a slide, go to the sidebar, click on the *Properties* “deck” (i.e., section – choose by using the buttons on the right-hand side), and then scroll down to *Layouts*. You’ll see a set of alternative designs, as shown in Figure 3.

To add additional text boxes, go to *Insert | Text Box* or press F2. You can then click and drag to determine the exact position and size of the text box. Formatting text inside these boxes is very straightforward – just use the sidebar’s *Properties* deck, and then the *Character* and *Paragraph* decks (if you can’t see the decks, click the + parts to change them to +, which makes them visible). Most of the options there are the same as in Writer, so you can just carry over your knowledge!
