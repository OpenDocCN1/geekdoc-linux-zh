## Hour 3
### Security Is Job 1
Security is so much an integral part of Linux and all UNIX operating systems that its built-in system of file access and permissions forms the basis for controlling every file in the system. The UNIX operating systems see everything as a file, and so security has a material part in every function and every action taken within the system.

The inner workings of the security system are covered throughout the book. Security is an all-pervading issue and you will be visiting it quite often. As you work with Linux, keep in mind that prevention is the name of the game where security is concerned.

In this hour, you will
- Maintain the physical security of your computer equipment, software programs, and data
- Restrict access to the system by assigning passwords, and perform system administration tasks from the superuser account
- Create user and group accounts to share files, and assign permissions to shared files
- Keep track of system usage, and schedule regular system and file maintenance

### Locking Up the System
Certainly one of the more obvious means of securing your equipment and data is just to lock them up in a location to which only you have the key. This might seem like an oversimplification of the problem, but in reality, maintaining the physical security of your equipment is very important. The investment in computers and peripherals can be quite significant for a home office or small business. Furthermore, this doesn't take into account the cost of the software or the value of your data.

Where and how the computer will be used are the determining factors in deciding how it should be protected. This might vary so much that it runs the gamut from doing nothing for a game computer you have at home, to permanently super-gluing the computer you make available for customer use right to the desk it's sitting on. You will have to decide where you fit. After you do that, you can determine what you need to do to physically secure your equipment in a way that meets the needs of your particular situation.

### Creating Backup and Disaster Plans
No one wants to turn on the computer in the morning only to find corrupted programs and missing data. It could be an uncomfortable situation if you find the big report for the big boss (you know, the report that you were so happy about because you had just gotten it finished on time) all scrambled into gibberish or just plain missing. And, it could get worse.

Since the advent of the computer age, computer users have searched for some protection from “The Case of the Missing File.” No one appreciates this kind of surprise. What you could do to recover from a disastrous loss like this would depend entirely on what assets you had put safely aside that could be brought back to restore (to whatever extent) what was lost.

### Backing Up the Filesystem
You might have experienced, or at least seen someone else experience, some major loss of computing capability and important data. These accidents have myriad causes—everything from unexpected hardware failures to inadvertent mistaken deletes. Looking back at any experience with good hindsight can make a good teaching tool, but you need foresight to keep yourself prepared for most disasters. No one can predict the future, but you can devise a plan, a strategy for dealing with it in sensible ways.

Planning and implementing good backup practices can take a lot of the worry out of managing a multi-user system. It can also provide a near-foolproof method for seeing
that at least some of your data (everything up to the last scheduled backup) is not lost. Obviously, the sooner you develop such a plan and get it in motion the safer your situation becomes.

### Keeping Files Secure
Anyone at all familiar with computers and their use knows that occasionally files just simply get lost. There is a long list of reasons why this might happen: accidental deletes, hardware failures, pilot error, software bugs, termites, cockroaches, and mysterious malicious alien hackers to name a few. But there is some relief. Tools are built into the security system that can help you deal with trouble. These tools can be used to protect your files from all the threats we just listed and many more.

### Planning for Fire and Flood
The first consideration for any disaster management and recovery plan is to decide what you can do to prevent every possible realistic threat to your system. Obviously, there are some broad differences between systems and situations that require special consideration. It is a truism that the more secure you make something, the more inconvenient it becomes to use. With that in mind, you can see that a very important part of this planning exercise is determining just how far you, and your users, will go in tolerating the inconveniences imposed by security measures taken to ensure what you want.

Plan for the worst: it might happen anyway. When you are deciding what to do about backups, you might try imagining what you would do if the building burned down overnight. Picture yourself having to get the business back up and running without anything but the data you backed up and stuck in the fireproof safe the night before. Think about it that way and see whether that doesn't stimulate some good ideas for you.

### Task: Creating a Security Checklist
Because so many people depend on a computer to run their business and personal affairs, it is important to think about the computer's security and safety. Here are a few questions you need to answer to help you start thinking about a good sound security plan:
- 1. Get out your Linux diary and start a section called "Security Plan." You'll use this section to answer a few basic questions that will get you started toward keeping your computer and data safe.
- 2. Is the computer located in a safe place? Make sure that the computer is safe from environmental hazards such as dust, heat, and humidity.
- 3. Is the computer available to anyone who walks by? You might not want everyone to use the computer. If this is the case, put the computer where only authorized users have access to it.
- 4. Does your homeowner/renter/business insurance policy cover the replacement of your computer? You should also find out whether the insurance policy would reimburse you for any lost time due to the disappearance of the machine.
- 5. Who will be using the computer? Each person who will be using the Linux system needs to be assigned a user account. Make a list of those people who need access to the machine.
- 6. Will any of the system users need to share files? If so, decide who needs to share files with whom and how each person will need to use the files.
- 7. Do you have a system for backing up important files? This is a definite "must do" item. Many work hours, dollars, and clients have been lost because files have been accidentally erased or a system crashed.

Remember that this checklist is just a beginning. As you progress through this hour and the hours that follow, think about your security plan and add new ideas along the way.

### Keeping the Superuser Safe
The root account belongs to the system superuser, which is also the system administrator. The person logged in to the root account has access to the entire system, including the accounts of other users on the system. A hacker breaking into your system really wants root access to be able to do whatever he wants. The necessity of keeping the root account sacrosanct from unauthorized persons and users who perhaps blunder in by accident cannot be overstated. If the root account is compromised, the whole system can be lost.

One rule of thumb that you should always follow is to use the superuser account only when performing system administration tasks. System administration tasks include making system configuration changes, such as adding new hardware or adding users to the system.
