
frequency (i.e., a nonzero value of α) exists such that A_x^α(τ) ≠ 0. Equivalently, the cyclic spectral density has peaks at cyclic frequencies that are multiples of 1/T0, the fundamental frequency of the received signal.

Notice how this technique is critically dependent on the assumption that the period T0 is known a priori. If this assumption is not valid, then cyclostationarity-based detection requires either the search for, or the extraction of, the cyclic frequencies, which entails a considerable increase of its complexity. This technique can also be used to discriminate among different types of transmission and of primary users. Checks for the presence of cycles in CAF and CSD can be done by performing an exhaustive search over candidate cycles, which are those yielding a statistically significant nonzero cyclic statistics. To check if A_x^α(τ) is zero for a given candidate cycle α, the following estimator can be used:

$$\hat{A}_x^\alpha(\tau)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}x_{m+\tau}x_m^*e^{-j2\pi\alpha\tau}$$ (4.41)

$$=A_x^\alpha(\tau)+\varepsilon_x^\alpha(\tau),$$ (4.42)

where ε_x^α(τ) denotes the estimation error. If the estimator is consistent, this error vanishes asymptotically as the observation length tends to infinity. In practice, due to the finite observation length, A_x^α(τ) ≠ 0, and we must discriminate between the two hypotheses A_x^α(τ)=ε_x^α(τ) (no primary signal) and A_x^α(τ)=A_x^α(τ)+ε_x^α(τ). This can be done by choosing a set {τ1,…,τN} and transforming the autocorrelation functions into N-vectors. An asymptotic analysis of the detector performance is contained in [8].

A possible (frequency-domain) test statistic for the detector at a given value of α (the “single-cycle” cyclostationarity detector for the cyclic feature of interest) is

$$Y=\int_{-f_s/2}^{f_s/2}S_y^\alpha(f)[S_y^\alpha(f)]^*df,$$ (4.43)

where f_s is the sampling frequency, and S_y^α(f) is an estimate of the spectral correlation function of the received signal.

## 4.3.4 Autocorrelation-based detection

This detection technique assumes that the primary signal is wide-sense stationary, and exploits its autocorrelation to discriminate it from white noise. Let the sampled observed signal have the form

$$y_n=x_n+w_n,\quad 0\le n\le N-1.$$ (4.44)

Then the L × L sample autocorrelation matrix of {y_n} is given by

$$\hat{\mathbf{R}}_y=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=L-1}^{N-1}\mathbf{y}_n\mathbf{y}_n^{\dagger},$$ (4.45)

where $y_n$ is the column vector

$$\mathbf{y}_n = (y_n, y_{n-1}, \dots, y_{n-L+1})^{\mathsf{T}}.\tag{4.46}$$

As $N \to \infty$, under the assumption that the additive noise is white and the signal is uncorrelated with noise, $\hat{\mathbf{A}}_y$ converges in probability to the autocorrelation of $(y_n)$:

$$\mathbf{A}_y \triangleq \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_n \mathbf{y}_n^\dagger] = \mathbf{A}_x + \sigma_w^2 \mathbf{I}_L,\tag{4.47}$$

where $\mathbf{A}_x$ is the autocorrelation matrix of the column vector

$$\mathbf{x}_n = (x_n, x_{n-1}, \dots, x_{n-L+1})^{\mathsf{T}},\tag{4.48}$$

$\sigma_w^2$ is the noise average power, as usual, and $\mathbf{I}_L$ denotes the $L \times L$ identity matrix. If there is no primary signal, then $\mathbf{A}_y$ equals the scalar matrix $\sigma_w^2 \mathbf{I}_L$, which has zero off-diagonal elements and equal eigenvalues $\sigma_w^2$. Thus, the presence of a primary signal can be detected when one of these properties does not hold, as detailed below. Notice that the tests based on autocorrelation use only quantities associated with the observed signal, so that no information on the transmitted signal and the transmission channel are needed.

### Dealing with nonwhite noise

In practice, the assumption that the noise is white is made invalid by the presence of a receiving filter. In this situation, consider the discrete filter impulse response $(h_n)_{n=0}^K$, normalized so that

$$\sum_{k=0}^K |h_k|^2 = 1.\tag{4.49}$$

After filtering, the received signal has the form

$$\tilde{y}_n = \sum_{k=0}^K h_k y_{n-k}.\tag{4.50}$$

Defining, similarly,

$$\tilde{x}_n \triangleq \sum_{k=0}^K h_k x_{n-k},\tag{4.51}$$

$$\tilde{w}_n \triangleq \sum_{k=0}^K h_k w_{n-k},\tag{4.52}$$

we obtain $\tilde{y}_n = \tilde{x}_n + \tilde{w}_n$. Defining further the vectors

$$\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_n \triangleq (\tilde{y}_n, \tilde{y}_{n-1}, \dots, \tilde{y}_{n-L+1})^{\mathsf{T}},\tag{4.53}$$

$$\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_n \triangleq (\tilde{x}_n, \tilde{x}_{n-1}, \dots, \tilde{x}_{n-L+1})^{\mathsf{T}},\tag{4.54}$$

$$\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_n \triangleq (\tilde{w}_n, \tilde{w}_{n-1}, \dots, \tilde{w}_{n-L+1})^{\mathsf{T}},\tag{4.55}$$

the autocorrelation matrices

A_y ≜ E[y_n y_n^†], (4.56)
A_x ≜ E[x_n x_n^†], (4.57)
A_w ≜ E[w_n w_n^†], (4.58)

and the L × (L + K) "filtering matrix"
H = [ h0 h1 … hK 0 … 0 ; 0 h0 … hK-1 hK … 0 ; … ; 0 0 … h0 h1 … hK ], (4.59)

we obtain, under the usual assumption of uncorrelated signal and noise,
A_y = A_x + A_w, (4.60)

where
A_w = σ_w^2 G, (4.61)

with G the positive definite Hermitian matrix
G ≜ H H^†. (4.62)

With the new definitions
A_y ≜ G^{-1/2} A_y G^{-1/2}, (4.63)
A_x ≜ G^{-1/2} A_x G^{-1/2}, (4.64)

we obtain
A_y = A_x + σ_w^2 I_L (4.65)
as in (4.47).

### Eigenvalue-ratio test

This technique was introduced in [53, 54]. Denoting by ρ_min and ρ_max the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of A_x, and by μ_min and μ_max the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of A_y, respectively, we have
μ_max = ρ_max + σ_w^2 and μ_min = ρ_min + σ_w^2, (4.66)

so that
μ_max / μ_min = 1 (4.67)
when there is no primary signal, and
μ_max / μ_min > 1 (4.68)

otherwise. Thus, a sensible decision test can be based on the ratio \hat{\mu}_{max}/\hat{\mu}_{min}, where \hat{\mu}_{min} and \hat{\mu}_{max} are the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of the sample covariance matrix \hat{A}_y.

### Maximum-eigenvalue test

Consider again (4.47). If there is no primary signal in (y_m), then A_x = 0, otherwise A_x ≠ 0. If μ_max and ρ_max denote, as usual, the maximum eigenvalues of A_y and A_x, respectively, then μ_max = ρ_max + σ_w^2, with ρ_max = 0 if and only if A_x = 0. This can also be expressed by saying that, if the primary signal is present, then μ_max > σ_w^2, while without a signal μ_max = σ_w^2. Based on this observation, the maximum eigenvalue can be used as a test of the presence of the primary signal [51].

### Energy/minimum-eigenvalue ratio test

This test [54] compares the energy of the observed signal (y_n) with the minimum eigenvalue of A_y. If their ratio exceeds a given threshold, then the primary signal is assumed to be present.

### Diagonal/off-diagonal ratio test

To quantify the terms lying outside of the main diagonal in the correlation matrix A_y = (r_{n,m})_{n,m=1}^L, we may define the quantities

U_d^α ≜ 1/L Σ_{n=1}^L Σ_{m=1}^L |r_{n,m}|^α,  (4.69)
U_o^α ≜ 1/L Σ_{n=1}^L Σ_{m=1}^L |r_{n,m}|^α,  (4.70)

with α = 1, 2. With no signal, we obtain U_o^α = U_d^α, while with signal U_o^α > U_d^α. The detection tests consist of comparing the ratio U_o^α / U_d^α with a suitable threshold [52].

## 4.4 An application: spectrum sensing with OFDM

As an application, we examine here the special case of spectrum sensing with orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) [18, Chapter 12], a scheme used in a number of current and future commercial communication systems (WiFi, WiMAX, LTE, and DVB-T). OFDM signals are cyclostationary, and hence spectrum sensors exploiting cyclostationarity can be used. However, better sensors can be designed that are tailored to the specific signal structure of OFDM, and, in particular, the presence in it of a cyclic prefix (CP) of known length. This duplicates the last information data at the beginning of an OFDM symbol, and hence generates a correlation in the symbol stream. We study, following [1], coherent and noncoherent detection.

Let d_i denote the N_D-vector of data associated with the i th OFDM symbol x_i. The latter is obtained by concatenating to d_i the last N_P elements of d_i. In vector form,

x_i = U d_i, (4.71)

where U is the (N_P + N_D) × N_D matrix

U ≜ [ 0_{N_P × (N_D - N_P)} I_{N_P} ; I_{N_D} ] . (4.72)

T ≜ diag[ U U … U ] . (4.73) K+1

The vector x consisting of K + 1 OFDM symbols is obtained by removing the first τ and the last (N_P + N_D) − τ samples from T d. Thus, we can write

x = T_τ d, (4.74)

where d ≜ [d_1^T d_2^T … d_{K+1}^T]^T, and T_τ is the K(N_P + N_D) × (K + 1)N_D matrix obtained by deleting the first τ and the last (N_P + N_D)−τ rows of T.

### 4.4.1 Neyman–Pearson detection

Invoking the central limit theorem, we assume the data vector d to be white Gaussian, i.e., d ∼ N_c(0, σ_d^2 I), where σ_d^2 is the variance of the complex signal samples. The conditional autocorrelation of x is

E[ x x^T | τ ] = E[ T_τ d d^T T_τ^T ] = σ_d^2 T_τ T_τ^T, (4.75)

so that x | τ ∼ N_c(0, σ_d^2 T_τ T_τ^T). We consider separately the cases of known and unknown values of σ_d^2 and σ_n^2.
