τ = 0, 1/W, …, m/W, …, with the power at τ = m/W being the mean-square value of c_m(t) in (3.40).

Another alternative for the case of many MPCs per delay bin is to treat the PDP as smooth, continuous function of delay, implying a continuum of echoes along the delay axis, see [7]. We shall invoke that representation here, where the value of P(τ) at any given τ is the sum of the powers of the MPCs arriving in a delay bin of width 1/W about that τ; and the normalization is such that the area under the integral over P(τ) is unity.

A very popular model for the PDP shape is the one-sided exponential,

P(τ) = (1/τ₀) exp(−τ/τ₀); 0 ≤ τ < ∞, (3.43)

where τ₀ is the decay time constant [56], and we note that the area under P(τ) is 1. The popularity of this shape stems from both its simplicity and its corroboration by many measurement campaigns conducted over the years, e.g., [16, 28]. Several other measurements have found more complex PDPs that consist of several clusters, e.g., [95].

The rms delay spread (for convenience henceforth simply called 'delay spread,' DS) is the second central moment of the PDP, i.e.,

τ_rms = √[∫₀^∞ P(τ) τ² dτ − (∫₀^∞ P(τ) τ dτ)²], (3.44)

Many papers report the measured delay spread (and not complete PDPs) to characterize the delay dispersion. For the decaying exponential PDP shape in (3.43), τ_rms is found from (3.44) to be precisely τ₀.

# 3.8.5 The frequency correlation function, F(Δf)

The Fourier transform of ĥ(t, τ) in the delay variable τ, with t fixed, can be recognized as the (normalized, complex) channel frequency response at time t, which we denote as H(f, t). In Section 3.6, where we assumed no time variation and a single frequency f_c, we denoted this function as Ĥ(f_c; x). (As above, we suppress here the use of x to designate the TX-RX spatial coordinates.) In Section 3.7.3, for the mobile case, we introduced an autocorrelation function, A(Δt), to describe the correlation between two complex time samples of H where f is fixed and the time samples are separated by Δt. Here, we consider the parallel case: t is fixed and we seek the correlation F(Δf) between two complex f-samples of Ĥ separated by Δf. Under conditions that generally apply in wideband wireless channels, this frequency correlation function (FCF) can be shown to be nothing other than the Fourier transform of P(τ). If fading is highly correlated over an interval of width B Hz (i.e., |F(Δf)| ≈ 1 for Δf up to B), and a deep fade from a primary TX to a cognitive radio RX is detected at some frequency f₀, then the fade can be assumed to occupy a band of approximate width B about f₀. Thus, an agile cognitive radio TX can send signals to that RX over that band without severe interference from the primary TX.
