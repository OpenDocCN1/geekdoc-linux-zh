This effect can also be visualized as follows. The channel gain for each MPC can be represented by a complex vector. The combining of the MPCs at the receiving antenna is then equivalent to the addition of complex vectors. As the location of the receiver changes, the phase shift of each MPC changes, namely by (2π/λ)d cos(φ), where φ is the angle between the direction of the arrival of the MPC and the direction of movement. This fact further implies that the relative phases among the MPCs change, since they are arriving at the receiver from different directions. Thus, MPCs that interfere destructively at one location can interfere constructively at a different location. This variation over space is what we call either small-scale fading or multipath fading, as discussed in Section 3.3.

If the net field, i.e., the superposition of waves, were sampled at many points within a radius of, say, 10λ, we would obtain a population of many (complex) samples. These can be interpreted as a statistical ensemble, whose values are describable using a probability density function (pdf). The most common and widely understood situation is the one where all the multipath components are of similar magnitude so that, in the limit of a great many components, their sum has a variation over space that resembles a complex Gaussian process. It has been shown that the phase of a complex Gaussian random variable (rv) has a uniform pdf over (−π, π]; and the magnitude r has a Rayleigh pdf [56, 76], i.e.,

$$p_r(r) = \frac{2r}{\bar{P}_r} \cdot \exp\left[ -\frac{r^2}{\bar{P}_r} \right], \qquad r \ge 0, \tag{3.17}$$

where $\bar{P}_r$ is the second moment of the distribution, i.e., the statistical average of $r^2$. In reality, the received power $P_r = r^2$ has an average value defined by its local spatial average $\langle P_r \rangle$. Thus, to the extent that (3.17) accurately models the distribution of $r$, the statistical average, $\bar{P}_r$, and the local spatial average $\langle P_r \rangle$ can be regarded as virtually the same. This case is referred to as Rayleigh fading.
