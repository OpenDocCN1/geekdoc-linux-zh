You have a choice of several tools to reduce the size of your DOS partition. Which one you choose depends on how much you value the existing data on your DOS disk, and whether or not you're a gambler at heart.

The FDISK command, which comes with DOS, is the official disk partitioning tool. But since it can't change the size of a partition, it makes you delete your existing partition before it will re-create it as a smaller one. Unfortunately, this means you lose all the data on the disk, so you'll need to perform a full backup and restore operation (along with reinstalling DOS) if you use FDISK. FDISK is a nuisance, but it works.

If you're willing to spend a bit of money, you can purchase the Partition Magic software from Power Quest (www.powerquest.com). Partition Magic can safely resize an existing DOS partition without data loss, and is very easy to use.

Another option, FIPS (a freeware utility program you'll find in the DOSUTILS directory on the CD), can resize an existing partition without deleting it, but it's a "use at your own risk" program. Have a look at the FIPS documentation and give it a try if you feel comfortable with it. Hundreds of thousands of people have used FIPS, which has been around for several years, and it's said to be very reliable, but don't call me if you choose to use FIPS and something screws up.

Regardless of the utility you choose, the amount of space you leave for your Linux partitions will depend on the size of your hard disk, how much space is currently occupied, and how you intend to use Linux. I recommend that you devote 600MB or more to Linux.

NOTE: The installation outlined in this chapter requires about 800MB of hard disk space and is the basis of future chapters, so I recommend you follow it pretty closely. If you don't have that much space available, you can omit certain components with a Custom Install, but that's recommended only for users already familiar with Linux.

In the example that follows, we'll start with a 3.2GB (3,200MB) hard disk, reduce the DOS partition to 1,600MB, and use the remaining 1,600MB for Linux.
