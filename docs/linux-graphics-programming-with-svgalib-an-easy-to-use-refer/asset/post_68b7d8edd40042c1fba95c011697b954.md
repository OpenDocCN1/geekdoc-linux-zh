## Conclusion

The nonrectangular polygon is one element of SVGAlib that's largely missing. The purpose of this chapter is therefore, to help you circumvent this absence. As you'll learn, polygons are a key element in the construction of three-dimensional images, and as such, they're highly useful.

In general, I'd recommend using the triangle functions in threeDKit over some of my earlier examples—the speed and simplicity can't be beat. But if you enjoy tinkering, there's nothing wrong with doing it from scratch either. SVGAlib programming is still regarded as a "dark art" (although hopefully this book will help alleviate that), and I imagine it'll be quite some time before the "rules" are set. ## Chapter 12
3D Images

To be taken seriously in today's video-centric world, the visual programmer needs to be proficient in 3D graphics. Simply put, there's no other way to go. One could be well versed—yes, even commercially successful—in several other visual disciplines, but this is the one field that separates the men from the boys (figuratively speaking, of course).

New media companies such as Pixar get all the press, and that's not likely to change anytime soon. Although it's true that anything involving Steve Jobs (a founder of Pixar) is based on a certain amount of hype, there's an element of prophecy in what's being done. 2D is out of fashion, and 3D is in. This development is a bit unfair to those who are skilled in "traditional" computer graphics—a field that still has plenty of valid applications—but it also stands as an opportunity to excel in a genre that hasn't yet seen all its players.

Hollywood has long since used computer-generated graphic effects to wow audiences. Tron was an early example, and some productions—the movie Toy Story comes to mind—are now being made entirely with 3D animation. This futuristic trend isn't restricted to the cinema, either—just turn on the tube for a demonstration. Modern television's repertoire is chock-full of dazzling 3D displays. The news, weather, and even sports have all sorts of electronic wizardry designed to jazz up the dull spots. Flashy visuals have progressed to the point where the same old stuff shown night after night can be made to appear fresh and exciting. Even a genre as trivial as commercials now relies heavily on 3D techniques.

So, how do we do this 3D thing? Of course, no image on a flat computer display can truly be “3D.” Nothing’s going to jump out of the screen at you, and you won’t need to wear special glasses. The term 3D, when applied to computer graphics, simply means that objects appear to have depth. In other words, through precise mathematical calculations, images are given shading and other characteristics that suggest depth and volume.

The key to all of this is proceduralism. Objects created in this manner (as opposed to the “hacked,” pseudo-3D sphere in Chapter 6) can often be rotated and moved by means of algorithmic methods alone. This is because true 3D images are the products of equations, and not merely static (that is, flat) line drawings (such as those an artist might create with packages such as Illustrator or Photoshop).

For instance, you can’t see the backside of an image created with a drawing program because, simply enough, there is no backside. But a 3D object—a true one—can be rotated and viewed from any angle.

### Four Types Of 3D Objects

There are four types of computer-generated 3D images, each one having its own unique advantages and drawbacks. In increasing order of complexity, these models include wire-frames, filled polygons, texture-mapped polygons, and the most beautiful of them all: simulated ray casts, or ray traces.

### Wire Frames

As the name suggests, a *wire frame* appears as though it’s been modeled from wire. This type of image is created when lines are drawn between a series of vertices plotted in a 3D space. You’ve probably seen this type of image before—see Figure 12.1 for an example of how it looks.

### Filled Polygons

Moving up from the wire frame, an image comprised of *filled polygons* is created by using the techniques suggested in Chapter 11. Instead of using bare lines as in the previous example, a number of solid triangles or other polygons appear everywhere there’s a surface,

![](img/2e475714ef8969ed272622c46473d334_262_0.png)

**Figure 12.1**
A sample wire frame model.

resulting in a filled image. This looks much better than the wire frame, and it's fast enough to allow rendering in real time.

### *Texture-Mapped Polygons*

Expanding on the filled polygon concept, a *texture-mapped polygon* is created by rotating and scaling a bitmap drawing (the texture), which is then placed on a 3D polygonal image. This is the basis for most modern 3D games.

### *Raytracing*

In this technique, simulated beams of light are radiated from an illumination source outside the screen, from which point they sail forth to penetrate any objects in their path. As the rays bounce, shades of color are created. Special attention is given to the material of the objects being struck, taking into account any transparency or translucency. This is the most realistic of the four techniques, as shadows and shading are made possible. Unfortunately, it's also rather time consuming, and thus too processor-intensive to be used in real time applications. See Figure 12.2 for an example.

A point in a polygon that comprises the intersection of two or more lines. Vertices also appear in angles, polyhedrons, and graphs.
