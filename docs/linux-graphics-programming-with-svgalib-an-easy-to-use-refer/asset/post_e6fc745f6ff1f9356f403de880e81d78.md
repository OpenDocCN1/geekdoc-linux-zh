
You might be wondering how I knew what the RGB values were for the 16 primary VGA colors. What I did was somewhat primitive, but effective. First, I drew 16 colors with `vga_drawpixel()`. Next, I set the RGB palette with `gl_setrgbpalette()` and then I read the color values of the 16 pixels I had drawn with `gl_getpixlergb()`. Hey, it works. You can use this same procedure to deduce the RGB values for 256-color GIFs as well.

With that, `ScreenShot.c` is wrapped up. I’ve had some difficulties using it with highly-colored images, but this might be resolvable by using `gl_rgbcolor()` to define all your screen colors to match your `GIF_setcolor()` colors.

Of course, you might choose not to bother with gifsave at all. I included gifsave for versatility, but some of you might have deduced that you can replace all of it with two commands: vga1’s `gl_getbox()` and liblug’s `write_gif_file()`.

## Conclusion

Graphic files can save you a lot of time. This applies both to programming *and* to program execution (if you use a complicated image). They allow you to share your work with others, who might be on a different operating system. This will be especially relevant when you get to the chapter on creating a paint program.

Although SVGAlib has no provision for reading and writing from graphic files, knowing the specifications of the BMP file and having libraries such as liblug can make the task much easier. Don’t you agree?

## What Is Computer Animation?

Similar to animated cartooning, computer animation is the art of fooling the eye into believing that fluid movement is taking place. Whereas real-world movement is continuous and smooth (analog), computers can only display still images (digital). Therefore, the secret of animation is to display a steady stream of still images. By this method, one can create the illusion of conjoined motion. In this respect, computer animation is similar to a cinematic presentation.

Despite the obvious parallels with conventional media, however, computer animation still requires a certain amount of effort to achieve realism. Although advanced techniques do exist, most arcade-style animation is awkward and choppy. As a result, moving images often appear to flicker as they cross the screen. It’s best to avoid this if you can, so I’ll include some tips along the way to help you.

## Animation Standards

You may know that cartoons are based on the "frames per second" standard. Animation in the cinema generally uses 24 frames per second, and, accordingly, this means that for every second that passes, you'll view 24 images in rapid succession. These images are still (i.e., motionless) and are very similar to one another. Only by viewing them quickly and in sequence does one get the illusion of motion.

Computer animation doesn't have a standard such as 24 frames per second. Matters are further complicated by the fact that different processors run at different speeds. Animation that appears normal and lifelike on a 486 will be much too fast on the latest Pentium and far too slow on a 386. Therefore, programmers must take this and many other factors into account when designing games and business applications that require animated graphical output.

One way to take the “pulse” of an individual computer is to measure its video refresh rate. Considering that most screens refresh at around 70Hz, relatively smooth animation can be achieved by drawing “between cycles.” In other words, for optimal performance, you'll want to draw immediately after one refresh, as the video beam is returning to the top of the screen. This can be achieved with `vga_waitrefresh(void)`. However, there's one possible snafu. Because Linux is generally used as a server OS, there may be more going on than just your SVGAlib program. For example, your processor may be handling mail delivery, serving Web pages, and even compiling other programs while your app is running. Therefore, your drawing may be delayed some (even as little as a fraction of a second), possibly negating the positive effect of `vga_waitrefresh()`. However, don't lose hope; if you can draw at least one “frame” for every two refresh cycles, you should still be okay. See `/etc/vga/libvga.config` for more on refresh rates. (Alternatively, you can consult Chapter 2.)
