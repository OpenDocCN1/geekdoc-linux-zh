In this chapter, you’re going to learn all about two-dimensional animation. 2D drawing is levels of magnitude easier than 3D, and as a result, you’ll be up and running in no time at all. I know three-dimensional rendering may sound more glamorous, as it seems every article published today on animation praises the virtues of ray tracing and other elements of 3D graphics. Further, when you go to your local computer mega store, you can’t buy anything less than a 3D adapter. However, don’t be fooled; 2D animation is alive and well. You can see it all the time in Java and Shockwave—the cutting edge of the Web. And plenty of *fun* games are still produced without the benefit of that illusory third dimension. (One might even continue by saying that 3D animation adds nothing more than the illusion of depth.) So it should come as no surprise that 2D has its place in SVGAlib.

> **Note**
> A PC graphics card that purports to be 3D isn’t radically different than a regular VGA card. “3D” is nothing more than a clever marketing ploy; it simply means that the card can handle high-resolution modes and has more onboard memory and perhaps some internal processing power. Unfortunately, nothing jumps out from the screen at you.

For an easy example of 2D animation, think of your favorite early ’80s video game. Asteroids, Pac-Man, Centipede, and Dig Dug were all 2D. If those games were before your time, think of the animated GIFs on the Web. Although “flat” animation such as this may not be the pinnacle of sophistication, it still provides a nice effect. Further, as with other aspects of SVGAlib, animation is fun. That is why you’re reading this book, right?
