## Foreword

I can’t draw. I love all forms of art, but I can’t draw. I can play guitar decently. I can cook and sew, but I can’t draw—and I’m envious of those who can. Sure, I can drive a golf ball 300 yards (downhill with the wind at my back), but don’t ask me to draw a picture of a cat. The trouble is, I see myself as a creative guy, and I’ve always wanted to be able to express myself in drawings. Big mistake.

So, no big deal right? Except, I’ve also always been a very competitive person, and to fail at something that I wanted to excel in was more than disappointing. It’s not like I haven’t tried. I can remember back to first grade where, happy as a clam, I worked diligently on a drawing for some assignment only to be humiliated by the way mine looked compared to those done by my classmates. And it never got any better. Was it in 6th grade that my art teacher taught us about perspective in drawings? My classmates drew towns, and farms and rail yards. I drew a lopsided house.

However, sometime in 1972, when at the University of Michigan, I took an independent study course in computer graphics. I was a very fortunate young man, because as it turns out, the grad student who taught the course was none other than Jim Blinn. Jim inspired me. He showed me how I could program a computer to draw what I could not draw with my hands. He showed me how to use matrix math to accurately represent a geometric object on a computer screen—and then a group of geometric objects. Then, he showed me how to move those objects around and look at them from different angles. But even more than that, Jim Blinn inspired me to think about ways to use a computer to create art I otherwise lacked the talent to create.

Because it was only one class, I was not able to learn too much more directly from Jim, but that class opened my eyes to what could be done. (Jim later went on to show us all a great deal more about what could be done with computers and art—and happily he's still doing it today. Now I was no longer ashamed of my artistic inadequacies. Rather, I was turned on to a whole new creative medium. Something that had just not existed before was now available to me. What's more, I was able to take advantage of it.

For me, this eventually led to design and development of computer games. In 1972, the term “PC” was meaningless: There was no such thing as a Personal Computer and thankfully, the notion of “political correctness” had not yet reared its ugly head. But now, more than 25 years later, the technology has advanced so far that it has become accessible to many more people.

The book you hold in your hands can open a door into a new medium. Though it has been around for more than 25 years, the medium of computer graphics is still very new. However, there are a few things that you have to learn in order to be able to make inroads into this new “frontier.” After all, you had to learn which end of a paintbrush to hold. And those of you who drew those nice towns and farms after learning about how perspective works in art—yes you—well, you had to learn about vanishing lines and such first too.

So, instead of sharpening your pastel pencils or mixing your paints, fire-up your compilers, assemblers, and IDEs. Dig into this book, roll up your sleeves, and start coding. Hey, compared to working in oils, the cleanup is a breeze.

—Ed Rotberg
Sole Programmer of Atari’s Battlezone
September 30, 1999
