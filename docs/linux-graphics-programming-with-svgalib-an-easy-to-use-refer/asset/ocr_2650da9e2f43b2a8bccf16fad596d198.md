Two steps are necessary to achieve 3D realism: the creation of the objects (that is, the definition of the mathematical and spatial properties of the shapes in question) and the rendering of those images on the screen. The previous chapter examined the first step—the coding of the 3D objects—but was remiss in the subject of rendering. We’ll go over that second step here.

# Rendering 3D Objects

Up until now, examples have relied on the threeDKit’s gl_triangle() function and its adaptation of Gouraud shading to create screen images with the illusion of depth. Perhaps you remember Chapter 6, in which I showed you how to create the look of a three-dimensional sphere with purely two-dimensional techniques (in other words, I cheated). These measures are adequate as a stopgap; they’re fast, and fairly easy on the processor. However, if you’re depending on photorealism, there’s a much better way.

To achieve the subtle variances of color and shadow that one expects in a life-like view, it’s necessary to employ a different technique. What we’ll use is something called raytracing.
