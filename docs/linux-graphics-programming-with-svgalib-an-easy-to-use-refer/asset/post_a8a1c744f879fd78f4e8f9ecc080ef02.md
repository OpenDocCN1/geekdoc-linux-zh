
## Refresh Rate

(Repeated from Chapter 1) The number of times per second your monitor is reenergized. Your screen is constantly losing its charge and must therefore be refreshed. Measured in hertz, display systems typically range from 56Hz to well over 100Hz. A minimum of 70Hz is recommended to help prevent eye strain.

## Video Bandwidth

The maximum display resolution of a video screen, measured in MHz, and calculated by the following formula:

horizontal×vertical×resolution×refreshes/sec

For example, 800×600×60 = 28.8MHz. By comparison, broadcast television is only 3.58MHz. (That said, I usually buy a monitor based on price and how “cool” it looks.)

The actual projection of an image is handled by three electron “guns” located in the back of a monitor’s case. These guns beam a rapid succession of electrons towards the front of the monitor, where they collide with the inside surface of the screen. The screen’s inside surface is covered with phosphors, generally arranged in groups of three (one phosphor for each primary color), and as the electrons strike, the individual phosphors glow. It’s these glowing phosphors that produce the visible display, as illustrated in Figure 2.2. Unfortunately, the phosphors lose their charge very quickly, and as a result they must be recharged (or *restruck*) several times per second. The frequency of this recharging is known as the *refresh rate*, as defined above and in Chapter 1. The faster your screen is refreshed, the better quality your image will have.

This activity takes place inside of a cathode ray tube, or CRT, which is the same as a picture tube inside of an analog TV. You may have heard the term CRT used in reference with the space shuttle’s displays. Despite how techno-cool it sounds, though, CRT is actually an old-fashioned name and has been superseded by the relatively plain-sounding *monitor*, or *display*, which is what we use today.

## Dot

Electron guns focus on *dots* when they shoot. A display has thousands of dots, which are small circles or rectangles on the screen. All video pictures are comprised of dots. Think of them as the *atoms* of computer screens.

## Pixel

*Pixel* is an acronym created from the words *picture element*. It refers to the smallest definable area on your display, and it must consist of only one color. Don’t confuse pixels with dots; dots are a physical part of your monitor, whereas pixels are defined by your video card and may be comprised of several dots. The more pixels your screen contains, the better its *resolution*. Another word for pixel is *pel*.

## Resolution

This is your screen’s sharpness, or clarity. The more pixels a monitor displays, the better its resolution. To get a higher pixel count, and subsequently better resolution, you need to instruct your video card to enter a different screen mode.

## Screen Modes

Screen modes are great fun. By changing screen modes, you can change how many pixels are on the screen at one time. Generally, the more pixels you have, the better the quality of your image. There’s a price to pay, however, in that high pixel counts equal heavy memory usage in your video card.

Screen modes work by dividing your viewing area into a grid, or what’s often referred to as a matrix. The matrix can be compared to a Cartesian graph/coordinate system in mathematics, with an x-axis running horizontally, and a y-axis running vertically. The points in a matrix are expressed in standard (x,y) format, with point (0,0) being in the upper left corner, and subsequent points continuing down and to the right.

## Matrix

The grid of horizontal rows and vertical columns that make up a display screen. For example, a matrix of 640×480 means that you have 640 pixels horizontally and 480 pixels vertically.

You're already familiar with text mode (whether or not you use that term); Linux gives you 80 characters horizontally and 25 characters vertically. To get beyond ASCII characters, you must enter into a graphics mode.

For a theoretical example, we can create a screen mode called 4×3. What this means is that your screen will be divided into a matrix consisting of three horizontal rows intersecting four vertical columns, thereby creating a total of twelve individual pixels. Refer to Figure 2.3 if you're having trouble visioning this.

However, a matrix of these dimensions is virtually useless. Knowing that pixels can only contain one color, we're limited to chunky blocks that each take up a twelfth of the screen. It would take some imagination to draw anything recognizable in *this* graphics mode, as evidenced by Figure 2.4. (Heck, even the Atari 2600 had better resolution!) Therefore, we need to create a larger grid.

In reality, SVGAlib's smallest matrix is 320 pixels wide by 200 pixels high. Look at Figure 2.5 for an example of mode 320×200.

Clearly this gives us more to work with. We actually have a recognizable house (albeit blocky, as it just uses black and white), and it's clear that one could make several primitive drawings in this manner. Furthermore, logic indicates that as the size of the matrix increases (say, to 1,024×768), the number of available pixels increases, and the problem with blocks and jagged edges goes away. Only one more element is needed to spice up this picture: color.

### Color In Screen Modes

Screen modes have a third and final dimension—color. The color element is listed after the width and height dimensions of the grid. For example, Listing 2.1 shows that there are actually six different variations of mode 320x200 under SVGA/lb.

**Listing 2.1 The six SVGA/lb screen modes that use a 320×200 matrix.**

- 320x200x16 colors
- 320x200x256 colors
- 320x200x32K colors
- 320x200x64K colors
- 320x200x16M colors
- 320x200x16M32 colors

Notice how the number of possible colors increases from a paltry 16 up to 16 *million*. Can you name 16 million colors?

> Note
> Incidentally, text mode has a resolution of 720×400 pixels and 16 colors.

## VGA Memory

Unfortunately, not every video card can handle the higher resolution modes. There’s usually a tradeoff between matrix size and the number of possible colors; a card that can hold a huge matrix in memory can’t also hold a large number of colors, unless it has a lot of memory. Let’s have a look at this in Table 2.1.

Obviously, every VGA card can handle mode 640×480×16. In fact, this was the high end of the original IBM VGA adapter. But, if your card has only 4MB of memory, then mode 1,600×1,200×16M is out of the question.

Table 2.1 Memory requirements for VGA cards, expressed in megabytes.

| Resolution | 16 Colors | 256 Colors | 64K Colors | 16M Colors |
|------------|-----------|------------|------------|------------|
| 640×480 | .5 | .5 | 1 | 2 |
| 800×600 | .5 | 1 | 2 | 2 |
| 1,024×768 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 4 |
| 1,280×1,024 | 1 | 2 | 4 | 4 |
| 1,600×1,200 | 2 | 2 | 4 | 8 |

## More on Resolutions

Speaking of resolutions, Table 2.2 lists some of the more common resolutions and their names. Note that although 800×600 is officially known as SVGA, SVGAAlb can actually handle everything listed in the table.

Did you notice how these resolutions seem to be proportionate to one another? That’s no accident. Beginning with mode 640×480, resolutions share the same aspect ratio. That is to say, the horizontal width is a third longer than the height. This enables pictures drawn at one resolution to be transferred to a different resolution without distorting.

Table 2.2 Common resolutions and their names.

| Resolution | Name |
|------------|---------------|
| 640×480 | Standard VGA |
| 800×600 | SVGA |
| 1,024×768 | XGA |
| 1,280×1,024 | SXGA |
| 1,600×1,200 | UXGA |
