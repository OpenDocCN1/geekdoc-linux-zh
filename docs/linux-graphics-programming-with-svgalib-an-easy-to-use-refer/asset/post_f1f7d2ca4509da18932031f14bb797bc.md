## Scalable Fonts

Although SVGAlib has no provision for handling scalable typefaces, there's an organization at www.freetype.org that's successfully developed an engine to interpret TrueType (TTF) character sets. Indeed, this means that you can now view "real" fonts (such as Arial) by way of SVGAlib. The code works much in the same way as the code for SVGAlib's bitmap fonts, although it's considerably more involved.

The Linux version of FreeType was designed for X11 (X Windows), but Matan Ziv-Av has graciously ported the sample/demo programs. The actual "engine" remains the same; it was just the rendering process that needed to be changed.

To begin, download the FreeType package from the official site at www.freetype.org. At the time of this writing, the current version is freetype-1.3.1.tar.gz, although a much-improved version 2 is in the works.

The next step is compilation. Note that you'll have to edit your Makefile if you don't have X11 installed on your system—you'll need to remove all references to the X Windows test programs, or you'll get some compile-time errors.

The resultant libraries (shared and static) are installed into the /usr/local/lib directory, so you'll probably need to run ldconfig afterwards to re-synch your system. By default, ld will look in /lib and /usr/lib.

Next, you can download Matan's FreeType SVGAlib demos from www.svgalib.org. These will show you how to integrate FreeType into your own programs.

Finally, last but not least, you'll need a font file. I went ahead and "borrowed" one from Windows 9.x—you'll find them in C:\WINDOWS\FONTS. Various others are available at no charge on the Web.

SVGAlib fonts may appear frightening at first, but they're really quite simple once you get the hang of them. ## What Is A Fractal?

A fractal often appears to be more than the sum of its parts. The components themselves are nothing much—just points on a plane. When viewed on the screen, however, those points seem to have a life of their own.

As you may know, a fractal is created when you plot the points returned by specific equations. Keep in mind the word *specific*—not just any equation can produce a fractal. Whereas the plots of some equations form bell curves and other everyday shapes, a fractal's equation is quite different.

The one thing that all fractals have in common is *self-similarity*. If you were to magnify a fractal, you'd find that any section you view resembles the whole. No matter what level of detail you're able to produce, the same structures will be observed. See Figure 8.2 for a better explanation of this concept.

See how the pattern reproduces itself? This self-similarity, or *recursion* (or even *scale invariance*), is what makes fractals unique.

### Fractal (Mathematical Definition)

A set of points whose fractal dimension exceeds its topological dimension.

You can view a better example of self-similarity in Figure 8.3. This is Koch's snowflake, a classic fractal form.

Theoretically, the edges of the snowflake will become infinitely detailed. This is because at each stage (or *iteration*), the length of the sides is increased by 4/3. The extra length is pushed outward, and then the sequence repeats. This results in a flowery, paisley-esque appearance.

### Iteration

A complete cycle of operations. In other words, fractals are created by running numbers through an equation. This must be done several times to produce enough points to create an image, and each run-through is known as an iteration.

### Complex Numbers

The next element in the fractal is the *complex number*. Complex numbers look like points on a plane, and indeed, that’s why they were developed. Casper Wessel, a Danish mathematician, used them for plotting, and they received attention because they helped to solve algebraic equations, too.

Complex numbers require special math. Because they are “ordered pairs,” you can’t add or subtract from them like you can with regular numbers. Instead, refer to Table 8.1 for a brief tutorial.

Complex numbers hold the key to fractals. There’ll be more on this in a bit, but for now know that fractals also depend on the square root of negative one (-1). It’s because of this that they went unnoticed for so long; mathematicians considered this equation an impossible proposition. Fortunately, this belief didn’t last forever.

Table 8.1 Math with complex numbers

| Operation | Desired Result | Formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Addition of two pairs | (a, b) + (c, d) | (a+c, b+d) |
| Subtraction of two pairs | (a, b) - (c, d) | (a-c, b-d) |
| Multiplication by a number | x*(a, b) | (x*a, x*b) |
| Division by a number | (a, b)/x | (a/x, b/x) |
| Multiplication of pairs | (a, b)*(c, d) | (a*c-b*d, a*d+b*c) |
| Division of pairs | (a, b)/(c, d) | ((a*c+b*d)/(c*c+d*d), (b*c-a*d)/(c*c+d*d)) |
