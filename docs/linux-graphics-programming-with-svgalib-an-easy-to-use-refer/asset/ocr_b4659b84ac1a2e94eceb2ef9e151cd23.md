If that isn’t enough to grab you, consider that a usable interface helps to sell your system, both figuratively and in the literal sense. It’s no coincidence that PCs in computer stores have colorful screen savers—graphics are catchy. You know that—that’s why you have this book.

# ## GUI Examples

In this section, I’ll briefly review some of the better interface designs that I’ve encountered. You’ll probably notice my predilection for Apple in this department—I think they’ve definitely got this mastered—but that doesn’t mean that the concept can’t evolve. Of course, there’s always room for improvement. Nevertheless, these examples illustrate the full spectrum of what current GUIs offer.

Although many graphical interfaces now make full use of pictures and color, simplicity remains a virtue. As such, Figure 19.1 shows an example of an early Apple Macintosh interface—the design of which is still highly effective. Like a Danish Modernist chair, or a Mies van der Rohe apartment tower on the north side of Chicago, this interface is crisp and succinct.

Note the elementary, clean—some might say intuitive—look. This is clearly the result of careful planning and experience. It would be very easy to accomplish a task with this interface.

Graphical interfaces aren’t limited to desktop managers, either. Most modern *applications* (outside of *Linux*) feature richly designed and intricate interfaces that work hard to make a user’s life easier. Figure 19.2 shows another interface, this time with icons, menus, and text boxes. Note the use of color, which adds an element of friendliness that the first interface perhaps lacked. And there’s accessibility—this second example should be immediately usable by most novices.
