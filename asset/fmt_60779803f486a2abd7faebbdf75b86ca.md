
# Blastula

The blastula is a hollow sphere. Its wall may vary from one to several cells in thickness. These eggs can contain considerable amounts of yolk which may be eccentric in position, that is, shifted towards the animal pole. The portion of its wall is always to be composed of relatively small cells, whereas the vegetal pole tends to be composed of large cells and may be incompletely encapsulated in cells.

The blastocyst contains a gelatinous or jellyfish substance which may exist as a secretion of the blastomeres or in part of way through the blastomeres or intercellular material, or in the blastula.

The wall of the blastula is a mass of cellular elements, each of which will eventually produce a structure during subsequent development. In others, each cell of the wall of the blastula has capacity to produce fate in normal development.

## Blimp

Blimp is an airplane used to employ rigid airships or small blimps. Early ones were built up to 100,000 ft (2,800 m³) and were only a fraction of the size of the later ones. The normal pressure-hull was substantially larger than its predecessor until ships with volumes of approximately 1,500,000 ft (42,000 m³) were operational in the first world war. Development showed that the name blimp would be replaced by the more general term airship. The principle of these structures is basically the same regardless of size. See AIRSHIP.

Typical internal section of rigid airships.

The fabric of the membrane or pressure hull is made of two or three layers of cloth impregnated with an elastomer; at least one of these layers is placed in a bias direction with the others to make the fabric more resistant to shear loads and in a stabilized structure. The materials used for the hull were lightweight, extremely strong, and inflated in a symmetrical form by the body of a balloon or elliptical, in cross section and inflated along its longitudinal axis.

## Bleaching

Bleaching is the process of removing color from a fabric. It may be done using fiber, yarn, or fabric. The processes of bleaching include fabric-bleaching; bleaching processes should be carefully controlled. These processes remove impurities (denaturizing) and neutralize corrosions (or scouring-bleach) so that the bleaching agent may not act upon the free material itself. Bleached fabric is not always free of impurities.

Bleaching is also classified as an oxidation process which removes the substance of chlorine or ferrous content of the material. It should not be confused with the stripping, which is the removal of the applied color.

The fabric off is of light gray, gray, or greys which differs from the partially or fully bleached state.

In the most common bleaching processes the bleach is followed, by the use of peroxide compounds such as hydrogen peroxide. This is done for minimum cost and permanent-press fabrics to utilize the whitening of the yarns and the stripping of cloth, which are always potential problems when chlorine bleaches are used.

Using organic compounds allows for the absorption of waves that are shorter than visual waves and within the visible range. The most notable of these waves are the ultraviolet rays.

These compounds absorb ultraviolet light which is less than 400 nanometers (0.02 in.), and emit blue light which is in the visible range of 400-700 nanometers (0.02-0.03 in.). This emitted blue light contains the yellow and by subtractive theory will produce white. These agents are also used as brighteners and are utilized in household.
